Bouyon gwada: the hardcore bouyon of Guadeloupe

By Smokiller · Published October 6, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Bouyon gwada, also called hardcore bouyon, is the branch of bouyon that grew between Dominica and Guadeloupe. The two islands share Antillean Creole, and their musicians together built a sound that is harder, faster and rawer than the classic bouyon of the live bands. This page explains where bouyon gwada comes from, how it sounds, who carried it from the parties of the 2010s to the gold records of the 2020s, and why its origin stays Dominican.

What is bouyon gwada?

Bouyon gwada is the Guadeloupean version of bouyon, the genre created in Dominica by WCK in 1988. This site’s research file, based on Air Caraïbes and on the history of Guadeloupean music, describes it as a regionalisation of Dominican bouyon: the same jump up foundations, Creole lyrics and electronic beats. So it is neither a separate genre nor a copy: it is a branch, with its own artists, audience and codes, that owns the pressure of the club where the bouyon of the live bands spoke first to carnival.

The word hardcore comes back in every definition. Reference articles on the music of Guadeloupe present hardcore bouyon as a popular current within bouyon gwada, characterised by bawdy and violent lyrics. The Brussels collective Rebel Up, which documented the scene in the early 2010s, describes the same sound: raw lyrics, in English, French or Creole, over heavy percussive riddims with simplified melodies. That direct register, aimed at an informed audience, is what sets the Gwada branch apart from the carnival bouyon that speaks to the whole crowd.

A creation between two islands

Bouyon gwada was born from musical collaborations between citizens of Dominica and Guadeloupe. Bouyon needed no translation to cross the channel: in the French Antilles, the public filed it in the jump up family, a name already given to highly rhythmic carnival music after Exile One’s “L’hivernage” in 1987. According to reference articles, that jump up had its golden age in the 1990s, with songs such as WCK’s “Met Veye”, while often staying confined to background and carnival music.

The handover is documented by the same references. Thanks to exchanges with Dominicans and Guadeloupe’s massive participation in the World Creole Music Festival, leading Dominican bands, Triple Kay and the MFR Band, popularised the genre on the island. Local artists were then brought in, as Triple Kay’s “Allo” remix with Daly and Daly’s solo “Big Ting Poppin” show. Guadeloupean producers and singers then made the sound their own, with their codes, their language and their street energy: bouyon gwada was born from that appropriation.

A harder sound: machines, bass and Creole voices

Where the original bouyon of WCK rests on bands and traditional instruments like lapo kabwit and the jing ping accordion, bouyon gwada pushes the drum machines, the basslines and the voices sung or rapped in Guadeloupean Creole. Rebel Up speaks of a digital, rough club music, born with the generation of accessible production software. The centre of gravity moves from the band on stage to the riddim built in the studio, and the voice becomes a percussive weapon as much as a song.

The riddim logic is the branch’s other marker. Rebel Up notes that a single hit riddim receives several versions there, as in dancehall: a producer lays down a base and several voices come to live on it, a workflow our bouyon riddim page explains. The tempo stays that of party bouyon: published track data gives 160 BPM for Gaza Girls Crew’s “Sa Zot Vlé” in 2011, 157 BPM for “Bwé Rhum” in 2023, and 155 to 160 BPM for Aknose’s 2023 collective tracks. See also bouyon BPM.

The 2010s: hardcore bouyon and the first stage in Paris

The bouyon gwada of the early 2010s travelled first through parties, mixtapes and videos. Reference articles name Wee Low, Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza among its popular musicians, with Suppa as a central figure. Gaza Girls Crew left a rare trace of it: the single “Sa Zot Vlé”, released on 1 May 2011 on Bouyon Concept. Many acts of that era left only one track, or none, in the platform catalogues, which makes every dated credit a piece of archive.

It is also the moment the branch crossed the Atlantic. In its article on hardcore bouyon, which accompanies its mixtape “Bouyon Hardcore 2k13”, Rebel Up writes that it took until December 2012 for the first Dominican and Guadeloupean MCs and DJs to come to Paris, and that a 50-minute documentary, in French and Creole, films that first performance by Suppa, Gaza Girls, Dj Joe and others. Bouyon thus reached France through the diaspora and through the raw sound of bouyon gwada, long before the certifications.

The branch’s reputation fits in one sentence from the Faya podcast, in its episode “Voicenotes from Guadeloupe: le bouyon”: for ten years bouyon has crept into every Guadeloupean party, a very fast music with raw lyrics that even came close to being banned, an outlet for the young that gets talked about, often badly, but above all gets people dancing.

Bouyon gwada in the 2020s: producers, circles and gold records

The next generation moved from mixtapes to platforms. 1T1, a composer, producer and singer from Guadeloupe, is one of its centres: “Moustik” with Aknose in September 2023, “Bouwéy” with Theomaa on the EP “CHORD” of 13 December 2024, and “Mas La Rivé”, which gathers nine names on one track on 12 January 2024. In the same year 2023, “New Bouyon Wave #1” brought together TIITII NBA, 1T1, Softee, Aknose, Nils, Lucky Lukee, Le Juh and Théomaa. In December 2025, in an interview published by CCN, the music developer Maïté Berchel-Bordin named Lejuh, 1T1, Theomaa, Piscine OSP, Aknose and Shanika among the most emblematic bouyon artists of the moment in Guadeloupe.

Official figures followed. “Je l’ai vu”, by the Basse-Terre family trio HollyG with Arendi and VJ Ben, released on 22 October 2021, was certified gold single by SNEP on 10 July 2025, the second bouyon track certified gold in France according to the reference article on the genre; it was composed by Dj Flip Tha Boss, whom the site ToutGwada presents as coming from Dominica. Miimii KDS broke through in 2025 with “Sé Miimii” and is credited on “Pow Pow / Allo Doudou”, with Niska and Litleboy LSBeats767, certified Single Or in 2026. And according to Dominica News Online, TIITII NBA’s “Kitty Kitty”, produced by DJ Luchshiy, has passed 1.65 million streams.

Traffic in both directions between Dominica and Gwada

The relationship no longer runs one way. TIITII NBA, from Morne-à-l’Eau, discovered WCK through his parents, under the name jump up, then the freestyle approach of the Dominican Mr Ridge showed him what the genre could become; in his view, bouyon belongs to Dominica and Guadeloupe is its second chapter. 1T1 has produced for Mr Ridge, “Leave Me Alone”, released on 6 September 2024, ran in Dominican carnival-season mixes alongside Reo, Asa Bantan and Signal Band, and Lejuh sings on Reo’s “Adios Amigos”, with Jixels, released on 22 May 2025.

Labels and life paths follow the same road. More Sewo, a Guadeloupean label created in 2007 by Arturo Bellot, signed the Dominican artist BONNA. Mr Gwada, real name Kernel Stevens, left Dominica in 2000 to join his father in Guadeloupe, and his song “Gwada”, produced by DJ Flip, gave him his stage name. On 5 February 2023, “Bwé Rhum” brought together DJ JOE and the Dominican voice of Asa Bantan on Vadore Concept Groupe. Our chapter Gwada from Dominica tells this relationship from the source island.

Bouyon gwada, jump up, shatta, New Bouyon: reading the labels

The words overlap, and each has its meaning. Jump up is the everyday name for bouyon in the French Antilles. Bouyon gwada is its Guadeloupean, hardcore branch. Shatta is a Martinican dancehall genre, a cousin of bouyon and not a branch born from it; people speak of shatta bouyon when producers of that generation work bouyon material, its keys, its tempo and its Creole. Our bouyon vs shatta page draws the line in detail.

The labels of the 2020s often come from the Gwada scene itself: TIITII NBA’s New Bouyon Age, the New Bouyon concept of Ricky Bishop, an artist of Guadeloupean and Malagasy origin based in Bordeaux, who blends bouyon with trap and electronic music, or the trap bouyon tag in Lejuh’s own posts. For Bouyon Shattaz, New Bouyon and RnBouyon inherit the method of Dominican Wrecketeng. A stage name does not make an origin either: Gwada G is a Dominican artist, despite his name.

The place of bouyon gwada today

In Guadeloupe, bouyon is everyday music: fetes, carnival, social media and sound systems. Bouyon gwada keeps an image there of an authentic, unfiltered sound, defended by fans who want to keep the link with the genre’s Dominican origin. Guadeloupe’s DJs and bouyon pages play a decisive role: their choices often turn a riddim into a carnival track, then relayed to Martinique and Paris. The debate about lyrics stays open: for Maïté Berchel-Bordin, bouyon will gain maturity by diversifying its lyrics and melodies, and Lejuh was asked on a podcast whether bouyon can be made for all audiences.

When Bouyon Shattaz covers the scene, it uses the local flag Gwada people call their own: green bands at the top and bottom, a red middle band, a yellow star. Frequently asked questions

What is bouyon gwada?

Bouyon gwada is the Guadeloupean, hardcore branch of bouyon, the genre created in Dominica in 1988. It keeps the jump up foundations but pushes drum machines, heavy bass and vocals in Guadeloupean Creole. Reference articles attach hardcore bouyon to it, a current with bawdy and violent lyrics, and the collective Rebel Up describes it as raw lyrics over heavy percussive riddims with simplified melodies.

What is the difference between bouyon and bouyon gwada?

Bouyon is the original genre, created in Dominica by WCK in 1988, with its live bands, traditional instruments such as lapo kabwit and the jing ping accordion, and its carnivals. Bouyon gwada is its hardcore branch developed with Guadeloupe: harder, more electronic, built on studio riddims and sung largely in Guadeloupean Creole. It is a regionalisation of the Dominican sound, not a separate genre.

Where does bouyon gwada come from?

It comes from Dominica, by way of Guadeloupe. Bouyon arrived there through the shared Creole, carnival and the tours of Triple Kay and the MFR Band, helped by Guadeloupe’s participation in the World Creole Music Festival. Local artists such as Daly were then brought in, and Guadeloupean producers and singers built their own version of the sound. Our bouyon history traces that route.

Who produces bouyon gwada today?

Published credits name several makers. 1T1 composes and records his own tracks, mixed by Jbreazy on the EP “CHORD”, and he composed and produced Lejuh’s “Air Force 1”. DJ Luchshiy produced TIITII NBA’s “Kitty Kitty”. “Je l’ai vu” by HollyG and Arendi, certified gold, was composed by Dj Flip Tha Boss, whom ToutGwada presents as coming from Dominica.

Are bouyon gwada and shatta the same thing?

No. Bouyon gwada is a branch of bouyon, born in Dominica and reworked in Guadeloupe. Shatta is a Martinican dancehall genre, a cousin of bouyon that shares its speed, synth bass and party culture. When producers work bouyon material in that spirit, people speak of shatta bouyon. See our bouyon vs shatta page.

How fast is bouyon gwada?

Bouyon gwada keeps the tempo of party bouyon, around 155 to 160 BPM. Published track data gives 160 BPM for Gaza Girls Crew’s “Sa Zot Vlé” in 2011, 157 BPM for “Bwé Rhum” in 2023, and 155 to 160 BPM for Aknose’s 2023 collective tracks. The speed stays the same: what changes is the weight of the drum machines and the bass. See bouyon BPM.

Is bouyon gwada the same as jump up?

Not quite. Jump up is the everyday name for bouyon in the French Antilles, a name already given to highly rhythmic carnival music after Exile One’s “L’hivernage” in 1987. Bouyon gwada is the Guadeloupean, hardcore branch of that bouyon, harder and more electronic. TIITII NBA himself discovered WCK through his parents, under the name jump up.

Do Dominican and Guadeloupean bouyon artists work together?

Because of its lyrics.
