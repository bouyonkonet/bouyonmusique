Bunji Garlin: the Viking of soca who sang on a Dominica bouyon riddim

Published October 20, 2026

Bunji Garlin is a soca star from Trinidad and Tobago, and this profile starts by saying so plainly: he is not a bouyon native artist. His real name is Ian Antonio Alvarez, born on July 14, 1978 in Arima, and he is the leading figure of ragga soca, the blend of soca and dancehall he imposed from the late 1990s with dense writing and a fete commander voice. A four time Soca Monarch winner and a Soul Train Award winner for “Differentology”, he has carried soca to stages around the world. His place on this site rests on precise, verified facts: two of his biggest road records, “Famalay” and “Forward”, are productions by Dada, the Dominican producer of bouyon soca, and “Forward” is sung on a hybrid riddim that blends soca with the bouyon of Dominica. This profile tells the soca career on its own merits, then documents, credit by credit, that point of contact with bouyon.

Who Bunji Garlin is: Ian Alvarez of Arima, and ragga soca

Bunji Garlin real name is Ian Antonio Alvarez. He was born on July 14, 1978 in Arima, Trinidad, and references describe him as of Saint Lucian and Venezuelan descent. His long time label, VP Records, presents him as a Trinidadian soca artist known for his energetic play on words and double entendre, in the studio and in live performance. His biographies place him among the leading artists of ragga soca, the branch of soca influenced by Jamaican dancehall, more aggressive and more digital than the melodic soca of the big parades. That is the register in which he made his name: a fast, almost rapped delivery, lyrics that mix social commentary with fete commands, and a fire chant that earned him the fireman nickname in the press.

His best known nickname calls for the attribution this site applies to every artist title. He is widely presented as the Viking of Soca, a nickname published by references and taken up by his audience, not an official title granted by any institution. He presents himself as a lyrics king, and his biographies note that few would dispute that standing as a writer. This profile therefore prints the Viking as a documented nickname, exactly as it prints the crowns of bouyon artists with their attribution, and it turns no nickname into a civil status fact.

Even his stage name has two published accounts, and this profile does not choose between them. VP Records explains that Bunji is a nickname given by his family and that Garlin was inspired by the actor Errol Flynn. The AllMusic biography, carried by several platforms, tells another story: Bunji came from the bungee cord, for flexibility, and Garlin from a type of gun, for firepower, the artist saying that whatever is sent at him, he always bounces back. Caribbean Beat offers a third lead, a street corruption of Galil, the Israeli arms manufacturer. Three serious sources, three versions: this page gives all of them, attributed, because credit begins by not inventing a certainty the sources do not hold.

The frame of this profile must stay clear. Bunji Garlin did not grow up in bouyon, he was not formed in a Roseau band and he claims no Dominican origin. He belongs to the history of Trinidad and Tobago soca, which is a great history in itself, with its competitions, its carnival seasons and its record labels. If this site, devoted to the bouyon of Dominica, opens a page to him, it is because the two worlds touch at documented points: the same carnival stages, the same producers moving between islands, and, in his case, one precise Dominican producer, Dada, behind two of his heaviest records. The last section of this profile documents that contact record by record.

Wallerfield, the maxi taxis and lyrics written on a wall

The reference portrait published by Caribbean Beat tells a childhood that explains the delivery. Ian Alvarez grew up in Wallerfield, near Arima, the last of eight children. He lived alone with his father, who had been retrenched from his job, while his mother worked abroad in the United States. The family was so poor, he recounts, that they could not afford copybooks: the teenager wrote his lyrics on a wall of the house, with stones found in the yard. His father never repainted that wall, keeping it as a memento of his son rise. At sixteen, young Ian took his first job, at Carib Glassworks, then told his father he wanted to live from music. His father got angry, then recognised his own rebellious character in his son. Today, Bunji says, his father is his biggest fan.

The musical training happened in the street and on the buses. In the 1980s and the early 1990s, Jamaican dancehall dominated Trinidad outside carnival season, carried by DJ mixtapes and by the maxi taxis, the minibuses with huge speaker systems that worked as clubs on wheels. A student at Arima Comprehensive, young Alvarez trained there in improvised chanting, heir to the local Trinidadian extempo. Caribbean Beat tells of the Friday evening clashes by Arima market, opposite the Windsor Cinema: an empty fried chicken box set on the ground to collect the crowd money, an improvised competition, a final round defeat in the first week against his friend Jadee, then eleven straight weeks of wins. That, he says, is where he understood that music could pay.

The move from dancehall to soca came through a producer advice. Bunji was part of a collective called Schizophrenic, which staged its own events and wanted to press its own records, having no access to radio. Producer Darryl Braxton, then working in dancehall and hip hop, advised the group to apply its dancehall style to soca, since their chances of breaking into the Jamaican market were slim to none. He gave Bunji a beat, and out of that beat came “Send Dem Riddim Crazy”, his debut soca record, which VP Records lists among the tracks that raised him to prominence in the early 2000s, with “Fire Fi Dem”. The artist presented himself then with green hair and green shoes, a raw image that got him stereotyped as a ghetto artist.

That label, he tells it himself, was a real obstacle: some promoters refused to book him in certain places for fear of the crowd he would attract, while he says he was always making music for everybody. It took “Differentology” in 2013 for the so called uptown fetes to fully accept him, and even then his bookings rested on the album released the year before, full of records like “Tun Up”, “Irregular” and “So and So”. That slow recognition at home is part of the verified record of the artist, and it lights what follows: the international success of Bunji was not a gift of the Trinidadian market, it was a conquest against the social ranking of his own country.

The crowns: Ragga Soca Monarch, Soca Monarch and a decade of albums

The first consecration came through the ragga soca door. His biographies report that he won Trinidad Ragga Soca Monarch competition in 2000 and in 2001, the two years that competition was staged, along with the Young King title in 2001. Then came the major crown of Trinidad carnival: the International Soca Monarch. References credit him with four wins, and they name the winning songs, which makes them checkable one by one: in 2002, “Down in the Ghetto”, tied with Iwer George; in 2004, “Warrior Cry”; in 2005, “Blaze the Fire”, both of those records produced by then band member Shawn Noel; and in 2008, “Fiery”. In 2009 he placed second in the same competition with “Clear de Road”, beaten by his wife Fay-Ann Lyons and her “Meet Super Blue”.

That Fay-Ann Lyons is no private life detail, she is a central piece of the career. Daughter of the great soca singer Superblue, herself a Road March and Soca Monarch winner, she married Bunji Garlin on December 23, 2006. Caribbean Beat describes her as a direct influence on his writing: he studied her songwriting formulas, and the couple write together, up to the record that would give them the 2023 Road March. Bunji is also, according to references, a second cousin of singer Patrice Roberts. Trinidadian soca is a world of families, and his stands at the top of that world on both sides of the marriage.

Across the same decade, the artist built an album catalogue the references list with consistency: “The Chronicles” in 1999, “Revelation” in 2002, his first album for the VP label, “Black Spaniard” in 2003, “Graceful Vengeance” in 2004, “Flame Storm” in 2005, “Next Direction” in 2006, “Global” in 2007, an international opening album with reggae artists T.O.K. and Freddie McGregor, then “Fiery” in 2008. Between albums, one fact shows the place his voice had already taken outside the Caribbean: his track “Brrt” is featured on the soundtrack of the video game Grand Theft Auto IV, one of the best selling games of its generation.

After 2008, Bunji stopped releasing albums for several years, but his singles kept making the season, season after season. That period of records without an album prepares the shock of 2012 and 2013. It also shows the difference between the two musical economies this site often compares: in Trinidad, a career is played out on competitions and on the carnival road, year by year, record by record, where Dominican bouyon long played out on the albums of live bands. Bunji reached the top of that competition system before becoming, with a single record, the world face of soca.

Differentology: the record that carried soca across

“Differentology”, subtitled “Ready for the Road”, was written by Garlin, recorded at Black Ice studios in Trinidad, produced by Keron Thompson and released by Sheriff Music in late 2012, ahead of the 2013 carnival season. In Trinidad, the record dominated the season, but it lost the 2013 Road March, the prize for the song most played on the road, to the return of Super Blue with “Fantastic Friday”. Wired868 states it plainly: 2013 was arguably Bunji best chance to conquer the road, and it slipped away to his father in law. The Road March would come later, and by another door, the bouyon soca door, as the last section of this profile tells.

Outside Trinidad, by contrast, the record exploded. In 2013 it won the Soul Train Award for Best International Performance, in a category where Machel Montano was also nominated. Major Lazer remixed it, reaching No. 9 on the iTunes World Music Chart, and a version with Busta Rhymes also circulated. The record was featured in an episode of the television drama Grey’s Anatomy, won the Battle of The Beats competition of New York radio HOT 97, was chosen by public radio NPR among its favourite anthems of the year and by MTV Iggy as its Song of the Year. Rolling Stone presented him, in the coverage carried by VP Records, as America first soca star. On May 1, 2014, he made his national TV debut in the United States on BET 106 and Park, then took the main stage of Hot 97 Summer Jam concert on June 1.

The industry consequence followed at once. In 2014 Bunji signed with RCA Records, in conjunction with VP Records, and in August released the album “Differentology”, his first full length album since “Global” in 2007. The album, which followed the digital only EP “Carnival Tabanca”, blends soca, EDM, hip hop and dancehall, with tracks like “Red Light District”, “Savage”, “Touchless” and “Carnival Tabanca”, the record that named the EP and describes that carnival sickness, the tabanca, the longing that grips the fete goer when the fete is done. The album also holds “Truck on D Road”, whose remix with rapper A$AP Ferg, met at Trinidad carnival 2014, was released as a single by VP Records. The album reached No. 6 on the Billboard Reggae Albums chart in the United States.

The music press took the album seriously, which does not always happen to soca. NPR noted that “Differentology” is the singer eleventh album and that after sixteen years in the business he was finally poised for crossover success. Rolling Stone placed the album on its list of the 20 best EDM, electronic and dance albums of 2014. The Washington Post heard in it a rich, pulsating pan Caribbean energy that should translate on any dance floor. For a genre often treated as seasonal music, that reception matters: “Differentology” did not only make people dance, it made writers treat soca as a major music.

The bridge builder: world collaborations, Turn Up and the Hummingbird Medal

After 2014, the career of Bunji became that of a bridge builder between soca and the world stages. His documented collaborations draw the map: “Jungle Bae” with Jack Ü, the duo of Skrillex and Diplo, in 2015; “All or Nothing” with Elliphant and Diplo, a record also heard in the pilot of the Netflix series Impulse; “The Message” with Damian Marley in 2015; “Buss Head” with Machel Montano in 2017, written among others by Keegan Taylor, Kit Israel, Machel Montano and Ian Alvarez; “Ride It” with Sebastian Ingrosso and Salvatore Ganacci the same year; and, on the Caribbean pop side, “Carnival Tabanca” with Tarrus Riley. In 2017 he also released the single “Big Bad Soca”, which platforms still list among his most streamed records.

In 2017 as well, the album “Turn Up”, released by VP Records, reached No. 3 on the Billboard Reggae Albums chart, his highest chart placing to date according to references. On September 24, 2017, the state of Trinidad and Tobago bestowed on him a national award, the silver Hummingbird Medal. His label artist page also notes philanthropy and community work in Trinidad and Tobago alongside the music. The path is that of an artist become an institution in his country, without ever softening the register that had him labelled at the start.

Family stays at the centre of the operation. The Alvarez Lyons couple works as a production house as much as a stage couple: Fay-Ann co writes, negotiates with labels and lawyers, and holds her own winning career. When “Hard Fete” won the Road March in 2023, the reports of TUCO, the calypsonians organisation, announced the couple as joint champions: the record, co written by Fay-Ann, was played 135 times at the judging points, ahead of “Come Home” by Nailah Blackman and Skinny Fabulous, played 106 times. It was Bunji second Road March, and the first of the post pandemic era. It arrived four years after the first one, and that first one is the bouyon fact of this profile.

What is left to prove after four Soca Monarch titles, two Road March wins and a national medal? The recent discography answers: the album “Ayl’Ian” came out in 2024 on the Bad Beagle label, and VP Records points to a 2024 record, “Party Nice Right Now”, among his recent successes. Music statistics platforms keep listing “Famalay”, “Jungle Bae” and “Big Bad Soca” among his most streamed records week after week. A quarter century after “Send Dem Riddim Crazy”, the voice from Arima is still a voice of the season, and it is in that context of longevity that his documented move into bouyon takes its meaning: it is no end of career accident, but the act of an artist at his peak, chosen by a Dominican producer to carry his fusion.

The bouyon link: Famalay, the Smoke Riddim and Forward

The first point of contact is one of the biggest soca records of the decade. “Famalay”, performed by Machel Montano, Skinny Fabulous and Bunji Garlin, won the Trinidad and Tobago Road March in 2019, played 346 times at the judging points according to Wired868. For Bunji, it was the first Road March of his career, after the “Differentology” miss of 2013. For Skinny Fabulous, from Saint Vincent, it was a historic first for a non Trinidadian artist. The official credits of the record, published with its performance video, name the producers: DadaMuzic and DSM, with mixing by DadaMuzic, and the composers Gamal “Skinny” Doyle, Machel Montano, Ian Alvarez and Krishna Clyde Lawrence. DadaMuzic is Dada, the producer from Dominica.

That name connects this record to the history this site documents page after page. According to the signed testimony of Smokiller, a Dominican-born producer and a participant in that era, Dada coined the term bouyon soca and started the style in Dominica in 2003, before Triple Kay and others popularised it. The producer of that start is therefore the same man who, sixteen years later, produced and mixed the record that finally gave Bunji Garlin the Road March. The references of the genre themselves hold up “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”, the other Dada production for Machel Montano, Skinny Fabulous and Iwer George, as the emblem records of bouyon soca. The 2019 Road March of Bunji is, by its credits, a record of that Dominican branch of soca.

The second point of contact is even more direct. On December 26, the Trinidad site Ebuzztt published an article on the “Smoke Riddim” of Dada: a hybrid riddim, the producer says, blending soca and the bouyon music of Dominica. Dada explained there that he had chosen two artists, Bunji Garlin and Shurwayne Winchester, for their ability to carry big fetes, with the flags, the rags and the water, and to bring back the energy of fetes as they used to be. The Bunji record on the riddim is called “Forward”, and the Shurwayne record is “Wet Dem Down”. The track was later included on the Bunji album “Ayl’Ian”, released on February 9, 2024 by Bad Beagle according to Shazam, which credits Ian Alvarez and Krishna Clyde Lawrence as songwriters and lists a tempo of 160 BPM, the fast tempo of bouyon. An artist of this level does not lend his voice to a bouyon riddim by chance: he was chosen, named and credited.

The regional context completes the picture without stretching it beyond the sources. The major Billboard feature on bouyon, carried by United States press, notes that once soca giants like Bunji Garlin, Fay-Ann Lyons, Problem Child, Mr Killa, Voice and Machel Montano took note of bouyon, they incorporated the genre into their own releases. The sentence holds for the whole movement, and the case of Bunji is its best documented demonstration: a Dominican producer behind his first Road March, a bouyon soca riddim under his name, and a record, “Forward”, that now sits in his official catalogue. To hear that meeting point, start with “Famalay” and “Forward”, then read our bouyon soca page and our bouyon playlist. Bunji Garlin remains a soca artist from Trinidad and Tobago, and this profile claims nothing else. But in the history of bouyon soca, his name is written in credits, not in rumours.

Frequently asked questions

Is Bunji Garlin a bouyon artist?

No. Bunji Garlin, real name Ian Antonio Alvarez, is a soca and ragga soca artist from Trinidad and Tobago, born in Arima on July 14, 1978. His link to bouyon is precise and documented: Dominican producer Dada produced “Famalay”, his first Road March record in 2019, and Bunji sang “Forward” on the Smoke Riddim of Dada, a hybrid riddim of soca and Dominica bouyon.

How many times has Bunji Garlin won the Soca Monarch?

Four times the International Soca Monarch, according to references: in 2002 with “Down in the Ghetto”, tied with Iwer George, in 2004 with “Warrior Cry”, in 2005 with “Blaze the Fire” and in 2008 with “Fiery”. He also won the Ragga Soca Monarch in 2000 and 2001 and the Young King title in 2001, and he won the Road March in 2019 with “Famalay” and in 2023 with “Hard Fete”.

What did Differentology win, and where was it heard?

“Differentology”, released in late 2012 and produced by Keron Thompson, won the Soul Train Award for Best International Performance in 2013. The record was remixed by Major Lazer, featured in an episode of the television drama Grey’s Anatomy, won the HOT 97 Battle of The Beats competition in New York and was chosen by MTV Iggy as its Song of the Year. The album of the same name was released in August 2014 by RCA and VP Records.

Why did Differentology not win the Road March in 2013?

Because the road chose another record that year. Wired868 reports that “Differentology” was brushed aside at the 2013 Road March by the return of Super Blue with “Fantastic Friday”. Super Blue is the father of Fay-Ann Lyons, the wife of Bunji. Bunji first Road March came in 2019 with “Famalay”, produced by Dominican Dada, and his second in 2023 with “Hard Fete”, co written by Fay-Ann.

What is Bunji Garlin real name, and where does his stage name come from?

His real name is Ian Antonio Alvarez. On the stage name, sources do not tell the same story: VP Records says Bunji is a family nickname and Garlin was inspired by actor Errol Flynn, while the AllMusic biography speaks of the bungee cord for Bunji and a type of gun for Garlin. This site prints both accounts with their attribution, without choosing one.

Did Bunji Garlin really sing on a Dominica bouyon riddim?

Yes. Ebuzztt reports that Dominican producer Dada chose Bunji Garlin and Shurwayne Winchester for his Smoke Riddim, a hybrid riddim blending soca and the bouyon of Dominica. The Bunji record on that riddim is called “Forward”. It now appears on his album “Ayl’Ian”, released in 2024, with Ian Alvarez and Krishna Clyde Lawrence credited as songwriters.
