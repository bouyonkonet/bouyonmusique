Frequently asked questions

What is jing ping?

Jing ping is the traditional dance music of Dominica, led by the accordion. It accompanies the island’s Creole ball dances, such as quadrille, mazurka and bèlè, inherited from its Creole history. It is one of the two founding ingredients of bouyon, with cadence-lypso.

What instruments are played in jing ping?

The classic line-up brings together an accordion, which leads the music, drums like the lapo kabwit, a goatskin drum, a boom-boom and a bamboo flute. These sounds remain the soul of bouyon, and good producers still sample them, as our page on bouyon instruments explains.

What is the link between jing ping and bouyon?

When WCK created bouyon in Roseau in 1988, the band took the melodies, the rhythms and the spirit of jing ping and pushed them with electronic keyboards. It is this dialogue between the village ball and the machine that gives bouyon its unique character in the Caribbean.

Where do I start with bouyon?

Start with the history of the genre, then the Bouyon Road Mix playlist by Bouyon Shattaz, which mixes Dominica carnival classics with new bouyon. To understand where the sound comes from, also listen to its two roots: jing ping and cadence-lypso, the dance traditions WCK brought together in 1988.

Which country created bouyon?

Dominica, in the Caribbean, in the late 1980s, with WCK as the pioneering band. WCK created bouyon in Roseau, the capital, in 1988 by plugging cadence-lypso and jing ping into electronic keyboards and drum machines, to make the island’s fetes and carnival dance.

What dances does jing ping accompany?

Jing ping accompanies Dominica's traditional ball dances: quadrille, mazurka, bèlè and other dances inherited from the island's Creole history. These dances structure the fetes and the memory of the villages. Bouyon descends from them directly: when WCK plugged jing ping into the machines, older listeners recognized familiar steps and gestures.

What is the difference between jing ping and cadence-lypso?

Jing ping is Dominica's traditional ball music, led by the accordion, with the lapo kabwit, the boom-boom and the bamboo flute. Cadence-lypso is a Dominican genre of the 1970s, carried by bands like Exile One and the Grammacks, with an aggressive rhythm guitar and lyrics in Creole. Bouyon was born from the fusion of the two.

How did jing ping become bouyon?

Through an instrument-by-instrument translation. Cornell "Fingers" Phillip, WCK's founding keyboardist, recalls that his brother Daryl recorded traditional bands at the family home and that the boys played along: the boumboum became the bass guitar, the syak and the tambal became the drum machine, and the accordion became the keyboard. See Cornell Phillip.

Can you still hear jing ping in today's bouyon?

Yes. Its sounds remain the soul of bouyon, and good producers still sample them. Many build their drum kit by sampling recordings of jing ping and carnival bands: a generic kit makes a fast beat, while a kit fed with jing ping and lapo kabwit makes bouyon. See the bouyon drum kit.

Does bouyon dancing come from jing ping?

Largely, yes. Bouyon dancing comes from the dance music the genre descends from: the jing ping of Dominica's traditional balls, bèlè, kwadril and the chanté mas of carnival. Bouyon kept that function, with a fast tempo and looping keyboards made for the street, the fete and the parade. See the bouyon dance page.

What does bouyon mean, and where does jing ping fit in?

Bouyon means soup in Creole: you put many ingredients in, everything mixes and nothing is wasted. Jing ping is the oldest root of this recipe, alongside cadence-lypso, bèlè, kwadril, chanté mas, lapo kabwit, mazurka and zouk. As in the dish, no single ingredient completely dominates.
