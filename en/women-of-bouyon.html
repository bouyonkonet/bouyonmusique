Women of bouyon: headliners, not decoration

By Smokiller · Published September 26, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

This chapter sets a simple line: in bouyon, women are headliners and hitmakers, not decoration. Maurane Voyer carries “Joli BB” with Reo. Miimii KDS stacks a viral hit and an SNEP gold record. Tamillia lends her voice to DJ Blax’s “Don’t Care”. Ebony Empress fronts an official riddim. And the remix economy itself runs on women’s voices, from Ayra Starr to Shenseea.

Maurane Voyer and Miimii KDS, proof by titles

Maurane Voyer comes from Martinique. Her album “BBW” dates from 2022, and her voice carries “Joli BB” with Reo, released on October 9, 2026. She also appears on the bill for Night 1 of the World Creole Music Festival 2026. Three facts that draw an established artist: an album, a noted bouyon duet, and a major festival stage in the genre’s island of origin.

Miimii KDS, from Guadeloupe, offers another kind of proof: the French numbers. Her “Sé Miimii” went viral in 2025, and “Pow Pow / Allo Doudou” was certified Single Or by the SNEP, with 15 million equivalent streams in France. Her Miimii KDS page details that journey. A Gwada artist turning bouyon into French gold: that is an argument the whole genre can use.

Tamillia and Ebony Empress, voices of the riddims

Tamillia grew up in Saint Martin and was revealed by The Voice Kids France at 14. She sings DJ Blax’s “Don’t Care”, released on October 16, 2026. Her path links three worlds bouyon constantly crosses: the Dutch and French island of Saint Martin, French television, and the Caribbean riddims where one voice can tip an entire track.

Ebony Empress, for her part, gives her name to the “Bounce 2D Riddim”, whose official video is carried by a title written by Everlyne Walters and Philisburt Carette and produced by McGyver. That full credit deserves a slow re-read: writers, production, performer. Riddims are collective works, and women hold places in them as performers and as creators. Naming them all is this site’s method.

Remixes, dancers and the audience

The bouyon remix economy also runs on women’s voices. Ayra Starr’s “Heaven Baby” and Shenseea’s “Vulnerability” became Bouyon Shattaz remixes by Smokiller. Two major female voices, two titles reworked at bouyon tempo, and a demonstration that the wrecketeng method described in our wrecketeng chapter applies first, often, to songs carried by women.

Finally, female audiences and dancers drive bouyon danse diffusion on social platforms. The steps are born in fetes, challenges and videos, and dancers are their first ambassadors. Saying that women make bouyon is therefore not only about the singers. It is recognising the whole chain: the voices, the pens, the dancing bodies and the audiences who pass it on.

Listening with the story in mind

This chapter is also an invitation to move around the site. Hear them via the bouyon playlist, then read Maurane Voyer, Miimii KDS and Ebony Empress. Every page answers another one: an artist page lights up a culture page, a style page lights up a festival page, a playlist turns reading into listening. You do not have to read everything in order. Come in through the name that intrigues you, the rhythm that hooked you, the festival everyone is talking about. Then come back here and read this chapter again with those sounds in your head. The second reading will be different, because the names will have become voices. That is exactly the journey Bouyon Shattaz wants for bouyon: first curiosity, then listening, then loyalty. And at every step, the same reflex: check who sings, who produces, who wrote, and where the sound comes from. An audience that asks those questions becomes an audience that protects the music it loves.

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Credit, dance and language

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of the women of bouyon. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.

Reading, linking, passing it on

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because the women of bouyon does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

Protecting what makes the party

To listen to the women of bouyon with good ears, you have to slow down a little. Bouyon moves fast. It is built for the party, for the body, for the crowd answering back right away. But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. A genre is never just its best known title. It lives in the transitions, in the choruses sung back together, in the way a producer decides to let a loop breathe or to fire it up again. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

There is also a very concrete side to the women of bouyon: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the women featured in this chapter?

The chapter features Maurane Voyer from Martinique, Miimii KDS from Guadeloupe, Tamillia, who grew up in Saint Martin, and Ebony Empress, the voice of the Bounce 2D Riddim. It also cites Ayra Starr and Shenseea, whose voices carry Smokiller bouyon remixes: headliners and hitmakers, not decorations.

Which women-led title was certified gold in France?

“Pow Pow / Allo Doudou”, with Miimii KDS, an artist from Guadeloupe, was certified Single Or by the SNEP with 15 million equivalent streams in France. Miimii KDS had already scored a viral title in 2025 with “Sé Miimii”. A Gwada artist turning bouyon into French gold is an argument the whole genre can use.

Who is Maurane Voyer?

Maurane Voyer is a singer from Martinique who released the album “BBW” in 2022. In 2026 she carries “Joli BB” with Reo, released on October 9, 2026, on the Mauve label and written by Maurane Voyer, Dario Yankey and Nicolas Lanclume. She plays Night 1 of WCMF 2026 in Roseau. Her story is on the Maurane Voyer page.

Who sings Don't Care by DJ Blax?

Tamillia, full name Tamillia Chance, sings and wrote the lyrics of “Don't Care”, the DJ Blax single released on October 16, 2026. She grew up in Saint Martin and took part in The Voice Kids France at 14. The video was shot in St Maarten, on the island where she grew up. See DJ Blax.

Who wrote and produced the Bounce 2D Riddim?

The “Bounce 2D Riddim” is performed by Ebony Empress, written by Everlyne Walters, her civil name, and Philisburt Carette, and produced by McGyver, according to the official video. The same video credits dancers Denise Edwards, Shantel Duruo and Yaya. Writers, production, performer, dancers: a riddim is collective work, and this site names everyone.

Where is Ebony Empress from?

Ebony Empress, born Everlyne Walters in the village of Lagoon, Portsmouth, Dominica, is now based in Saint Martin, according to the profile published by Lazie Indie Magazine in April 2025. She broke through in 2018 with “Quality Bumper”. Her own videos present her as The Goddess of Bouyon, a title she gives herself. See Ebony Empress.

Which female bouyon artist is nominated at the 2026 Caribbean Music Awards?

Ebony Empress is among the Saint Martin artists nominated at the 2026 Caribbean Music Awards in the Best Bouyon Artist category, according to The Peoples Tribune SXM. It is regional recognition for a Dominica-born voice who also leads anthems for women such as “Strength of a Woman” and “Woman is Boss”, and who is relaunching her career in 2026.

Which Smokiller bouyon remixes rework women's voices?

Ayra Starr's “Heaven Baby” and Shenseea's “Vulnerability” became Bouyon Shattaz remixes by Smokiller. Two major female voices reworked at bouyon tempo, following the Wrecketeng method: take a familiar melody and voice and re-dress them on a bouyon rhythm. Both are free downloads on smokiller.com, his link hub.

Does female bouyon only come from the French Antilles?

No. Ebony Empress, for one, was born in Dominica. The women of bouyon are present on every floor of the house: Martinique, Guadeloupe, Saint Martin, and also the very island where the genre was born. It is not an imported theme.

What role do dancers play in bouyon?

Dancers and female audiences spread bouyon dance across social platforms. Steps are born at parties, in challenges and in videos, and dancers are their first ambassadors. Saying that women make bouyon therefore means recognising the whole chain: the voices, the pens, the bodies that dance and the audiences that pass it on.
