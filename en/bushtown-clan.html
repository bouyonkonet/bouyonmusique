Bushtown Clan: the crew that moved bouyon to the DJ

By Smokiller · Published October 20, 2026

The Bushtown Clan is a Dominican collective the sources place at the centre of the early development of Wrecketeng, the sampling based offshoot of bouyon. A product of bouyon-muffin, inspired by the work of DJ Cut, the Clan helped establish the style in Dominica through DJ performances, remixes and recordings. Its most concrete biography fits in two modest but precious documents: the biography of producer Dada, which names the four artists behind the group, and the profile of Nemesiz, which dates his arrival in the Clan to December 1998 and a local debut by the group to December 2000. This profile assembles those verified pieces, and adds nothing to them.

Who the Bushtown Clan are: a collective, not a classic stage band

The Bushtown Clan belongs to the family of DJ and producer crews, not to the big live bands that built the first reputation of bouyon. WCK, formed in Roseau in 1988, had created the genre with a full band. The Clan worked differently: DJs, voices and machines, dances, mixes and remixes. That difference of format is the heart of its story, because the style it helped establish, Wrecketeng, is precisely the branch of bouyon born in the booth rather than on the stage.

The most direct description of the group comes from the biography of Dominican producer Krishna “Dada” Lawrence, published on ShowcaseYourMusic. It presents the Bushtown Clan as a popular dancehall, soca, hip hop and bouyon group. Those four labels, given by a musician who grew up next to the group, sum up its ground: not a pure and closed bouyon, but a bouyon open to the music young Dominicans were listening to and dancing to at the end of the 1990s.

The current English Wikipedia bouyon page confirms the importance of the collective in measured words: the Bushtown Clan played an important role in the early development and popularisation of Wrecketeng. It specifies the mode of action: through DJ performances, remixes and recordings, the collective helped establish the style within Dominica music scene. Three verbs, three grounds: the dance, the home studio of the remix, and the record.

This profile must also state what the record does not contain. The sources consulted give no formal founding date, no complete discography and no verified official page for the Bushtown Clan on the platforms. The history of the group travelled like that of many Dominican crews of that era: in the dances, in the mixes passed from hand to hand, and in the memory of the musicians who grew up around it. The next two sections gather the names and dates that two artist biographies do allow us to verify.

The names behind the Clan: four artists named by the biography of Dada

The biography of Dada on ShowcaseYourMusic contains the most precious list in the Bushtown Clan file. It tells how the young Krishna Lawrence grew up around artists such as Kendel “Loosin” Knight, Kerry “Syco Fury” Severin, Nigel “Jonny Blaze” Anthony and Steven “Bofa” Lander, and it adds: they all formulated the popular dancehall, soca, hip hop and bouyon group Bushtown Clan. Four names, four stage nicknames, one group: to this day, it is the only published line up of the collective in a source consulted.

That list must be read for what it is: the testimony of an insider, published in his own artist biography, not an official registry. But its value is real. Dada is not an outside commentator: he says he grew up around those artists, and he credits the Bushtown Clan with the motivation that pushed him to dig deeper into music. When a major producer of the next generation names his elders, he does archive work the institutions did not do. This profile therefore repeats his four names exactly as he gives them, with their nicknames in quotation marks.

A second document adds a fifth name to the circle of the Clan, with a date. The profile of the artist Nemesiz on Bandmine tells how, in December 1998, he joined a group called Bushtown Clan, and how over the next months he was in demand to perform at numerous shows. The same profile tells how, on the road with the Clan, he met a Kerry with whom he formed the DJ duo C.A.P. The biography of Dada, for its part, names a Kerry “Syco Fury” Severin among the four who formed the group. Each source speaks of a Kerry close to the Clan, under different presentations: this profile reports them separately, without merging them into a single identity the documents do not allow us to establish.

That caution with names is part of the method of this site. Bouyon has travelled too often without its credits for Bouyon Shattaz to invent new ones, even with good intentions. For the Bushtown Clan, the verified state of the file is therefore the following: four founders named by the biography of Dada, one member dated to December 1998 named by his own profile, and a DJ duo, C.A.P., born on the road with the Clan. It is less than a complete biography, and it is infinitely more than the silence surrounding most Dominican crews of that period.

From bouyon-muffin to Wrecketeng: the “Party” inspiration

To understand the Bushtown Clan, you have to step one rung up, to bouyon-muffin. That bouyon offshoot incorporates Jamaican raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall. Its documented figure is Skinny Banton, later known as Shadowflow, who collaborated with WCK from 1995, chanting with ragga influenced vocals on top of bouyon rhythms. Bouyon-muffin is the soil the Clan grew from: the older versions of the English Wikipedia page describe it literally as a product of bouyon-muffin.

The trigger named by those same sources is one recording: “Party”, featuring Joanne and DJ Cut of Bucktown Sounds. Its consequence is worded almost identically in English and in French: tracks such as “Party” gave bouyon-muffin products such as the Bushtown Clan a further inspiration to incorporate more hip hop and dancehall into the genre, until the new style was created. The Clan is therefore not a simple heir of bouyon-muffin: it is among the actors whose work turned the inspiration into a style.

That new style, Wrecketeng, defines itself by its method: a bouyon based on sampling, which remixed existing dancehall and hip hop recordings over bouyon instrumentals. Like dub, it consists predominantly of instrumental remixes, achieved by removing the beat from an existing piece and emphasising the bouyon drum and bass parts, on a base sometimes called a riddim. The contribution of the Bushtown Clan sits in the passage of that method from an isolated gesture to a collective practice: an entire crew repeating it, playing it out and making it desirable.

On the name of the style, this site follows the same rule as for DJ Cut: the official spelling is Wrecketeng. According to Smokiller, founder of Bouyon Shattaz and a participant of the Nursery Krew era, that spelling comes from DJ Cut himself. The spelling reketeng, widespread on the internet, is a mistake that became common, and this profile uses it only when flagged as such. For a collective whose work consisted of taking other people records and turning them into bouyon, keeping the right name of the style is a way of returning the favour: retelling its history, but with the right credit.

The Clan method: samples, existing voices and rearranged rhythms

The current English Wikipedia page sums up the production approach of the Bushtown Clan in one dense sentence: it emphasised sampling, rhythmic experimentation and the use of existing vocal recordings over newly arranged bouyon instrumentals. Every term matters. Sampling states the material: records already there. Rhythmic experimentation states the work: not copying, but twisting. And existing voices over rearranged instrumentals states the result: known songs coming back dressed as bouyon, recognisable and new at the same time.

The French Wikipedia page places the Bushtown Clan among the first groups and crews to play an essential role in popularising the style, alongside Klockerz Krew and Nursery Krew. It says they became known by mixing popular hip hop and dancehall anthems with aggressive bouyon drum programming and samples. That page presents as emblematic examples of the period bouyon remixes of tracks such as “Big Pimpin’” by Jay-Z, with UGK, and “Ridin’ Dirty” by Chamillionaire, described as founding classics of the later hybrid styles.

That last point requires an honesty note this profile takes on openly. The French section containing those examples carries, on the page itself, a banner stating that it does not cite its sources sufficiently. Bouyon Shattaz therefore reports them as the French page account of the pioneer crews taken together, not as a track by track verified discography of the Bushtown Clan alone. No source consulted allows those two precise remixes to be credited to the Clan rather than to the pioneer crews as a whole, and this profile does not do so.

What remains is the sound, which the current sources describe consistently for the style as a whole: fast percussion, heavy basslines, repetitive rhythmic patterns and vocals influenced by Caribbean dancehall and rap, with R and B added by the French page to the list of fusions. That is the sound the Bushtown Clan worked in, dance after dance. The neighbouring bouyon playlist on this site lets you hear the current descendants of that grammar, from today bouyon remixes to tracks built on existing voices.

December 1998, December 2000: the two dates of the Nemesiz profile

The profile of Nemesiz on Bandmine gives the Bushtown Clan something rare in its file: a chronology. First date: December 1998, when Nemesiz joined the group. Second marker: over the following months, he was in demand to perform at numerous shows, on the road with the Clan. The collective of the late 1990s was therefore not a studio project that stayed on paper: it was a working outfit that toured, played, and drew enough attention for a young artist to build the start of his path with it.

The same profile tells of the birth of a side project born from the Clan: the DJ duo C.A.P., formed by Nemesiz with the Kerry he met on the road. Within a couple of months, the profile says, C.A.P. made underground history, creating unique mixes in Dominica and St Maarten. That detail redraws the map of the Bushtown Clan: its activity was not limited to one local scene. Through its members and their duos, the work of the Clan already circulated between Dominica and St Maarten, along the road of the mixes and the dances.

Second date: December 2000. The profile says the Clan then dropped their local debut, and that C.A.P. dropped its debut mix at around the same time, titled “Weve Just Begun Vol. 1” in the spelling of the profile. Local debut is the only description given of that release by the group: no album title published, no track listing and no label named in the source. This profile therefore reports the fact and its date, credited to the account of Nemesiz, without turning that release into a discography it cannot document.

Those two dates, 1998 and 2000, frame what can be called the documented period of the Bushtown Clan: a collective active on the shows at the end of the 1990s, a local release at the turn of the 2000s, and an influence that already exceeded its own catalogue. Because the most measurable legacy of the Clan is not a record: it is a musician. The next section follows that thread, from the child who grew up beside the group to the producer who would relay its work under another name, Nursery Krew.

The legacy: Dada, Nursery Krew and the relay of the 2000s

The biography of Dada gives the Bushtown Clan a named legacy: the group, it says, gave him the motivation to dig deeper into the music. The young Krishna Lawrence, who had started music at the age of twelve according to his biography, grew up around the four who formed the Clan, then became Dada, a producer. That path, from a close listener to an actor, is exactly how crews transmit a style: not through manifestos, but through teenagers who listen, watch how it is done, and decide to do it in their turn.

The relay becomes institutional in the early 2000s. The sources place Nursery Krew Inc. at the next stage of Wrecketeng: the crew further developed the style through original recordings, with associated artists and producers such as Burn Brain Jolly, Charlie Black, Watty and Smokiller, and recordings such as “Peanuts”, “Sorti Desandre” and the NKI Medley. The biography of Dada adds the human link: he is the one who brought out the group Nursery Krew Inc., and he produced tracks there including “Peanuts”. The influence of the Bushtown Clan therefore flows, through Dada, into the team that gave Wrecketeng its phase of original production.

The name of Dada also carries another thread this site documents with care. According to the first hand testimony of Smokiller, a participant of that era, bouyon soca was started by Dada in 2003 in Dominica, and Triple Kay later popularised it. The musician motivated by the Bushtown Clan thus finds himself, a few years later, at the attested origin of another branch of the genre. The Clan did not create bouyon soca, and this profile does not claim it did: it formed the ear of one of those who created it, which is another, documented, form of musical parentage.

In December 2025, the whole lineage was named in public. On December 3, 2025, Dominica News Online announced the Smokiller collaborative album devoted to the origins of Wrecketeng, with contributions from DJ Cut, Klass Money, Charlie Black, Keith Goddard, O Banga and Mopo Neck, and an acknowledgement of the influence of Burn Brain Jolly and the Nursery Krew recordings. According to the report of that announcement, the project explicitly seeks to draw attention to the contributions of DJ Cut, the Bushtown Clan and Nursery Krew Inc. to the sounds of contemporary bouyon. Thirty years after bouyon-muffin, the collective with no official discography finds itself cited, by name, in the project that tells the origins of the style. This profile exists to document that name before it fades, alongside our history of bouyon.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the members of the Bushtown Clan named in the sources?

The biography of producer Dada, on ShowcaseYourMusic, names four artists as those who formed the group: Kendel “Loosin” Knight, Kerry “Syco Fury” Severin, Nigel “Jonny Blaze” Anthony and Steven “Bofa” Lander. The profile of Nemesiz, on Bandmine, adds that he joined the Clan in December 1998.

What kind of group was the Bushtown Clan?

A Dominican collective of DJs and producers. The biography of Dada describes it as a popular dancehall, soca, hip hop and bouyon group. Its work ran through DJ performances, remixes and recordings, with a production approach centred on sampling.

What is the link between the Bushtown Clan and Wrecketeng?

The Clan played an important role in the early development and popularisation of Wrecketeng in Dominica. A product of bouyon-muffin, it was inspired by tracks such as “Party”, featuring Joanne and DJ Cut, to incorporate more hip hop and dancehall, in the chain that created the style.

When did the Bushtown Clan make its local debut?

In December 2000, according to the profile of Nemesiz on Bandmine. The same profile tells how Nemesiz had joined the Clan in December 1998, was in demand for numerous shows, and how the DJ duo C.A.P., formed on the road with the Clan, created mixes in Dominica and St Maarten and released its first mix at the same time as the local debut of the Clan.

How did the Bushtown Clan influence Dada?

According to his biography, Krishna “Dada” Lawrence grew up around the four artists who formed the Bushtown Clan, and the group gave him the motivation to dig deeper into music. Dada later brought out Nursery Krew Inc., the next stage of Wrecketeng in the early 2000s, where he produced tracks including “Peanuts”.

Is the Bushtown Clan cited in the 2025 Wrecketeng project?

Yes. The Smokiller collaborative album announced on December 3, 2025 by Dominica News Online seeks, according to the report of the announcement, to draw attention to the contributions of DJ Cut, the Bushtown Clan and Nursery Krew Inc. to the development of sounds now associated with contemporary bouyon.
