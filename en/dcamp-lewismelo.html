Dcamp & Lewismelo, the duo that dared to call its track BOUYON

Published October 8, 2026

Dcamp & Lewismelo, Dylan and Lewis Campbell, are a French-Creole bouyon duo in which Lewis also produces their riddims. Their reference track carries the name of the genre itself: BOUYON, with an official video on YouTube. Calling your song BOUYON is a statement of intent: no detour, no translation, the Dominican sound claimed as the title. In October 2026, Bouyon Shattaz built a 55-second vertical edit of the official video, on the active section from 1:20 to 2:15, in its producer-reel style. Their precise island origins are not confirmed in our records: this page therefore speaks of their work from the French and Creole side of bouyon, without turning it into an invented biography.

A duo, two roles: the voices and the riddims

The way Dcamp & Lewismelo works fits in one sentence: Dylan and Lewis Campbell sing as a duo, and Lewis also makes the riddims. That double role changes the nature of the project. When the producer is inside the duo, the beat is not bought outside and then endured by the voices, it is built with them, for their tones, their relays and their way of answering each other. The riddim becomes an internal conversation before it becomes a public track.

That duo-plus-in-house-production model brings the project close to the founding bands of bouyon, who made their sound themselves, and sets it apart from circuits where an artist records over an anonymous beat. It also joins the family of profiles followed on our page about the producers and passers, except that here the passer and the singer are the same person. For the audience, the result is a more stable sound identity from track to track, because the producing hand does not change.

No verified official profile of the duo appears in our records, so this page gives no unconfirmed account link. The documented entry point remains the official BOUYON video on YouTube, and the coverage Bouyon Shattaz made of it.

BOUYON, the manifesto title

It takes a certain confidence to title your song BOUYON. The title does not only describe the song’s genre, it claims a place inside it. For a French-Creole duo, it is also a way of speaking to the audience of the French Antilles and France in its own musical language: there, bouyon is not an exotic novelty, it is the sound of parties, of circulating excerpts and of playlists, and the duo presents itself there with no user manual needed.

The official video, published on YouTube, is the source for the Bouyon Shattaz coverage. The platform drew a 55-second vertical edit from it, centred on the active section of the track, from 1:20 to 2:15, in its producer-reel style: the song full frame, the title in a pill, and the Guadeloupe flag placed by the title, the local Gwada flag with green bands, a red middle band and a yellow star. That flag is Smokiller’s branding choice for the duo’s coverage, it signals the French and Antillean side of their work as this site presents it, without asserting an origin our records do not confirm.

That care with framing and signs matters in the bouyon of 2026: a vertical excerpt circulates faster than the full video, and it often decides whether the audience will go and hear the whole track. To continue, the ROAD MIX bouyon playlist gathers the tracks Bouyon Shattaz puts forward, from the Dominican side and the French side of the genre alike.

The French and Creole side of a Dominican sound

Bouyon was born in Dominica, and its story, told on our page about the history of bouyon, is that of an island that invented a sound which became regional and then international. Dcamp & Lewismelo work that material from the other shore of its current history: the French Antilles and France, where Creole artists from several islands take up the Dominican riddim, sing it in Creole and French, and play it for a networked audience fed on excerpts and playlists.

That position makes the duo a good observatory of French-speaking bouyon. Neither a simple copy of the Dominican sound nor a product outside the genre, their work shows how a musical form travels when it is carried by artists who share Creole culture on both sides. The precise home islands of the two brothers are not confirmed in our records, so the page sticks to what is established: a French-Creole duo, an in-house producer, a manifesto title and an official video.

For a reader discovering the genre through this profile, the path is simple: the BOUYON video for the track in context, the Bouyon Shattaz edit for the vertical version made for social media, then the other artist profiles on this site to compare the Dominican, Guadeloupean, Martinican and French approaches to one same sound. Each comparison makes the duo’s own choices clearer: in-house riddims, a Creole duo format and a title that refuses any detour.
