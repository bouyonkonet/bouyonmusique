What is bouyon? The simple definition

By Smokiller · Published October 8, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Bouyon is a musical genre created in Dominica, a small Caribbean island, in the late 1980s. It is fast-tempo party and carnival music that mixes Dominican traditions with keyboards and electronic rhythms. In Guadeloupe and Martinique, it is also called jump up.

The bouyon recipe

Take jing ping, the traditional Dominican accordion music. Add cadence-lypso, the danceable sound of the 1970s. Put in traditional drum rhythms like lapo kabwit, elements of bèlè, kwadril, chanté mas and mazurka, a little zouk, then plug in electronic keyboards and a drum machine. You get bouyon: in Creole, the name of a soup where everything mixes together.

Who created it, and when

Bouyon was created by the band WCK, Windward Caribbean Kulture, formed in 1988 in Roseau, the capital of Dominica. Roots, Stems and Branches and First Serenade are among the other original groups. From the 1990s, bouyon becomes the dominant music of Dominican carnival.

How to recognize a bouyon song

A fast tempo that never lets up, a catchy keyboard loop, a heavy synthetic bass, percussion that recalls the traditional drum, and a voice in Creole or English that talks to the audience more than it sings. If the song makes you want to jump, you are probably listening to bouyon.

Frequently asked questions

Which country does bouyon come from?

From Dominica, an English-speaking and Creole-speaking Caribbean island, not to be confused with the Dominican Republic. The genre was born there in the late 1980s with the band WCK, formed in 1988 in Roseau, the capital. From the 1990s, it becomes the dominant music of Dominican carnival.

What does bouyon mean?

In Antillean Creole, a soup, a gumbo: the symbol of the mix that defines the genre. As in a soup, everything goes in: jing ping, cadence-lypso, traditional drums, a little zouk, electronic keyboards and a drum machine. See the page on the meaning of the word bouyon.

Who created bouyon?

Bouyon was created by the band WCK, Windward Caribbean Kulture, formed in 1988 in Roseau, the capital of Dominica. Roots, Stems and Branches and First Serenade are among the other original groups. From the 1990s, bouyon becomes the dominant music of Dominican carnival. See the WCK page.

How do you recognize a bouyon song?

A bouyon song has a fast tempo that never lets up, a catchy keyboard loop and a heavy synthetic bass. The percussion recalls the traditional drum, and the voice, in Creole or English, talks to the audience more than it sings. If the song makes you want to jump, you are probably listening to bouyon.

Are bouyon and jump up the same thing?

Yes, in everyday language. In Guadeloupe and Martinique, bouyon is also called jump up: it is the name the French Antilles give to this party and carnival music from Dominica, whose fast tempo makes everyone jump. See the jump up page.

Is bouyon carnival music?

Yes, first and foremost. From the 1990s, bouyon becomes the dominant music of Dominica's carnival, Mas Domnik, in Roseau, and each season launches the competition of bouyon songs, whose winners become the classics of the year. In Guadeloupe and Martinique, it also drives the carnivals under the name jump up. See Dominica Carnival.

What are the different styles of bouyon?

Around WCK's original bouyon, several forms grew: bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng, born in Dominica in the 1990s, bouyon soca, launched in Dominica by Dada in 2003, bouyon gwada, the hardcore branch born with Guadeloupe, and the shatta bouyon of today's producers. People also say old school bouyon for the sound of the live bands.

Is bouyon dance music?

Yes, it descends directly from dance music: the jing ping of Dominican balls, bèlè, kwadril and the chanté mas of carnival. There is no single official step: you sway on the refrains, jump together on the build-ups and answer the calls of the singer or the DJ. See the bouyon dance page.

Which instruments are used to make bouyon?

Two families of instruments. On one side, those of Dominican tradition: lapo kabwit, bélé drum, chakchak, syak or gwaj, accordion, bamboo flute and boom-boom. On the other, the machines of the 1980s: keyboards, synthesizers and drum machines, replaced today by producers' software. See the bouyon instruments page.

Is bouyon sung in Creole?

Yes, first in Kwéyòl, the Creole language of Dominica, mixed with English. This language carries the calls to the crowd, the jokes, the social commentary and the energy of chanté mas. Close to the Creoles of Guadeloupe, Martinique and Saint Lucia, it helped bouyon cross the sea. See bouyon and Kwéyòl.

Is bouyon a kind of soca?

No. Soca was born in Trinidad in the 1970s, from the fusion of calypso with Indian rhythms; bouyon was born in Dominica in the late 1980s, from jing ping and cadence-lypso. Bouyon is faster and rawer. The two genres meet in bouyon soca. See bouyon vs soca.

What should I listen to first to get into bouyon?

The founding bands, WCK, Roots Stems and Branches and First Serenade, then recent songs like Life Nice by Asa Bantan or Mauvais Plan by HYPNO.
