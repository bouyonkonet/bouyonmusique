Cadence-lypso: the first Dominican sound to travel

Published September 1, 2026

Before bouyon, Dominica had already invented a genre that travelled across the whole Caribbean: cadence-lypso. It is the other parent of bouyon, and the proof that the island is a land of musical invention.

Exile One, the Grammacks and the 1970s

In the 1970s, Dominican groups like Exile One and the Grammacks become the stars of cadence-lypso: an aggressive rhythm guitar, lyrics in Creole that comment on social life, a groove that makes the whole region dance. It is the first style of Dominican music to become popular in the Caribbean.

From cadence to bouyon

In 1988, WCK takes up the heritage of cadence-lypso and fuses it with jing ping, replacing part of the orchestra with keyboards and drum machines. Bouyon is born from this passing of the torch between Dominican generations. For what comes next, read the history of bouyon.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I start with bouyon?

Start with the history of bouyon: the genre was born in Roseau, Dominica, with WCK in 1988, out of cadence-lypso and jing ping. Then listen to the Bouyon Road Mix playlist by Bouyon Shattaz, on Spotify and YouTube, which mixes Dominica carnival classics with new bouyon.

Which country created bouyon?

Bouyon was born in Dominica, in the Caribbean, in the late 1980s. Its pioneering band, WCK (Windward Caribbean Kulture), formed in Roseau, the island's capital, in 1988, created it by fusing cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines.

What is cadence-lypso?

Cadence-lypso is a Dominican genre that bands like Exile One and the Grammacks carried across the Caribbean in the 1970s. It mixes Haitian cadence, calypso and the Creole language: a danceable cadence, an aggressive rhythm guitar and lyrics in Creole that comment on island life. It is the first Dominican style to become popular across the Caribbean.

What is the link between cadence-lypso and bouyon?

Cadence-lypso is one of bouyon's two parents. In 1988, WCK fused its heritage with jing ping and replaced part of the orchestra with keyboards and drum machines. From cadence, bouyon keeps the guitar heritage, the Creole storytelling and the party function. See the cadence-lypso roots of bouyon.

Which bands played cadence-lypso?

Exile One and the Grammacks are the Dominican stars of 1970s cadence-lypso. The Midnight Groovers from Grand Bay, formed in the late 1960s and led by Phillip “Chubby” Mark, have 39 albums and more according to their DOM767 profile, and are still on the World Creole Music Festival bill in 2026.

Who were Exile One and the Grammacks?

They are two Dominican bands that became the stars of cadence-lypso in the 1970s and carried the sound across the Caribbean. Exile One also left its mark on the French Antilles: its 1987 track L'hivernage, nicknamed The Yo, is associated with the term jump up, which described very rhythmic carnival music before it was given to bouyon.

Is cadence-lypso from Dominica or Haiti?

It is a Dominican genre. Cadence-lypso mixes Haitian cadence, calypso and the Creole language: it borrows one ingredient from Haiti, but its star bands of the 1970s, like Exile One and the Grammacks, were Dominican. It is the first style of Dominican music to become popular across the Caribbean, long before bouyon.

What is the difference between cadence-lypso and bouyon?

Cadence-lypso, the sound of the 1970s, relied on acoustic instruments, with acoustic drums and an aggressive rhythm guitar. Bouyon, created by WCK in 1988, puts the emphasis on keyboard rhythm patterns, drum machines and a fast carnival tempo. Yet it kept from cadence the guitar heritage, the Creole storytelling and the party function.

Where can you hear cadence-lypso in 2026?

On stage, at the World Creole Music Festival in Roseau: the Midnight Groovers, a cadence-lypso band from Grand Bay, are a night 1 act, on Friday, October 23, 2026, according to the festival's published programme structure. Today's bouyon acts play the same weekend, so you can hear the root and the genre a few hours apart.

Was cadence-lypso the first Dominican genre to travel?

Yes. Cadence-lypso is the first style of Dominican music to become popular in the Caribbean: in the 1970s its bands toured and its records circulated across the region. Bouyon followed in 1988. For an island with fewer than 70,000 inhabitants in its modern history, inventing these two travelling genres is remarkable.

Why does Grand Bay matter in cadence-lypso history?

Because Grand Bay, a village in the south of Dominica, is home to the Midnight Groovers, formed in the late 1960s and led by Phillip “Chubby” Mark. It is also the village of Asa Bantan, widely called the King of Bouyon. The cadence root and a modern figure of bouyon therefore share the same village.

What do cadence-lypso lyrics talk about?

They tell the life of the island in Creole: cadence-lypso is both party music and a sung social chronicle. Bouyon inherited this commentary role, on politics, village stories or carnival rivalries, with a faster delivery and a style close to the shout and the toast. See bouyon and Kwéyòl.
