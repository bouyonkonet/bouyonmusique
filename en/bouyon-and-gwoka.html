Bouyon gwada: gwoka, the drum of Guadeloupe

By Smokiller · Published June 19, 2026 · Updated September 22, 2026

Before zouk, before bouyon, there is gwoka. In Guadeloupean Creole, gwo ka means big drum: a family of hand drums and the music that goes with them, at the heart of Guadeloupe's folk music. Responsorial singing in Guadeloupean Creole, rhythms played on the ka drums and dancing: gwoka is one of the roots of zouk, and through zouk, a cousin of bouyon.

Gwoka, born in the 17th century

Gwoka emerged in the 17th century, during the transatlantic slave trade, from a process of creolization. Under the Code Noir, drums were forbidden to enslaved people in Guadeloupe, who then developed a vocal technique called bouladjel, which imitates drums.

UNESCO describes gwoka as a practice that combines responsorial singing in Guadeloupean Creole, rhythms played on the ka drums and dancing. It is found among all ethnic and religious groups of Guadeloupean society, and it was inscribed in 2014 on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Seven rhythms, two drums

Seven simple drum patterns form the basis of gwoka music: lewoz, kaladja, toumblak, graj, woule, mende and padjanbel. Each carries a meaning: lewoz is the war rhythm, and also an incantation dance; kaladja symbolises the struggle in love; toumblak deals with love, belly dance and fertility; padjanbel is the cane-cutting dance.

Graj accompanies agricultural work, woule is the creole waltz, and mende, the last rhythm to arrive, symbolises the collective celebration of carnival. Traditional gwoka is ideally played with at least two hand drums: the boula, the largest, which plays the central rhythm, and the make, smaller, which embellishes and interacts with the dancers, the audience or the singer. Ka drums were historically made from a recycled cured-meat keg for the body, a goat skin, tensioning metal hoops, wooden pegs and rope.

From gwoka to zouk, then to bouyon

Gwo ka is listed as one of the stylistic origins of zouk, and zouk as a fusion genre of gwo ka. Kassav played an important role in modernising gwoka: the band first played in a gwoka format, taking up songs from the mas a Sen Jan carnival tradition.

Modern gwoka adds instruments such as conga or djembe drums and chimes to electric bass guitar, while keeping the same seven fundamental rhythms. The most famous modern gwoka performer is William Flessel, whose song Message Ka, in 1994, became an international hit. Akiyo, a legendary drum group from Pointe-a-Pitre formed in 1978, reintroduced skin drums and traditional masks at carnival.

A living memory, from Sainte-Anne to UNESCO

In 1981, Gerard Lockel published the Traite de Gwoka moden, the first attempt to formalise the genre. In 1988, lawyer and nationalist activist Felix Cotellon created the Festival de Gwoka de Sainte-Anne, in Sainte-Anne. The festival and the drum groups keep alive a practice born more than three centuries ago.

The link with bouyon is indirect, and that should be said plainly: no opened source names an artist or a song that connects gwoka and bouyon directly. The verified bridge runs through zouk, of which gwoka is one of the roots, and bouyon counts zouk among its ingredients. That chain, gwoka then zouk then bouyon, makes the ka drum a cousin of the Dominican sound.
