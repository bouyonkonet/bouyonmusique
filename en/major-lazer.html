Major Lazer: global dancehall, next door to bouyon, never bouyon itself

Published October 20, 2026

Major Lazer is an American electronic music group formed in Los Angeles in 2008 by Diplo and Switch, later the trio of Diplo, Walshy Fire and Ape Drums, joined in 2025 by vocalist America Foster. This profile says it in the first line, and it repeats it at the end, because the distinction is the whole point of publishing the page on a bouyon site: Major Lazer is not a bouyon artist, and no verified bouyon recording by the group was found during this research, in its albums, its EPs, its remixes or its documented collaborations. Its place on this site is that of a major neighbour: a project built on Jamaican dancehall, fed by Trinidad and Tobago soca, which carried Caribbean carnival sounds to the biggest pop stages in the world, up to the remix of “Differentology” by Bunji Garlin.

Who Major Lazer is: one project, three eras, a cartoon hero

Major Lazer starts as a duo. Diplo, an American producer, and Switch, a British producer, met while working with the singer M.I.A., then founded the project in 2008. The name comes from a fictional Jamaican action hero invented for the project, and the first album, “Guns Don’t Kill People... Lazers Do”, was released on June 16, 2009 on Downtown Records. It was recorded in Jamaica at Tuff Gong Studios, a choice that already states the method: start from dancehall, do not imitate it from a distance.

The second era begins in 2011. Switch left the project and two recruits changed its centre of gravity: Jillionaire, a DJ from Trinidad and Tobago, and Walshy Fire, an MC and producer born in Kingston, Jamaica and raised in Florida, a co-founder of the Miami sound system Black Chiney. Group notices stress that their arrival consolidated the project Caribbean influence. Jillionaire in particular brought soca rhythms, which he knows from the inside, as his carnival mixtapes published in the early 2010s show.

The third era opens in June 2019, when Jillionaire left the group to focus on solo projects and Ape Drums replaced him. The trio of Diplo, Walshy Fire and Ape Drums released “Music Is the Weapon” in 2020, then the album “Piano Republik” in 2023 with South African act Major League DJz, on the amapiano side. In 2025, British-Jamaican vocalist America Foster became an official member, and the group released the mixtape “GYALGEBRA”. The project has therefore crossed three line-ups without changing its compass: the Caribbean dancefloor, widened to the whole world.

The studio albums form the backbone of the file: “Guns Don’t Kill People... Lazers Do” in 2009, “Free the Universe” in 2013, “Peace Is the Mission” in 2015 and “Music Is the Weapon” in 2020. Documented EPs orbit around them, including “Apocalypse Soon” in 2014, with Pharrell Williams and Sean Paul, and “Know No Better” in 2017, with Camila Cabello, Travis Scott, Quavo and J Balvin. The group also produced “Reincarnated”, the reggae album by Snoop Dogg under his Snoop Lion name. In March 2018, Billboard ranked it number five on its Dance 100 ranking of dance musicians.

Dancehall first: Tuff Gong, Vybz Kartel and “Pon De Floor”

The first album is a dancehall statement of intent. Its guest vocal list reads like a survey of Kingston at the end of the 2000s: Vybz Kartel, Busy Signal, Mr. Vegas, Turbulence, Ward 21, T.O.K and Leftside, alongside Santigold, Nina Sky, Amanda Blank and M.I.A. The group press release, published by Sacks & Co., describes Diplo and Switch as among the first to bring Caribbean dance and pop sounds into the American mainstream, combining them with other global influences.

The record that sums up that era is “Pon De Floor”, with the voice of Vybz Kartel. Its rhythm served as the framework for Beyoncé “Run the World (Girls)”, produced with Switch involvement, and the record remains, according to the same release, a dancefloor staple. That path says a lot about Major Lazer position in the industry: a group able to take a riddim built for dancehall and carry it, through production, into the centre of American pop.

“Hold The Line”, the album first single, and the “Pon De Floor” video, directed by Eric Wareheim, also set the project visual world: saturated colours, absurd humour, references to dancehall videos and Saturday morning cartoons. In June 2009, the group landed its first cover of The Fader magazine. The EP “Lazers Never Die” followed in 2010, with remixes including one by Thom Yorke. Dancehall stayed the raw material, but the packaging was already that of a global pop product.

For a reader of this site, the useful comparison is one of methods. Dominica bouyon was born in the live bands of Roseau, around WCK, before moving to studio producers. Major Lazer travels the opposite road: a studio project, designed by producers and DJs, recruiting Caribbean voices record by record. The two stories meet on the dancefloor, but they share neither the same birthplace, nor the same economy, nor the same starting audience. This profile keeps that distinction clear, from start to finish.

Soca in the project blood: Jillionaire, Trinidad and carnival

If dancehall is the foundation, soca is the second pillar, and it has a name: Jillionaire, real name Christopher Leacock, born in Trinidad and Tobago. Notices credit him with a mix of indie dance and big room house together with the Caribbean rhythms of dancehall and soca. In 2013, during Trinidad carnival, he published the “Fog Up D Place” mixtape for LargeUp, a selection of the season soca, from Kes to Bunji Garlin, which shows a Major Lazer member working soca like a Port of Spain DJ, not like a tourist.

Soca also enters the group catalogue through its collaborations. “Sound Bang”, with Machel Montano, was ranked by LargeUp among the best soca singles of 2014, described as calypso and EDM meeting, with the shared energy of carnival fetes and electronic festivals. Montano, the biggest star of modern soca, returned on the remix of “All My Love”, with Ariana Grande, the closing record of “Peace Is the Mission” in 2015. Soca is therefore not a decoration in Major Lazer work: it is carried by artists who carry it at home.

In 2017, the “Know No Better” EP contained “Front of the Line”, with Machel Montano and Jamaican artist Konshens, created for the Bacardi Sound of Rum campaign. The record itself speaks of its double belonging, to the sound of dancehall and the sound of soca. Three years later, in February 2020, the group surprise released the “Soca Storm” EP: three records, “Soca Storm”, “Wet It Up” and “King of the Party”, with Grenadian soca artist Mr. Killa on two records and Zeek on “Wet It Up”. A global pop group naming a whole EP after soca states plainly where it draws from.

This is where the link with this site becomes precise. Soca is one of the two parents of bouyon soca, the fusion started in Dominica by producer Dada in 2003, according to the signed testimony of Sean “Smokiller” Francis published on this site, then popularised by Triple Kay. Major Lazer works soca from Los Angeles and Miami, with Trinidad stars. Dada works the same soca from Roseau, plugged into the Dominican bouyon rhythm machine. Same ingredient, two kitchens: confusing the two would be an error of geography and of credit.

The “Differentology” remix: the best documented meeting point with carnival

The best documented Caribbean fact in the Major Lazer file is a single remix. “Differentology (Ready for the Road)”, by Bunji Garlin, was released on November 7, 2012 by Sheriff Music, written by Garlin and produced by Keron “Sheriff” Thomson, as one of the first offerings for the 2013 carnival season in Trinidad and Tobago. The record became the anthem of the season, then travelled far beyond the island: it was featured in an episode of Grey’s Anatomy, played in NBA arenas, and earned Garlin the Best International Performance award at the 2013 Soul Train Awards, according to VP Records.

Major Lazer remixed the record, and that remix circulated in its free Lazers Never Die series, then as a full record on Garlin “Differentology” album on VP and RCA, in a five minute version. VP Records recounts that the record caught the group attention, and that it jumped on the remix. For soca, the effect is concrete: a remix by a group at the top of global EDM puts a Port of Spain carnival anthem into the sets of festival DJs worldwide, without changing its voice, its writer or its island.

This remix also matters for the story told by this site, because Bunji Garlin is a passer between worlds. The same Garlin sings “Forward” on the Smoke Riddim of Dominican producer Dada, the hybrid riddim blending soca and Dominica bouyon. On one side, a global remix signed Major Lazer; on the other, a bouyon soca production from Roseau. The singer is the same, the function is the same: carry soca further than its island. But the two productions do not hold the same origin credit, and this profile does not blend them.

After “Differentology”, the roads kept crossing without merging. Garlin collaborated with Jack Ü, the duo of Diplo and Skrillex, on “Jungle Bae”. Major Lazer, for its part, kept recruiting in the Caribbean basin: Busy Signal on “Watch Out for This (Bumaye)” and later on “Sun Comes Up”, with Joeboy, Sean Paul on the “Apocalypse Soon” EP, Shenseea on “Tiny”, Skip Marley on “Can’t Take It From Me”, and Kranium on a record from the Extended edition of “Peace Is the Mission”. The English speaking Caribbean is, from end to end, the group first reservoir of voices.

“Lean On”, “Light It Up”, “Cold Water”: the global hit machine

The commercial peak arrives with the third album. “Lean On”, a collaboration with French producer DJ Snake and Danish singer MØ, was released on March 2, 2015 as the first single from “Peace Is the Mission”, released on June 1, 2015. The record reached number four on the US Billboard Hot 100 and number two in the United Kingdom. In November 2015, it was announced as the most streamed song in Spotify history at that time. Its video, shot in India and inspired by a group tour of the country, later passed two billion views on YouTube.

The same album holds “Light It Up”, with Jamaican singer Nyla, whose remixed version with Fuse ODG became a global hit in turn, and “Powerful”, with Ellie Goulding and Jamaican singer Tarrus Riley. The album peaked at number twelve on the Billboard 200. The recipe reads clearly: a short, memorable electronic production, a pop voice on the chorus, and a Caribbean colour carried by the guests. It is that recipe, inherited from dancehall and soca, which made Major Lazer a stadium group without renouncing island material.

In 2016, “Cold Water”, with Justin Bieber and MØ, debuted at number two on the Billboard Hot 100 and number one in the United Kingdom. “Run Up”, with PartyNextDoor and Nicki Minaj, followed in 2017, along with the title track “Know No Better”, with Camila Cabello, Travis Scott and Quavo, and “Sua Cara”, with Brazilian artists Anitta and Pabllo Vittar, which opened the group to the Brazilian audience. In 2019, “Que Calor”, with J Balvin and El Alfa, did the same on the side of reggaeton and of dembow from Santo Domingo in the Dominican Republic, not to be confused with Dominica, the island of bouyon.

“Music Is the Weapon”, in 2020, spreads the method across five languages, according to the group release: Nicki Minaj and K4mo on “Oh My Gawd”, Alessia Cara, Marcus Mumford, Paloma Mami, French Montana, Busy Signal and Shenseea elsewhere on the album. Its Reloaded edition was Grammy nominated, and “Rave de Favela”, with MC Lan, Anitta and Beam, was Latin Grammy nominated, according to the group notice on Qobuz. In 2023, “Piano Republik” plugged the project into South African amapiano. The constant, since 2009, is the same: spot the rising party sound somewhere in the world, and amplify it with voices from that place.

Between the two album peaks, the group also kept its catalogue working in public. In October 2018 it released “Major Lazer Essentials”, a ten year anniversary compilation gathering its greatest hits alongside new records, including “Blow That Smoke”, with Tove Lo, released on October 17, 2018. In March 2019 it followed with the “Africa Is the Future” EP, another sign of the method at work: rather than treating each regional sound as a one record visit, the project returned to the African pop and dance scenes it had already worked with on “Particula” and “Sua Cara”, and built a whole short release around that connection, two years before the amapiano album made the African link explicit at full length.

Major Lazer exact place next to bouyon: neighbour, amplifier, not origin

This profile ends with the question other profiles of the group do not ask: what does Major Lazer have to do with bouyon? The honest answer holds in three points. First, no verified bouyon recording by Major Lazer was found during the research for this page: no bouyon record in the catalogue, no documented collaboration with a Dominica bouyon band, no documented stop in Roseau. The group therefore does not appear here as a bouyon artist, and its schema classes it as a dancehall and soca crossover group.

Second, the indirect link is real, and it runs through soca. Bouyon soca, born in Dominica in 2003 according to the signed testimony published on this site, fuses Dominican bouyon with Trinidad and Tobago soca. Major Lazer spent a decade carrying that same soca to EDM and pop audiences worldwide, with Machel Montano, Mr. Killa and the Bunji Garlin remix. A listener who discovers soca through “Front of the Line” can then reach bouyon soca, then bouyon itself. That is a listening path documented on our bouyon playlist, not an invented lineage.

Finally, the contrast is instructive. Major Lazer shows what a global pop machine can do for a carnival sound: billions of streams, stadiums, official remixes. Bouyon, for its part, was born in Dominica, grew with tiny means by comparison, and still fights for its origin credit to be recognised, as our history of bouyon page recounts. Placing the two stories on the same site, without confusing them, is exactly the work of this profile: give Major Lazer its real size, global, and bouyon its own, Dominican.

To hear Major Lazer with the map in hand: start with “Pon De Floor” for the dancehall of the beginnings, “Differentology” in its remixed version for Trinidad carnival, “Sound Bang” and “Front of the Line” for the stated soca, then “Lean On”, “Light It Up” and “Cold Water” for the global pop. Then come back to the bouyon soca of Dada and the bouyon of WCK: you will hear two different answers, one from Los Angeles and one from Roseau, to the same Caribbean party question.

Frequently asked questions

Is Major Lazer a bouyon artist?

No. Major Lazer is an American electronic music group built on dancehall, soca, reggae and reggaeton. No verified bouyon recording by the group was found during the research for this profile. Its presence on this site rests on its documented links with soca and dancehall, the two neighbouring worlds of bouyon, and not on a bouyon origin.

Who are the members of Major Lazer?

The group was founded in 2008 by Diplo and Switch. Switch left in 2011, replaced by Jillionaire and Walshy Fire. Jillionaire left in June 2019, replaced by Ape Drums. The line-up of Diplo, Walshy Fire and Ape Drums was joined in 2025 by vocalist America Foster as an official member.

What is the link between Major Lazer and Bunji Garlin?

Major Lazer remixed “Differentology (Ready for the Road)”, the 2013 carnival anthem of Bunji Garlin, the soca singer from Trinidad and Tobago. The remix circulated in the group Lazers Never Die series, then on Garlin “Differentology” album on VP and RCA. Garlin also sings “Forward” on the bouyon soca Smoke Riddim of Dominican producer Dada.

What are Major Lazer biggest records?

The best documented records are “Pon De Floor” (2009), “Get Free” (2013), “Watch Out for This (Bumaye)” (2013), “Lean On” with DJ Snake and MØ (2015), announced as Spotify most streamed song of its time, “Light It Up” with Nyla (2015), “Cold Water” with Justin Bieber and MØ (2016), “Run Up” (2017), “Know No Better” (2017) and “Que Calor” (2019).

Did Major Lazer make soca?

Yes, in a documented and repeated way. Trinidadian Jillionaire, a group member from 2011 to 2019, comes from soca. The group recorded “Sound Bang” with Machel Montano (2014), “Front of the Line” with Machel Montano and Konshens (2017), and the “Soca Storm” EP (2020) with Mr. Killa and Zeek. Group notices list soca among its genres.

Where can I hear Major Lazer after bouyon, and bouyon after Major Lazer?

Hear “Soca Storm” and the “Differentology” remix for the group carnival side, then our bouyon soca page for the fusion born in Dominica in 2003, and our bouyon playlist for the sound of Roseau itself, from WCK to new Dominican releases.
