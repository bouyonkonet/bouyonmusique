History of bouyon: the genre Dominica gave the Caribbean

Bouyon was born in Roseau, Dominica, in the late 1980s. In less than a generation, it became the soundtrack of carnivals in Guadeloupe and Martinique, then a sound that all of France dances to.

Before bouyon: jing ping and cadence-lypso

Dominica has its own musical culture, rooted in its Creole traditions. Jing ping is its traditional dance music: an accordion leads the melody, accompanied by drums like the lapo kabwit, a boom boom and a bamboo flute. In the 1970s, Dominican bands like Exile One and the Grammacks carried cadence-lypso across the whole Caribbean: a danceable cadence, an aggressive rhythm guitar and lyrics in Creole that tell the story of island life.

When Martinican and Guadeloupean zouk dominated the airwaves in the 1980s, Dominican musicians looked for a sound of their own, modern, that kept the soul of their traditions. The answer would be bouyon.

1988: WCK creates bouyon

In 1988, the band Windward Caribbean Kulture, WCK, was formed in Roseau. Its musicians experimented with a fusion: cadence-lypso meets jing ping, traditional drum rhythms like the lapo kabwit and elements of old dances, bele, quadrille, chante mas and mazurka. Where cadence relied on acoustic instruments, WCK bet on technology: keyboards up front, synthetic rhythm patterns, fast tempo and carnival energy.

The name says it all: in Antillean Creole, bouyon means a soup, a gumbo. Everything blends, nothing is lost. Alongside WCK, Roots, Stems and Branches (RSB) and First Serenade are among the original groups of the genre.

The 1990s: the sound of Dominican carnival

In the 1990s, bouyon dominated the music scene in Dominica. It became the music of Roseau carnival, Mas Domnik, and of village fetes. Vocals evolved: alongside classic singing came a more spoken style, influenced by Jamaican dancehall and ragga, which would give branches like bouyon-muffin and reketeng. The language of bouyon is a mix of English and Dominican Creole, direct, festive, sometimes loaded with social commentary.

Jump up: bouyon crosses to Guadeloupe and Martinique

Dominica shares the Creole language with Guadeloupe and Martinique. So bouyon naturally crossed the sea. In the French Antilles, it took the name jump up and became essential at fetes and carnivals. Collaborations between Dominican and Guadeloupean musicians gave birth to bouyon gwada, the hardcore branch of the genre. For its part, the fusion with Trinidad soca produced bouyon soca, also popular in Saint Lucia and Antigua.

Today: bouyon in France and around the world

Bouyon now lives a second life in mainland France and on social media: Antillean producers turn it into riddims built for the club and for dance videos, under names like shatta bouyon. Bouyon songs earn certifications in France and the genre feeds the playlists of Gwada, Martinique, French Guiana and Paris.

This success raises a simple question, at the heart of the work of Bouyon Shattaz: bouyon was born in Dominica, and it is Dominica that must stay at the center of its history, its credits and its economy. That is why this site exists: to tell the story of bouyon in French, without ever erasing its origin.
