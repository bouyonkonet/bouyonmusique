History of bouyon: the genre Dominica gave the Caribbean

Bouyon was born in Roseau, Dominica, in the late 1980s. In less than a generation, it became the soundtrack of carnivals in Guadeloupe and Martinique, then a sound that all of France dances to. This page follows the thread, from cadence-lypso to the labels of 2026, and points to the full chapter at every step.

Before bouyon: jing ping and cadence-lypso

Dominica has its own musical culture, rooted in its Creole traditions. Jing ping is its traditional dance music: an accordion leads the melody, accompanied by drums like the lapo kabwit, a boom boom and a bamboo flute. It accompanies the ballroom dances inherited from the island's Creole history, quadrille, mazurka and bele.

In the 1970s, Dominican bands like Exile One and the Grammacks carried cadence-lypso across the whole Caribbean: a danceable cadence, an aggressive rhythm guitar and lyrics in Creole that tell the story of island life. Midnight Groovers of Grand Bay, formed in the late 1960s and led by Phillip "Chubby" Mark, are the living bridge between that era and today: they are still on the World Creole Music Festival bill in 2026. Grand Bay is also the village of Asa Bantan, presented on this site as the modern figure of bouyon: the root and the present share the same ground. The cadence-lypso roots chapter tells that lineage, and the Midnight Groovers page tells the band's story.

When Martinican and Guadeloupean zouk dominated the airwaves in the 1980s, Dominican musicians looked for a sound of their own, modern, that kept the soul of their traditions. The answer would be bouyon.

1988: WCK creates bouyon

In 1988, the band Windward Caribbean Kulture, WCK, was formed in Roseau. Its musicians experimented with a fusion: cadence-lypso meets jing ping, traditional drum rhythms like the lapo kabwit and elements of old dances, bele, quadrille, chante mas and mazurka. Where cadence relied on acoustic instruments, WCK bet on technology: keyboards up front, synthetic rhythm patterns, fast tempo and carnival energy.

WCK's move had two parents. Cadence brought the guitar, the Creole storytelling and the party function; jing ping brought its instruments and the memory of the dances. Keyboards and drum machines replaced part of the orchestra without replacing the intention: to make Dominica dance to its own rhythms, at the speed of its time.

The name says it all: in Antillean Creole, bouyon means a soup, a gumbo (see meaning of bouyon). Everything blends, nothing is lost. Alongside WCK, Roots, Stems and Branches (RSB) and First Serenade are among the original groups of the genre. The detail is on the origins of bouyon page, and why the credit still matters on the origin of bouyon page.

The 1990s: Mas Domnik and the band era

In the 1990s, bouyon dominated the music scene in Dominica. It became the music of Roseau carnival, Mas Domnik, and of village fetes. That carnival, held before Lent, is considered one of the most traditional in the Caribbean: it keeps chante mas, lapo kabwit groups, traditional characters and sensays. Bouyon found its natural ground there, because it is made from those traditions. Every season opens the competition of bouyon songs: bands and producers release their new tracks, the public picks its favourites, and the winners become the classics of the year (see Dominica carnival).

This was the era of live bands. After WCK, bands answered one another from fete to fete and from carnival to carnival. TK International, also known as Triple Kay Band and Triple Kay International, is a current representative of that tradition, and became one of the major Dominican references of the 2000s. The language of bouyon is a mix of English and Dominican Creole, direct, festive, sometimes loaded with social commentary.

Bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng: the ragga voice, then the sample

Through the 1990s, vocals evolved: alongside classic singing came a more spoken style, influenced by Jamaican dancehall and ragga. Bouyon-muffin was born from that mix, somewhere between singing, shouting and toasting. Its documented history starts from 1995, when Skinny Banton, later known as Shadowflow, worked with WCK and laid ragga-influenced vocals over bouyon rhythms.

The next step changed families: from the band to the DJ. The track "Party", with Joanne and DJ Cut of Bucktown Sounds, is cited as the bridge between bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng, the Dominican style built on sampled melodies and rap vocals: strip the rhythm out of an existing track, bring the bouyon drums and bass forward, and set the voice back down on that new base. The Bushtown Clan popularised the style, then Nursery Krew Inc. developed it in the early 2000s through original recordings, with artists and producers such as Burn Brain Jolly, Charlie Black, Watty and Smokiller.

A word on spelling, because it matters: the official spelling is 'Wrecketeng', and it comes from DJ Cut himself, the founding member who pioneered the style. The 'reketeng' spelling spread across the internet and picked up by publications up to Billboard is a misspelling that became common. This site writes Wrecketeng everywhere, including for the historical practice, and keeps 'reketeng' only to flag that widespread error.

The style is not just a memory: on December 3, 2025, Dominica News Online announced a collaborative album produced by Smokiller, devoted to the origins of Wrecketeng, with DJ Cut among the announced contributors.

2003: bouyon soca, started in Dominica

Bouyon soca is the most popular fusion born around bouyon: the fast rhythms and keyboards of Dominican bouyon meet the energy and the codes of Trinidad soca. The fusion was started in Dominica by Dada in 2003, according to the first-hand testimony of Smokiller, who was in the rooms with Dada and Nursery Krew. The style then took off when Triple Kay and other bands adopted it in their own version of bouyon.

Reference works often describe the label as born off the island: a direct participant's testimony says otherwise, and this site stands on it, signed. The bouyon soca page gives that testimony in full, with the rest of the fusion's journey beyond Dominica. In Dominica itself, purists insist on distinguishing bouyon from soca: the two families live side by side at carnival, but bouyon keeps a clear, recognizable style.

Jump up and the Gwada chapter

Dominica shares the Creole language with Guadeloupe and Martinique: bouyon needed no translation to cross the sea. In the French Antilles, it took the name jump up, a term that already existed there for very rhythmic carnival music, notably after Exile One's 1987 track L'hivernage, nicknamed The Yo.

Collaborations between Dominican and Guadeloupean musicians then gave birth to bouyon gwada, also called hardcore bouyon: drum machines up front, heavy basslines, Creole vocals sung or rapped, a club-ready format. It is also the bridge to modern forms like shatta bouyon. The Gwada from Dominica chapter tells that branch from the island of origin: the scene of Miimii KDS and of the New Bouyon Age wave, whose artist TiiTii NBA himself says, in substance, that bouyon is Dominica's and Guadeloupe is the second chapter.

Traffic now runs both ways: the Guadeloupean label More Sewo, founded in 2007 by Arturo Bellot, has signed the Dominican artist BONNA.

The WCMF: the home stage

Every October, during the independence celebrations, the World Creole Music Festival turns Roseau into the capital of Creole music: three nights at Windsor Park, where the bouyon and cadence of Dominica share the stage with the zouk of the French Antilles, the kompa of Haiti and guests from the diaspora. For a bouyon artist, it means playing at home, in front of the audience that saw the genre born. It is also the meeting point for collaborations and contracts (see the WCMF in context).

The 2026 edition, from Friday October 23 to Sunday October 25, sums up this history in three evenings. According to official communications and published guides, night 1 features TK International, Ridge and Midnight Groovers, with Maurane Voyer on the bill; night 2, Reo and Asa Bantan; night 3, Shelly with Signal Band. The cadence root, the live bands and the new generation on the same stage: few festivals offer such a live demonstration of their own history.

Women, producers and passeurs

The quick story often forgets two floors of the house. First, the women: headliners and hitmakers, not decoration, as the women of bouyon chapter puts it. Maurane Voyer, from Martinique, carries "Joli BB" with Reo. Miimii KDS, from Guadeloupe, scored a viral hit with "Sé Miimii" in 2025 and a SNEP Gold Single with "Pow Pow / Allo Doudou". Tamillia lends her voice to DJ Blax's "Don't Care", and Ebony Empress gives her name to the "Bounce 2D Riddim".

Then, the hands behind the sound. The producers and passeurs chapter names six: Smokiller and his Wrecketeng-style remixes, Denmath and his riddims, HYPNO and "MAUVAIS PLAN", Savion and the snippet format, MB On The Track and his custom beats, Dboss and his "Come Here" riddim. In October 2026, the agreement between Bouyon Shattaz and Denmath's team set a written framework: recording on a beat is free, but releasing a track requires the producer's prior written approval. The chapter's thesis: the next decade of bouyon will be a story of producers and rights as much as a story of singers.

The 2020s: New Bouyon, RnBouyon and the Wrecketeng legacy

The 2020s brought new labels. The term New Bouyon covers three distinct uses: TiiTii NBA's EP "New Bouyon Age", released on March 22, 2023 on GVE Music; the New Bouyon concept launched in 2024 by Bordeaux-based Ricky Bishop, according to his Deezer biography; and a generic use for contemporary bouyon that blends hip hop, trap, R&B and dancehall. RnBouyon, written as one word, names the fusion of bouyon and R&B: Deezer used it in February 2026 to present the singer Fallon in its Deezer Next 2026 programme, and Smokiller uses it as a production label, with a "RnBouyon Instrumental" chapter in his Bouyon Music 2026 mix.

The lineage does not stop at Smokiller. For Bouyon Shattaz, the 2020s labels claiming newness, New Bouyon and RnBouyon alike, are the heirs of Wrecketeng: they reuse its method, taking existing songs, hip hop, trap or R&B, stripping them down and rebuilding them on the bouyon machinery, sampled melodies, programmed percussion and electronic bass. The new labels change the vocabulary; the grammar was born in Roseau.

2026: the crossover, France and the future

Bouyon is living a second life in mainland France and on social media. France has become its main economic market: that is where tracks get certified and distribution is structured, and Paris and the big French cities are home to a Guadeloupean, Martinican, Guianese and Dominican diaspora (see bouyon in France).

In 2026, the evidence of the crossover keeps adding up, and the bouyon goes global chapter gathers it piece by piece. Smokiller flipped Coi Leray's "Nothing Into Something" into a bouyon version within two days of its release: as of October 19, 2026, the flip stood at 854,700 views on TikTok, and Coi Leray reshared it. His remix of Ayra Starr's "Heaven Baby" anchors a thirty-day campaign, from October 19 to November 16, 2026. "Pow Pow / Allo Doudou", by Niska, Litleboy LSBeats767 and Miimii KDS, was certified Gold Single by the SNEP at 15 million stream equivalents. And on October 20, 2026, Ricky Bishop plays his first solo concert in Paris.

This success raises a simple question, at the heart of the work of Bouyon Shattaz: Caribbean genres have already gone global while their island of origin stayed poor in rights and credit. The model described in the future of bouyon combines a media outlet that tells the origin, a distribution and rights desk for artists, and a network of pages and partners in the French Caribbean. Bouyon was born in Dominica, and it is Dominica that must stay at the center of its history, its credits and its economy. That is why this site exists: to tell the story of bouyon in French, without ever erasing its origin.
