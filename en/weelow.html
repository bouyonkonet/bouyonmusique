Weelow: Frappéy at 155 BPM and the bouyon hardcore, bouyon

By Smokiller · Published October 20, 2026

Weelow is a name the reference books know and the platforms have almost forgotten. The reference articles on the music of Guadeloupe, of France and of the Lesser Antilles cite him, under the spelling Wee Low, among the popular musicians of bouyon gwada, in the neighbourhood of bouyon hardcore, alongside Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza. On its side, the streaming catalogue keeps under the spelling Weelow only one verified track: “Frappéy”, a single released on 3 June 2012, at 155 BPM, whose credits name a composer called Wilow Désirade. This profile publishes those two documents, explains the branch of bouyon they attach the artist to, and states without detour what the public record does not give: no biography, no commune, no confirmed second track. A thin file, presented as such.

Who Weelow is: two spellings, one file

The file on Weelow starts with a spelling question, and this profile raises it instead of hiding it. The streaming platforms write the name as one word: Weelow, performer and songwriter of the single “Frappéy”. The reference articles on Antillean music write the name as two words: Wee Low, cited in a list of popular musicians of Guadeloupe bouyon. No source consulted writes in so many words that the two forms designate the same artist. This profile treats them as one and the same name, on the basis of three matches every reader can judge: the same genre, the bouyon of Guadeloupe; the same period, the early 2010s for the single as for the writing of the lists; and the absence of any other competing artist carrying either form in this scene.

That caution is not an editorial detail. The bouyon gwada of the early 2010s is a scene of videos, parties and short stage names, where spellings float from one flyer to the next and official registers are rare. A single artist can exist there under two spellings without anyone ever having had to align them. By publishing both forms and the basis of the match, this profile does what the reference lists do not do: it shows its reasoning, and it will remain correctable if a new document one day separates the two names.

For the rest, the record is completely dry. No artist biography, no interview, no label page and no verified official account was found during this research. The full name of the artist is given by no source consulted, any more than his home commune or his date of birth, so none of those elements is printed here. What the file holds fits in two documents: a citation in the reference works, and a single. The following sections read them one by one.

The citation: Wee Low in the reference articles on the music of Guadeloupe

The first document is a list. The reference article on the music of Guadeloupe gives a section to bouyon gwada. It recalls there that bouyon is a form of popular music of Dominica, known as jump up in Guadeloupe and Martinique, that the best known band of the genre is WCK, who originated the style in 1988, and that the Guadeloupe form grew with Dominican bands touring the French Antilles and with exchanges around the World Creole Music Festival of Dominica. The text then presents bouyon hardcore as a popular offshoot within bouyon gwada, characterised by lewd and violent lyrics, and it closes with the names: popular bouyon gwada musicians include Wee Low, Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza.

That list does not exist in a single copy. The same passage, with the same four names in the same order, is carried by the reference articles on the music of France and on the music of the Lesser Antilles. In other words, the name of Wee Low travelled through the whole general documentation on Antillean music, copied from article to article for over a decade. It is a strange form of consecration: to be cited everywhere as a popular name of a branch, without any of those articles writing a single further line about him. No track, no date, no town. The list says he mattered; it does not say what he did.

The list companions draw the exact neighbourhood of that citation. Suppa, the voice of the Vadore Concept circle who died in May 2013 according to the Rebel Up collective, has his page on this site. Doc J, cited in the same list, has his as well, built on the same kind of thin file. Yellow Gaza completes the quartet. Weelow is therefore in good documentary company: that of the voices of hardcore bouyon gwada whose written trace is inversely proportional to the place the references grant them. Our Suppa page and our Doc J page tell the two neighbouring files, with their own documents.

“Frappéy”: the verified single of 3 June 2012, at 155 BPM

The second document is a record, and it is the only one. The record page of the track “Frappéy”, consulted on Shazam for this profile, gives a precise release: 3 June 2012, on Bouyon King and LABEL BOUYON MUSIC, as a single titled “Frappéy - Single”. The page classifies the track as World, states the French language, and measures its tempo at 155 BPM. That tempo is not an idle technical detail: 155 BPM is the regime of party bouyon in its fastest form, the same regime the Rebel Up collective noted for its bouyon hardcore mixtape of the same period. The only kept track of Weelow runs at exactly the speed of the branch in which the references cite him.

The credits on the page are short, and they open the only biographical lead of the file, without closing it. Performer: Weelow. Songwriter: Weelow. Composer: Wilow Désirade. That last name reads like a full signature, a stage first name, Wilow, close to the artist name, followed by a second element, Désirade, which is also the name of an island of the Guadeloupe archipelago, La Désirade. This profile prints that credit for what it is, a composer credit published by the platform, and refuses to draw an origin from it: no source says the artist was born in La Désirade, lived there or took his name from it, and turning a credit into a birthplace would be exactly the kind of invention this site forbids itself.

The page also counted, at the moment it was opened, a little over a thousand recognitions on the application. That is little, and it is consistent with the rest of the file: a 2012 track, released at a time when bouyon gwada lived on video and direct download more than on organised streaming, and whose platform trace has shrunk to this single page. No album, no other single and no full artist page was found under the name Weelow during this research. The schema of this page therefore carries only one recording, “Frappéy”, rather than an inflated list of unverified titles.

Bouyon hardcore: the branch where the references place Weelow

To understand the place the lists give to Wee Low, the branch they describe has to be heard. Bouyon hardcore is presented by the reference articles as the hard form of bouyon gwada: raw lyrics, often sexually explicit or violent, carried by heavy, stripped down riddims, on the border of dancehall, sung in Creole, French or English depending on the track. The Rebel Up collective, which documented this scene at the very moment it was running, described it in the same terms and noted the debates it caused in Guadeloupe, up to calls to regulate it as adult content.

That branch was born of a circulation, and the references tell that too. Bouyon, created in Dominica at the end of the 1980s by bands such as WCK, was adopted in Guadeloupe under the name jump up, then reworked by Guadeloupe artists into bouyon gwada, through Dominican tours and the World Creole Music Festival. Its hardcore version matches the moment when the party and video scene, in the early 2010s, hardened the lyrics and sped up the tempos. The single by Weelow, dated June 2012 and measured at 155 BPM, falls right inside that moment, which supports, without proving it on its own, the citation in the references.

What that belonging is worth, for a name without a catalogue, has to be weighed. To be placed by the references in the hardcore branch of bouyon gwada is to be placed in the rawest and most debated current of the genre, that of the parties argued over as adult only in the debates of the time, of videos at a few tens of thousands of views, and of names that circulated by word of mouth more than through the press. Whole careers of that scene fit in a handful of videos and a local reputation. That the career of Weelow fits, in the searchable record, in one single and one citation says less about the artist than about the way that scene was archived: barely at all. Our bouyon gwada page retraces the whole branch, from its Dominican origins to its present forms.

What this profile knows, what it does not, and why it exists anyway

Let the file be summed up, because on a profile this thin, clarity is the only form of honesty available. Claimed and sourced: the reference articles on the music of Guadeloupe, of France and of the Lesser Antilles cite Wee Low among the popular musicians of bouyon gwada, in the neighbourhood of bouyon hardcore, alongside Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza. Claimed and sourced as well: a single, “Frappéy”, was released on 3 June 2012 under the name Weelow, on Bouyon King and LABEL BOUYON MUSIC, with Weelow as performer and songwriter and Wilow Désirade as composer, at a measured tempo of 155 BPM.

Not established, and therefore not printed: the full name of the artist, his commune, his date of birth, any second track, and the formal equivalence between the spellings Weelow and Wee Low, which this profile sets out as an argued match and not as an archive fact. No official account is linked here, for lack of a URL verified by direct opening, and the recording list in the schema is limited to the single verified title. If a new document appears, a Guadeloupe press article, a label record, a testimony from the circle of the time, this profile will be completed, corrected and dated, like every page on this site.

Why publish a profile on two documents? Because the reference lists, for their part, have already published the name, with nothing behind it. A reader who searches for Wee Low after reading the article on the music of Guadeloupe finds, before this page, only an orphan single and lists copying each other. This profile at least gathers the two traces, says which one is a record and which one is a citation, and refuses to manufacture the rest. In the history of bouyon gwada, where so many voices left neither album nor archive, keeping that exact register is worth more than one more silence. Frequently asked questions

Who is Weelow?

Weelow is an artist cited by the reference articles on the music of Guadeloupe, under the spelling Wee Low, among the popular musicians of bouyon gwada, in the neighbourhood of bouyon hardcore, alongside Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza. Under the spelling Weelow, only one single is verified: “Frappéy”, released on 3 June 2012. No published biography of the artist was found during the research for this profile.

Are Weelow and Wee Low the same artist?

This profile treats the two spellings as the same name, and it states what that match rests on: the same genre, the bouyon of Guadeloupe; the same period, the early 2010s; and no other competing artist under either form in this scene. No source consulted writes the equivalence in so many words, so this profile presents it as an argued match, correctable if a new document appears.

What is the verified track by Weelow?

“Frappéy”, a single released on 3 June 2012 on Bouyon King and LABEL BOUYON MUSIC, as “Frappéy - Single”, according to the track record page consulted for this profile. The page classifies it as World and states the French language. No album, no other single and no full artist page were found under the name Weelow during this research.

Who wrote and composed “Frappéy”?

The track page credits Weelow as performer and songwriter, and Wilow Désirade as composer. That last name looks like a full signature, with a stage first name close to the artist name, but no source says whether it is the same person. This profile prints those credits as the platform publishes them, without drawing more from them.

Does Weelow come from La Désirade?

The public record does not say so. The only published element is a composer credit, Wilow Désirade, on the record page of “Frappéy”. Désirade is also the name of an island of the Guadeloupe archipelago, La Désirade, but no source consulted says the artist was born there, lived there or took his name from it. This profile prints the credit and refuses to turn it into an origin.

What is the tempo of “Frappéy”?

The track page measures “Frappéy” at 155 BPM, the regime of party bouyon in its fastest form. It is the same regime the Rebel Up collective noted for its bouyon hardcore mixtape of the same period: the only surviving Weelow track therefore runs at exactly the speed of the branch where the references cite him.

What is bouyon hardcore, the current where the references place Weelow?

According to the reference articles, bouyon hardcore is a popular offshoot within bouyon gwada, the Guadeloupe form of the bouyon born in Dominica, characterised by lewd and violent lyrics. The name Wee Low appears in the list of popular musicians of that current, with Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza, in the early 2010s.

Was bouyon hardcore controversial in Guadeloupe?

Yes. The Rebel Up collective, which documented the scene while it was running, described raw lyrics, often sexually explicit or violent, over heavy and stripped riddims, and noted the debates it caused in Guadeloupe, up to calls to treat it as adult only content. That is the current the references attach to the name Wee Low.

Which artists are cited alongside Wee Low?

Suppa, Doc J and Yellow Gaza. The four names form the list of popular bouyon gwada musicians in the reference article on the music of Guadeloupe, repeated word for word by the articles on the music of France and of the Lesser Antilles. Suppa, the voice of the Vadore Concept circle, died in May 2013 according to the Rebel Up collective. See our Suppa page.

Is Weelow’s bouyon from Guadeloupe or Dominica?

Bouyon comes from Dominica, where bands like WCK created it in the late 1980s. Guadeloupe adopted it under the name jump up, then its artists reworked it into bouyon gwada, through Dominican tours and the World Creole Music Festival. Weelow belongs to that Guadeloupe form. Why does Weelow have so few traces online?

Because his 2012 track came out when bouyon gwada lived on video and direct download more than on organised streaming. Whole careers of that scene fit into a few videos and a local reputation. The platform trace of Weelow has shrunk to a single track page, which counted a little over a thousand Shazam recognitions when it was opened.
