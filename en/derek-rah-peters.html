Derek Rah Peters: the drum and the voice of the early WCK

Published October 20, 2026

Derek “Rah” Peters was the drummer, the lead singer and the principal songwriter of WCK during the band formative years, according to Timothy Rommen academic study of the music of Dominica. He is therefore one of the voices, and one of the drums, that turned the Roseau fusion into a named genre: bouyon. This profile follows his own thread, distinct from the keyboards of Cornell Phillip: a child from Rosalie formed by jing ping from the age of two, a carnival drumming corps that made its own drums, then the band that modernized those rhythms, and finally, in 2024, the cultural icon teaching drumming to the children of the Dominica State College.

Who Derek Rah Peters is: three roles, three spellings

The Culture of Dominica reference page sums up his stature in one sentence: Derek “Rah” Peters, born in Dominica, is considered to be one of the most influential figures in the development of the bouyon genre. The wording is attributed here as it stands in that source, in line with this site rule on titles and reputations. What is documented without any crown, by contrast, is precise and remarkable: in the band that created bouyon, Rah Peters held the drums, the lead vocals and the principal songwriting at the same time, during the years when the sound was taking shape.

Timothy Rommen study, published in the Journal of Musicology and based on interviews conducted in Dominica in October 2012, gives the three roles in one line: Rah was WCK lead singer, principal songwriter and drummer during the band formative years. The member lists published by Wikipedia and Alchetron confirm it in shorter form, placing him among the former members with drums and lead vocals. A drummer who sings and writes is not an accompanist: he is a centre of gravity, and this profile treats him as one.

One word of honesty about his first name, because it is part of the record and almost nobody flags it. The sources opened for this research write it three ways: Derek in DOM767, which names him twice, in its Cornell Phillip profile and in its article on the Dominica State College summer camp; Derick in the WCK member lists and in the Civic Vibes programme listing; Derrick in Rommen academic article and in Culture of Dominica. It is the same man, the drummer and singer nicknamed Rah. This profile uses Derek, the form carried by DOM767 and by this page address, and flags the variants so that every search reaches the same record. The FAQ below returns to it.

His nickname, by contrast, never varies: Rah. It is central enough to his public identity for a 1999 WCK track, “Mic Rah Phone”, to carry it in full, a pun on microphone that this profile reads as a nod to the singer, without turning it into a writing credit no source publishes. The rest of this profile follows the order of his own story: first the village and the drums of childhood, then arrival in an already named band, then the records, then the teaching.

Rosalie: jing ping from the age of two

The richest testimony about Rah Peters is his own. Rommen interviewed him on 24 October 2012, and the article quotes his memories to explain how much his early experiences with carnival drumming and jing ping affected his approach to composition. The starting point is a village: Rosalie, which the article presents as a small village on the Atlantic coast of Dominica. It was there, Peters recalls, that he followed his people from small, drawn to a quadrille group playing jing ping, the traditional Dominican music of accordion, boumboum, siyak and tanbou.

His words, as the interview reports them, deserve to be heard directly: from when he was two years old, he says, he was seeing everything, and that is where he really experienced that jing ping kind of vibe. Two years old: at the age when most children are learning to talk, Rah Peters was already absorbing, as a regular watcher of the village quadrille group rehearsals and outings, the rhythmic repertoire that would become, twenty years later, one of the declared ingredients of bouyon. The genre has documented roots in the houses of Roseau, at the Phillips; it also has roots in the villages of the east coast, in the children who watched the elders play.

The second ingredient of his childhood is carnival, and more precisely carnival drumming. Peters recalls that they had a drumming corps, the lapo kabwit, those ensembles of goatskin drums with scrapers and shakers that animated the processions, and that they used to make their own drums to drum when it was carnival time. Making your instrument before you know how to play it in a professional band: the sequence matters. The future WCK drummer did not receive rhythm from a music school, he built it with his hands, in the street, for the fete.

Rommen draws from these interviews a conclusion this profile repeats because it rests on three converging witnesses, Cornell Phillip, Rah Peters and bass player Keith Joseph: jing ping and lapo kabwit emerge as significant influences on bouyon. In other words, Rah part in the birth of the genre is not only technical. Where the Phillip brothers brought the machines and the electronic translation of the instruments, Peters brought the lived memory of the two traditions bouyon would fuse: the quadrille dance heard in Rosalie and the carnival drum made and played from childhood. A drummer formed that way hears bouyon before it exists.

Joining WCK: arriving after the core, becoming the voice

The exact order of his arrival in WCK is one of the most interesting points in his record, and one that quick summaries tell wrongly. Rommen writes plainly that Peters joined WCK after they had already recruited the core personnel and named the band, by which time Cornell and Keith had decided to modernize Dominica traditional rhythms. DOM767, for its part, places him among the original members gathered in 1988 around Phillip, alongside Keith Goddard, to create a new sound that resonated with the youth. The two sources do not truly contradict each other: they describe a formation in layers, a Phillip core and its close circle first, then the quick arrival of the drummer singer who would carry the formative years.

What Peters says about that meeting is revealing: why the WCK approach touched him, he recalls, was because of the drums, carnival as he tells it, and the jing ping music that the band incorporated into bouyon. He recognized, in the others project, the music of his own childhood. The junction was therefore natural on both sides: the band was looking to modernize rhythms Peters had held in his hands since childhood, and Peters found a band that took those rhythms seriously, with keyboards, a drum machine and stage ambition.

The result is the triple role already cited: lead singer, principal songwriter, drummer, during the formative years. It is worth measuring what that means in a band of the late 1980s. The voice the public heard on the first hits was his; the songs the band built came largely from his pen; and the tempo, the one everything else locked onto, came out of his drum kit. Histories of bouyon often tell the genre as a keyboard story, and Fingers keyboards carry a great deal of it. But a keyboard does not sing the choruses and does not write the songs: during the years when WCK invented its formula, Rah Peters held those two posts, while also keeping time.

The band he joins has a path our WCK page tells in full: formed in 1988 in Roseau to fill a void left by Dominica great bands that had left or stopped, it heralded a resurgence of live music on the island and experimented with the fusion of cadence-lypso and jing ping. References agree in crediting WCK with creating bouyon in the late 1980s. Peters place in that creation is that of a co-founder in fact rather than in law: absent from the first meeting, present as soon as the sound took shape, and inseparable from the records that defined it.

The WCK years: from Culture Shock to Mic Rah Phone

The band published discography gives the measure of those years. WCK debuted in 1988 with the album “One More Sway”, coinciding with Reunion Year, the tenth anniversary of independence. The 1990 album, “Culture Shock”, is described by the discographies as the defining moment for the band, with the title track and “Dance Floor”. Then come “Follow the Leader” in 1991, delivering “Follow the Leader” and “Land Of Sunshine”, then “Kannibal” in 1992, a further step in fine tuning the new sound. Peters is the drummer, the voice and the principal writer of this building period, the one in which bouyon moves from experiment to signature.

In 1993, the album “Forever” produced one of the band biggest hits, “Conch Shell”, also called “Vola Vole” or “The Fish Song”. In 1995, “Tou Cho Tou Flam”, presented as WCK most successful album, generated seven hits out of eleven tracks, including the huge “Balance Batty”, still popular today and described by Peters peers as one of the first big bouyon crossover hits to travel from island to island. The published list of notable tracks continues with “Mete Veye” and “Drum Song” in 1996, the latter title reading, for a singing drummer, like a calling card, then “Iron” in 1997 and “Marathon” and “Bouyon Connection” in 1998.

From 1995 to 1998, the band turned that success into tours of the United States, Canada, Europe and the Caribbean, packing dance halls, concert halls and arenas, and playing the Apollo Theater, according to the account published by Billboard. For the boy from Rosalie who made his own carnival drums, the distance is considerable, and it is measured in audiences: the rhythm born in village processions and quadrille rehearsals now held entire halls on three continents, carried by the drums and the voice of the same man.

The band official hit list dates the track “Mic Rah Phone” to 1999, on the album “Set My People Free”. No source opened for this research publishes the writing credit for that specific track, and this profile invents none. But the pun is there, in the middle of the discography: the nickname of the singing drummer turned into a song title, like a signature left in the catalogue. Member lists today place Peters among the former members of WCK, alongside Cornell Phillip and Pellam Jno Baptiste, the band other drummer. His own era is that of the formation and the first summits, and the recordings gathered in this page schema are the WCK recordings of that era, credited to the band.

The cultural icon: passing the drum to the children

Rah Peters second public life is that of a transmitter, and it is documented by recent Dominican press. In June 2024, DOM767 announced the launch of the Dominica State College A.C.T.S summer camp, at Stockfarm: four weeks, from 15 July to 9 August 2024, for two groups of children, aged 6 to 11 and 12 to 15, around four pillars, agriculture, culture, tourism and science. For the week focused on culture, the article lists drumming sessions by cultural icon Derek “Rah” Peters, alongside Creole language sessions, basket weaving and cultural dance appreciation. The cultural icon wording is DOM767; this profile quotes it with its attribution, and notes what it concretely covers: the man of the carnival lapo kabwit teaching drumming, in person, to schoolchildren in Roseau.

The loop with his childhood is hard to miss. The child from Rosalie who followed the quadrille group from the age of two and made his own drums for carnival became, forty years later, the professional the national college invites to put drums in the hands of children aged 6 to 15. Between the two stand the WCK records and the creation of a genre. The teaching is therefore not a retirement: it is the same gesture, continued. Bouyon was born when young people translated traditional rhythms onto machines; it survives when its inventors show children where those rhythms come from.

The same year, 2024, shows him in another role, that of official witness to the history of the genre. On 20 October 2024, the Civic Vibes programme devoted its live streamed show to the trending cultural topics of the moment: Gregory Rabess, cultural icon, discussed Dominica roots and culture in the first segment, then Derek “Rah” Peters, billed as the Original Bouyan Pioneer, took the second segment to discuss the impact of bouyon music on the culture in Dominica. The listing spells Bouyan, a spelling this profile flags without adopting it. The substance, however, is clear: when a Dominican cultural programme looks for the voice of the bouyon origins, it turns to its singing drummer, thirty-six years after “One More Sway”.

Why Rah matters, and what the archives do not say

Rah Peters matters first because bouyon, without a singing and writing drummer, does not sound the same. Accounts of the genre stress the fine technical invention: the accordion turned into a keyboard, the boumboum turned into a bass, the siyak and tambal turned into a drum machine. But a genre is not only a device: it is songs, a voice that carries them and a tempo that makes crowds dance. During the formative years of WCK, those three functions were gathered in the hands, the pen and the throat of a single musician, come from an Atlantic coast village with jing ping and lapo kabwit as his whole training. That is a part of the invention that machine centred histories forget, and that Rommen study took care to record.

He matters next as living proof of the real geography of bouyon. The genre was born in Roseau, in the houses and studios of the capital, but its ingredients came from the whole island: the jing ping groups Daryl Phillip recorded village after village, the lapo kabwit carnival processions, and children like Peters, in Rosalie, who grew up inside those sounds before carrying them into town. Reading his path corrects the map: bouyon is a capital city music made with village rhythms, and its drummer is one of the couriers between the two worlds.

This profile also states its limits, because they are real. No solo discography of Derek Rah Peters is documented in the sources opened for this research, so no solo title is attributed to him here: the recordings listed on this page are those of WCK, the band of which he was drummer, lead singer and principal songwriter in the formative years, without a track by track split that the sources do not publish. No verified official account of the musician was confirmed during this research, so this page links to no social profile. His dates of birth and the exact span of his years in the band do not appear in our sources either, and they are not guessed.

Until any additions arrive, listening follows the band catalogue: “Culture Shock”, “Conch Shell”, “Balance Batty” and “Drum Song” in our bouyon playlist, the full story on our WCK page, and the genre context on our history of bouyon page. Behind each of those formative years titles stands the same simple fact: the rhythm came from a Rosalie child who made his own drums, the voice was his, and the songs, for the most part, came from his pen.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Derek Rah Peters and what did he do in WCK?

Derek “Rah” Peters is a musician born in Dominica, the drummer, lead singer and principal songwriter of WCK during the band formative years, according to Timothy Rommen academic study based on an interview with him on 24 October 2012. WCK member lists place him among the former members, drums and lead vocals. The Culture of Dominica page describes him as considered one of the most influential figures in the development of bouyon, a wording this site reports with its attribution.

Why is his first name spelled Derek, Derick and Derrick in different sources?

Because the sources themselves vary, and this profile is one of the few to say so openly. DOM767 writes Derek, in its Cornell Phillip profile and in its June 2024 article on the Dominica State College camp. The WCK member lists and the Civic Vibes programme listing write Derick. Timothy Rommen academic article and the Culture of Dominica page write Derrick. All three spellings name the same drummer and singer nicknamed Rah; this site uses Derek and flags the variants so that every search reaches the same record.

What did the village of Rosalie and jing ping give Rah Peters?

His own testimony, collected by Rommen in 2012, answers: as a child in Rosalie, a small village on the Atlantic coast of Dominica, he followed his people and watched the local quadrille group playing jing ping from the age of two, and that is where he really experienced that vibe. He also recalls the lapo kabwit carnival drumming corps, for which they made their own drums. Rommen concludes from his interviews with Peters, Cornell Phillip and Keith Joseph that jing ping and lapo kabwit are significant influences on bouyon.

Did Derek Rah Peters found WCK?

The documented answer is nuanced, and that is what makes it interesting. Rommen writes that he joined WCK after the core personnel had been recruited and the band named, when Cornell and Keith had already decided to modernize Dominica traditional rhythms. DOM767 nevertheless places him among the original members gathered in 1988 around Cornell Phillip. He is therefore a co-founder in fact: he arrived in the first hour, then served as drummer, lead singer and principal songwriter during the years when the sound of WCK and of bouyon was formed.

What is “Mic Rah Phone”?

It is a WCK track dated 1999 in the band hit list, the year of the album “Set My People Free”. Its title carries the Rah nickname in full, in a pun on the microphone. No source opened for this research publishes the writing credit for that specific track, so this profile presents it as a visible nod in the catalogue, not as a song whose authorship is established.

What does Derek Rah Peters do today?

Recent sources show him as a transmitter and a witness. In 2024, DOM767 presents him as the cultural icon leading the drumming sessions of the Dominica State College A.C.T.S summer camp, for children aged 6 to 15. On 20 October 2024, the Civic Vibes programme received him as the original bouyon pioneer to discuss the impact of the genre on Dominican culture. No recent solo activity or discography is documented in the sources opened for this profile.
