Reggae, the Jamaican thread in bouyon

By Smokiller · Published March 17, 2026 · Updated November 10, 2026

Reggae was born in Jamaica in the late 1960s, from mento, ska and rocksteady. Listed as UNESCO intangible cultural heritage in 2018, it travelled the whole world. Its thread also runs through bouyon: the ragga vocals of the early WCK years, bouyon-muffin, Wrecketeng sampling dancehall, and the World Creole Music Festival stages, where reggae and bouyon share the same bill.

From ska to reggae

Ska came first, in the late 1950s, then slowed into rocksteady in the mid-1960s, before giving way to reggae in the late 1960s. The word reggae was popularised by Toots and the Maytals' single Do the Reggay, in 1968, the first popular song to use the word.

Reggae's signature is a slower tempo than ska and rocksteady, a strong downbeat in drum and bass, short staccato guitar and piano chords on the offbeat, a central bass guitar with a thick, heavy tone, call-and-response patterns, and vocals often in Jamaican Patois. The Wailers, Bob Marley, Peter Tosh and Bunny Wailer, formed in 1963, are the band that went through all three stages: ska, rocksteady and reggae.

Bob Marley and the worldwide spread

Bob Marley, born Robert Nesta Marley on 6 February 1945 in Nine Mile, in the parish of Saint Ann, Jamaica, died on 11 May 1981 in Miami aged 36, remains the most widely known and best-selling performer of reggae, and the one credited with spreading Jamaican music and the Rastafari movement worldwide. His posthumous compilation Legend, in 1984, is the best-selling reggae album of all time.

Sound systems, the mobile speaker stacks run by selectors, such as Duke Reid's Trojan and Clement Coxsone Dodd's Downbeat, were the backbone of Jamaican music since the 1950s and the birthplace of ska. Toasting by deejays such as U-Roy and Big Youth, over records in the late 1960s and early 1970s, grew out of that sound-system culture.

The Jamaican thread in bouyon

WCK built bouyon by mixing native drum rhythms such as lapo kabwit, elements of the music of jing ping bands and ragga-style vocals. Bouyon-muffin is a modern offshoot that combines Jamaican raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall. Its most influential figure is Skinny Banton, now Shadowflow, who has collaborated with WCK since 1995, chanting ragga-influenced vocals over bouyon rhythms.

Wrecketeng is a bouyon style based on sampling and remixing existing dancehall and hip-hop recordings over bouyon instrumentals. DJ Cut and Bucktown Sounds' Bushtown Clan are named as its originators, and DJ Cut is its founder. On the internet, the style is often written reketeng, a widespread misspelling; the official spelling, given by DJ Cut himself, is Wrecketeng.

Reggae on the WCMF 2026 stage

The World Creole Music Festival 2026, the 26th edition, launched in 1997, was held from 23 to 25 October at Windsor Park in Roseau. Its programme covered bouyon, cadence-lypso, kompa, zouk, reggae, dancehall and soca, and it put reggae and dancehall on the same bill as bouyon.

The first night, Friday 23 October, featured Jamaican reggae icon Capleton and dancehall star Skillibeng. The second night, Saturday 24 October, featured Jamaican dancehall star Alkaline. The closing night, Sunday 25 October, closed with British reggae star Maxi Priest.

Frequently asked questions

When was reggae born?

In the late 1960s in Jamaica, from mento, ska and rocksteady. The word reggae was popularised by Toots and the Maytals' single Do the Reggay, in 1968. Reggae has been listed as UNESCO intangible cultural heritage since 2018.

How does reggae touch bouyon?

Through the ragga vocals of the early WCK years, through bouyon-muffin, which combines Jamaican raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall, and through Wrecketeng, which samples and remixes dancehall and hip-hop recordings over bouyon instrumentals.

Who carried reggae at WCMF 2026?

Capleton and Skillibeng on the first night, Alkaline on the second night, and Maxi Priest on the closing night, on the same bill as the festival's bouyon acts.
