Bouyon drum kit: building the bouyon drum sound

Published September 19, 2026

A bouyon drum kit is the collection of sounds that lets a producer recreate the pressure of Dominican carnival inside their software: sampled traditional percussion, fast drums, bass, keyboards and crowd vocals. This page is about the kit itself: what it holds, where its sounds come from, where to find them and how to organize them. The method for turning it into a beat is on the how to make a bouyon beat page.

The essential sounds: two families in one kit

The kit starts with the percussion of Dominica: the lapo kabwit, the bele drum, the chakchak, the syak or gwaj and the strikes of chanté mas. Then come the modern elements: fast kick and snare, tight hats, electronic percussion, a synth bass and the keyboard sounds that carry the main loop. This is the meeting our bouyon instruments page describes: the island's traditional instruments, inherited from jing ping and the Creole dances, plugged into the machines of the 1980s, keyboards, synthesizers and drum machines.

So the words drum kit are a little narrow: bouyon is music where the keyboard leads, as the nickname of Cornell “Fingers” Phillip, the founding keyboardist of WCK, reminds us. A real bouyon kit keeps together what strikes, what rumbles and what sings.

Where the sounds come from: the Goodwill translation

In the Phillip family home in Goodwill, the boys played along with the traditional bands that their brother Daryl, a cultural officer, brought in to record. Cornell Phillip, who lived that scene, told it to Billboard: the boumboum, a hollowed wooden bwa kan, became the bass guitar; the syak and tambal, which are percussive instruments, became the drum machine; and the accordion turned into the keyboard.

That sentence is the best possible filing plan for a bouyon kit. Every family of modern sounds has an ancestor: the bass descends from the boumboum, the drum patterns from the syak and tambal, the keyboard loop from the jing ping accordion. When WCK formed in Roseau in 1988, the band fused cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines. The software producer repeats the same move today.

The traditional percussion, one by one

The lapo kabwit is the goat-skin drum of chanté mas and Dominica carnival; at Mas Domnik, whole bands play in lapo kabwit. In the jing ping line-up, these drums give the beat. It is the first slot in a bouyon kit.

The bele drum sits at the heart of the dance of the same name, the bèlè. The chakchak, the Creole maracas, and the syak or gwaj, the scraper, bring the grain: they are texture sounds more than hits, and the syak is precisely one of the instruments Cornell Phillip names as having become the drum machine. The strikes of chanté mas, finally, recall the carnival street, where bouyon is played in front of the crowd.

Three jing ping sounds complete the list without being percussion: the accordion, which leads the dance, the boom-boom, which marks the bass, and the bamboo flute, which brings the high melody that runs through the party. The keyboard and the synth bass extend them, hence their place in the kit as samples or references. See the jing ping page.

The machines: fast drums, bass and keyboard

The modern part of the kit takes up what WCK set in place: keyboards up front, synthetic rhythmic patterns, electronic basses. The synth bass is heavy and simple. The bouyon of 2026 is born on software, with percussion loops inspired by lapo kabwit, sampled keyboards and basses drawn for phones as well as big systems, as our modern bouyon page describes.

Tempo guides the choice of these sounds. Bouyon is a fast genre: the examples documented on this site sit around 160 BPM, like MAUVAIS PLAN by HYPNO. At that speed, a track needs a clear loop, readable percussion and breathing points in the right places. A bouyon kit therefore favors clean hits over long sounds that overlap. The details are on the bouyon BPM page.

Crowd vocals: the audience inside the kit

A bouyon kit also has a folder that generic kits forget: the voices. A bouyon track is recognized by a voice, in Creole or in English, that talks to the crowd more than it sings, and its beats recreate the crowd with background vocals, shouts and responses. Those calls and responses, inherited from old school singers who worked the crowd like in chanté mas, are filed next to the percussion.

Language matters: the mix of English and Dominican Creole is part of the genre's identity, and a shout in the right language places a beat as surely as a lapo kabwit. See the bouyon type beat page for how these voices are used in an instrumental.

Sample the tradition, do not replace it

The best bouyon producers treat traditional sounds with respect: they are what give a track its Dominican identity. A generic drum kit makes a fast beat; a kit fed on jing ping and lapo kabwit makes bouyon. Speed alone is not enough: without the percussion and the spirit of Dominica, it is just a fast beat.

Bouyon gwada pushes drum machines, bass and vocals in Guadeloupean Creole harder. A kit can lean toward the machines in the same way, but the sounds of Dominica remain the reference that tells where the genre comes from.

Cornell Phillip put the stakes to Billboard, speaking about the outside investment now taking an interest in bouyon: there is such a thing as fusion, but you have to be careful or you might lose the original form, and keep bouyon organic and special to Dominica. A kit that keeps jing ping and lapo kabwit up front answers that concern.

Riddims, instrumentals and Wrecketeng: the kit's sources

The riddim is the instrumental that several artists record their vocals over; in bouyon, it brings together the loop, the bass and the percussion. The bouyon instrumental is that riddim without the voice, in its pure form. For anyone building a kit, that is the best listening library. Riddims often carry a name, like Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim by Denmath, both released in 2022.

Many producers also build their kit by sampling recordings of jing ping and carnival bands. That move has a Dominican history: Wrecketeng, a style built on sampled melodies and rap vocals, which today's producers cite as a root of modern bouyon and RnBouyon. Smokiller, a Dominican-born producer and the founder of Bouyon Shattaz, works explicitly in that style: his remixes take familiar melodies and voices and re-dress them at bouyon tempo. Sampling is not a shortcut foreign to the genre, then: it is one of its grammars.

Listening to a riddim does not give you the right to chop it up, though. A riddim belongs to its producer: in the agreement reached in October 2026 between Bouyon Shattaz and Denmath's team, pool members may record freely on a beat, but any public release needs the producer's prior written OK. For a kit, that means: study other people's instrumentals, sample only what you have the right to sample, and note the source of every file.

How the site's producers circulate their sounds

Our files do not document a commercial bouyon drum kit released by the producers this site follows. What they do document is how those producers circulate their sounds.

Smokiller offers free downloads on smokiller.com: the Coi Leray Nothing Into Something remix in WAV, the Shenseea Vulnerability Bouyon Shattaz remix in WAV and the Ayra Starr Heaven Baby remix in M4A. His page makes the point: WAV is the format of DJs and radios, and Bouyon Shattaz rotation packs go out to Caribbean stations. For a kit, the lesson is the same: clean files, in a working format.

With Denmath, sharing runs through a Google Drive folder of beats, led by his 2026 productions and reserved for the private Bouyon Shattaz pool: downloads are logged and emailed to all parties, and his team registers his beats on Musicstart. HYPNO puts his tempo up front in his reel, and Bouyon Shattaz chose bouyon drum kit as the primary keyword of that reel's caption, because the producers and beatmakers looking for sounds, kits and tempo references read those captions. The full overview is on the bouyon producers page.

Organizing and naming your kit

The filing system most faithful to the genre follows the Goodwill translation. A tradition folder for the lapo kabwit, the bele drum, the chakchak, the syak or gwaj and the chanté mas strikes. A drums folder for the kicks, snares, hats and electronic percussion, heirs of the syak and tambal. A bass folder, heir of the boumboum. A keyboard folder, heir of the accordion, with the bamboo flute references. A voices folder for the shouts, the responses and the Creole. This plan checks, beat after beat, that neither of the two families has disappeared.

Naming matters as much as filing. Writing lapo kabwit rather than perc 3 keeps the sound's origin attached to the file when it travels. Instrument and style names are spelled correctly: Wrecketeng is the official spelling, given by DJ Cut, the founding member and pioneer of the style, and reketeng is a widespread internet misspelling. For a loop, the tempo in the file name avoids surprises; for a sample, so do the source and the credit.

The site's producers show the way in their own public titles: MB On The Track publishes his instrumentals with the genre, the year and his signature, like “Cigarettes” Bouyon Instrumental 2026 (Prod By MB On The Track), and Smokiller puts the Bouyon Shattaz Remix tag on his house remixes. The producers and passers chapter sums it up: clean credits, written agreements, well-named files and identified rights holders decide who will still be able to record tomorrow.

From kit to beat, and to listening

A kit is only a starting point. Programming the rhythm grid, writing the keyboard loop, placing the bass and testing it on the dance floor: the method is laid out on the how to make a bouyon beat page, and the way shared riddims work on the bouyon riddim page.

The best ear training is still listening. The Bouyon Road Mix by Bouyon Shattaz brings together the riddims of the moment, bouyon soca and carnival classics, from the WCK era to the anthems of the 1990s and 2000s. Listen to it while hunting for the sounds of this kit: the lapo kabwit under the drums, the accordion turned keyboard, the boumboum turned bass. WCK's milestones, Culture Shock, Dance Floor, Conch Shell and Balance Batty, are the best place to start hearing the keyboard lead.
