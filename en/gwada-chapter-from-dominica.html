Gwada from Dominica: bouyon’s second chapter

Published September 24, 2026

This chapter tells Guadeloupe from the Dominica side. Bouyon crossed the sea through the shared Creole language and became jump up in the French Antilles, then bouyon gwada, the hardcore branch of the genre: drum machines, heavy bass, Creole vocals, club ready. The Dominican position argued here is clear and generous at once: Gwada made bouyon bigger, and the origin credit travels with it, everywhere it goes.

The crossing through the Creole language

Bouyon needed no translation to reach Guadeloupe. Dominican Creole and the Creoles of the French Antilles understand each other, and that shared language served as the genre’s passport. In the French Antilles, the sound took the name jump up, before the Guadeloupean branch asserted itself as bouyon gwada, with its own codes and its own audience.

Bouyon gwada is described in our coverage as the hardcore branch of the genre: drum machines up front, heavy bass, Creole vocals, a format ready for the clubs. It is not a copy of the Dominican sound; it is a version that owns the pressure of the club. Dominica recognises its invention there, transformed by a sister island that shares its language and its taste for the party.

The flag Gwada calls its own

One visual detail deserves to be set down here, because Bouyon Shattaz uses it in its designs. The Gwada flag the network uses is the local flag Gwada people call theirs: green bands at the top and bottom, a red middle band, a yellow star. It is neither an improvised symbol nor another place’s flag. It is the sign the Gwada scene itself recognises.

Respecting that flag applies the same rule to the image as to the sound: every scene has its signs, and naming them correctly is part of the credit. A design that celebrates bouyon gwada with the flag Gwada claims says, without a word, that the culture of the other shore is taken seriously. Details of that kind build trust between scenes, far more lastingly than a big speech.

Today’s Gwada scene

In our coverage, today’s Gwada scene has several faces. Miimii KDS, with her viral title “Sé Miimii” and the SNEP gold record for “Pow Pow / Allo Doudou”. TIITII NBA and the New Bouyon Age wave, who himself says, in substance, that bouyon is Dominica’s and Guadeloupe is the second chapter. Ricky Bishop and his New Bouyon from Bordeaux. The Dcamp and Lewismelo duo, on the French-Creole side.

The line attributed to TIITII NBA matters a great deal to this chapter, because it comes from inside the Gwada scene. When a Guadeloupean artist of the new wave recognises Dominica as chapter one, the origin debate stops being a quarrel between islands. It becomes a shared story: a Dominican first chapter, a Gwada second chapter, and a history still being written by several hands, in the right order.

Traffic in both directions

The relationship no longer runs one way. The Guadeloupean label More Sewo, created in 2007 by Arturo Bellot, signed the Dominican artist BONNA: a Gwada label is therefore investing in a talent from the island of origin. And in October 2026, bouyonews, TIITII NBA’s page, agreed to publish French versions of Bouyon Shattaz news as collaborations, as co-author, under clear conditions accepted on both sides.

Those two facts sketch the model this site defends: a Creole network where scenes strengthen each other instead of plundering each other. Dominica brings the origin, the artists and the founding repertoire. Gwada brings amplification, labels, media and a club audience. Everyone gains, as long as the names, the credits and the order of the chapters stay visible. That is the whole Bouyon Konet thesis Bouyon Shattaz is building around bouyon.

Language matters a great deal in this story. Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix is not a studio accident. It tells of an island that talks to its neighbours, that understands itself from one shore to another, that can move from one sentence to the next without losing the thread. For a French listener discovering the genre, that shared Creole is a doorway: the French Antilles hear it with immediate familiarity, while discovering an island that is English speaking and Creole speaking at the same time. So take the lyrics seriously. Even when the tone is light, even when the joke leads, the way of saying things is part of the music. A call, a nickname, an answer from the crowd: all of it builds the identity of the track. That is one reason this site names artists by their name and tracks by their exact title. Accuracy is a form of respect, in language as in credits.

Credit, dance and language

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because bouyon gwada seen from Dominica does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

Reading, linking, passing it on

To listen to bouyon gwada seen from Dominica with good ears, you have to slow down a little. Bouyon moves fast. It is built for the party, for the body, for the crowd answering back right away. But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. A genre is never just its best known title. It lives in the transitions, in the choruses sung back together, in the way a producer decides to let a loop breathe or to fire it up again. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Protecting what makes the party

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of bouyon gwada seen from Dominica. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.
