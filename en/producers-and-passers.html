Producers and passeurs: the hands behind the bouyon sound

Published October 2, 2026

Bouyon is often told through its singers. This chapter chooses the other end of the chain: the producers who make the sound, and the passeurs, the connectors who carry it, protect it and link it up. Smokiller and his remix method, Denmath and his riddims, HYPNO and his speed, Savion and the snippet format, MB On The Track and his custom beats, Dboss and his “Come Here” riddim. Behind them, an agreement signed in October 2026 shows how producer protection can become a model.

The producers: six signatures, six methods

Smokiller opens the list with his method: remixing in the wrecketeng style, taking familiar melodies and voices and re-dressing them at bouyon tempo, within Bouyon Shattaz. Denmath, from Saint-Martin and based between Nantes and Paris, signed the Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim in 2022, then Koko Bwa’s “Tarif” in 2025. Two different profiles: the Dominican-born remixer and the riddim producer based in France.

HYPNO made his mark with “MAUVAIS PLAN” at 160 BPM, a speed that states his aesthetic. Savion, reachable as @prod.bysavion, works the snippet format, those short excerpts that test a sound before its release. MB On The Track offers custom beats by DM and delivered “Rock Bouyon”. Dboss signs the “Come Here” riddim for the Skrazy, Dboss and Ras B trio. Six signatures, and as many ways of making bouyon.

The Denmath agreement: a protection model

In October 2026, Bouyon Shattaz and Denmath’s team concluded an agreement that deserves explaining here. Denmath’s beats are hosted for Smokiller’s pool of Dominican artists only. Downloads are logged and emailed to all parties. Recording over a beat is free, but releasing a title requires the producer’s prior written OK.

That mechanism protects the producer without blocking creation. The artist can try, record, show. The release, though, goes through a clear validation. Ambre Lazaro, Denmath’s manager, green-lit BONNA as the first pairing of the system. The Denmath page completes this chapter. That is what a modern passeur looks like: not just a contact, a written framework that secures everyone.

The passeurs: media, managers and conversations

The passeur does not produce the sound; they produce the conditions of the sound. Bouyon Shattaz plays that role as media, but also as a distribution and rights desk for Dominican artists. Jerlani Robinson, Signal Band’s manager, talks contracts and intellectual property on Riddim Talk, Bouyon Pulse’s show. When a manager discusses IP in public, the whole scene levels up.

Ambre Lazaro illustrates the other side of the role: a manager who protects her producer, validates a pairing with BONNA and puts a framework around the beats before the first title even exists. Those conversations matter as much as the releases. They decide who will be credited, who will be paid, and who can reuse a riddim tomorrow. Bouyon has always had passeurs, from radios to fete organisers. The novelty is that they now talk about rights.

The chapter’s thesis: a producer story

This chapter’s argument is simple: bouyon’s next decade will be a producer and rights story as much as a singer story. Voices make the choruses audiences remember, but producers decide the tempo, the texture, the speed and the sonic signature the whole world recognises in three seconds.

Protecting those producers, documenting their riddims, writing their names correctly: that is the daily work of a media outlet like Bouyon Shattaz and of a site like this one. Every producer page, every full credit, every written agreement adds a brick. The bouyon goes global chapter shows what happens when the sound travels. This one shows the hands that make it, and those that make sure the journey benefits its makers.

Listening with the story in mind

This chapter is also an invitation to move around the site. Also read Smokiller, Denmath, the wrecketeng chapter and the bouyon playlist. Every page answers another one: an artist page lights up a culture page, a style page lights up a festival page, a playlist turns reading into listening. You do not have to read everything in order. Come in through the name that intrigues you, the rhythm that hooked you, the festival everyone is talking about. Then come back here and read this chapter again with those sounds in your head. The second reading will be different, because the names will have become voices. That is exactly the journey Bouyon Shattaz wants for bouyon: first curiosity, then listening, then loyalty. And at every step, the same reflex: check who sings, who produces, who wrote, and where the sound comes from. An audience that asks those questions becomes an audience that protects the music it loves.

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because bouyon’s producers and passeurs does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

Credit, dance and language

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

To listen to bouyon’s producers and passeurs with good ears, you have to slow down a little. Bouyon moves fast. It is built for the party, for the body, for the crowd answering back right away. But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. A genre is never just its best known title. It lives in the transitions, in the choruses sung back together, in the way a producer decides to let a loop breathe or to fire it up again. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

Reading, linking, passing it on

There is also a very concrete side to bouyon’s producers and passeurs: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Protecting what makes the party

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of bouyon’s producers and passeurs. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the producers featured in this chapter?

Six producers, six methods: Smokiller and the Wrecketeng-style remix, Denmath and his riddims, HYPNO and his speed, Savion and the snippet format, MB On The Track and his custom beats, Dboss and his “Come Here” riddim. Together they show that bouyon is built in several ways. See also bouyon producers.

What does the October 2026 Denmath agreement provide?

Denmath's beats are hosted for Smokiller's pool of Dominican artists only, downloads are logged and emailed to all parties, recording is free, and any release requires the producer's prior written consent. The mechanism protects the producer without blocking creation: the artist can try, record and show, while the release goes through a clear approval.

What is a passeur in bouyon?

A passeur is a connector: a media outlet, manager or rights desk that carries the sound between islands and protects its creators. A passeur does not produce the sound, but produces the conditions for it. Bouyon Shattaz plays that role, as do Ambre Lazaro for Denmath and Jerlani Robinson for Signal Band. Bouyon has always had passeurs, from radio to party promoters.

Who is Denmath?

Denmath, real name Mathurin DENMATH Denis, is a producer, sound engineer and artistic director from Saint Martin, of Haitian heritage, based between Nantes and Paris. He made the bouyon riddims Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim in 2022, then “Tarif” by Koko Bwa in 2025, with Boutcha Bwa. See the Denmath page.

Who is Ambre Lazaro?

Ambre Lazaro is producer Denmath's manager. She approved the October 2026 agreement with Bouyon Shattaz and gave the green light for BONNA, an artist from La Plaine, Dominica, to be the system's first pairing. She illustrates the modern passeur: a manager who puts a written framework around the beats before the first track even exists.

What tempo is HYPNO's MAUVAIS PLAN?

HYPNO's MAUVAIS PLAN is built at 160 BPM, an openly fast tempo that states his aesthetic. The track was showcased in his producer reel released in October 2026, with a loop designed for social video and dance. HYPNO, also written HYPNO!, posts on Instagram as @hypnoticbeatz. See HYPNO.

What is a snippet in bouyon?

A snippet is a short excerpt of an unreleased track, posted to test a sound before release. The producer reads the reactions in the comments, then decides to release the track, rework it or keep it in the workshop. Savion, active on Instagram as @prod.bysavion, works in this format, which has become the method of the new generation of bouyon producers.

Who produced the Come Here riddim?

The “Come Here” riddim is by Dboss (Riddimz), with production by Bad Producer Ent and recording, mixing and mastering by Yw3 Ent (DiYoute). The track is written and performed by Ras B, Skrazy and Dboss, according to the official YouTube visualizer description. Ras B presents himself as a rap and bouyon artist from Dominica. See Ras B.

What is Rock Bouyon?

Rock Bouyon is the phrase MB On The Track displayed on his reel of October 16, 2026, in which he dances at night in a bus shelter to his own bouyon instrumental. It is his own framing, not a verified movement. MB On The Track presents himself as a Diamond Producer and offers custom beats by direct message.

How does Smokiller make his bouyon remixes?

Smokiller works in the Wrecketeng style: he takes familiar melodies and voices and re-dresses them at bouyon tempo, within Bouyon Shattaz. Born in Dominica, he flips afrobeats, pop, rap and R&B tracks this way. His method extends a Dominican school of sampling born long before social media. See Wrecketeng.

Who is Jerlani Robinson?

Jerlani Robinson is the manager of Signal Band, the Dominican band behind two of Shelly's trending tracks. In the first episode of Riddim Talk, the Bouyon Pulse show, he spoke publicly about contracts, royalties and protecting artists' intellectual property. When a manager discusses IP in public, the whole scene moves up a level.

Is Bouyon Shattaz a media outlet or a label?

Bouyon Shattaz is first a media outlet, but it also acts as a distribution and rights desk for Dominican artists. That is the framework in which the agreement with Denmath's team was concluded in October 2026. The network documents producers, writes full credits and builds written agreements that protect the makers of the sound.

Why do producers matter so much in bouyon?

Because they decide the tempo, the texture, the speed and the sonic signature the world recognises in three seconds. Voices make the hooks, but this chapter's thesis is that the next decade of bouyon will be a story of producers and rights as much as a story of singers.
