Wrecketeng: the sampled root of modern bouyon

Published September 5, 2026

Wrecketeng is the Dominican style that the quick history of the genre most often forgets. Built on sampled melodies and rap vocals, it stands, in Bouyon Shattaz’s research record, among the roots of modern bouyon and of RnBouyon. Today’s producers cite it as a source. And one artist on this site works explicitly in it: Smokiller, whose remix method descends directly from that school of sampling and of voices re-dressed over a bouyon rhythm.

A style built on sampling and rap

Wrecketeng rests on a simple, modern idea: take existing melodies, sample them, and lay rap vocals over them. In the Dominican laboratory that followed WCK’s 1988 creation of bouyon, that approach belonged to one burst of experiments, alongside bouyon soca and bouyon-muffin. This site’s styles family also lists Wrecketeng among the forms derived from the bouyon explosion.

Presenting wrecketeng that way means refusing to treat it as a curiosity. It is a producers’ style, designed in the studio, and it shifts the genre’s centre of gravity: the melody can come from elsewhere, but the treatment, the tempo and the voice make it Dominican. That logic of transformation is exactly what modern bouyon would generalise, through to the RnBouyon that today’s producers cite as an heir of this root.

Smokiller, the explicit heir

Smokiller works explicitly in the wrecketeng style. His remix method takes familiar melodies and voices and re-dresses them at bouyon tempo. Seen from that angle, his practice is not a platform trick: it is the continuation of an older Dominican method, that of the openly used sample and the voice re-set over a party rhythm.

The Smokiller page details his bouyon remixes, and this chapter gives you the key to hearing them differently. When a well known voice lands on a bouyon rhythm, the listener hears two stories at once: the original title and the Dominican treatment. Wrecketeng names that pleasure, and it recalls that remixing, in bouyon culture, has local roots long before social platforms.

The lineage does not stop at Smokiller. For Bouyon Shattaz, the 2020s labels claiming newness, New Bouyon and RnBouyon alike, are the heirs of Wrecketeng: they reuse its method, taking existing songs, hip hop, trap or R&B, stripping them down and rebuilding them on the bouyon machinery, sampled melodies, programmed percussion and electronic bass. The new labels change the vocabulary; the grammar was born in Roseau.

A word on spelling, because it matters: the official spelling is 'Wrecketeng', and it comes from DJ Cut himself, the founding member who pioneered the style. The 'reketeng' spelling spread across the internet and picked up by publications up to Billboard is a misspelling that became common. This site writes Wrecketeng everywhere, including for the historical practice, and keeps 'reketeng' only to flag that widespread error.

A revival announced in 2025

Wrecketeng is not only a studio memory. In December 2025, a collaborative wrecketeng album was announced, as reported by Dominica News Online. The style is therefore being actively revived, not just remembered. That nuance matters for understanding the Dominican scene: its older forms can return to the front when a new generation of producers decides to rework them.

That return also backs the producers who cited the style as a root. A root is not a museum piece: it is what keeps feeding the plant. Modern bouyon and RnBouyon draw on this stock of samples and rap delivery, and the announced collaborative album shows that the source itself can still produce new works, collective by definition.

Why Bouyon Shattaz puts it forward

On October 18, 2026, Bouyon Shattaz gave wrecketeng its own section in its big explainer post titled “Bouyon Music”. The reason is editorial: the bouyon story circulating on the French market usually skips this Dominican root. It keeps the recent hits, the dances and the viral titles, but moves quickly past the laboratory that made those hits possible.

Putting wrecketeng back into the story corrects an omission. This chapter belongs to the same effort as the origins of bouyon: documenting the Dominican forms one by one, under their exact names. A French audience discovering wrecketeng better understands why Dominica claims paternity of the whole genre. It is not an honorary title; it is a genealogy of styles born on the same island.

Listening with the story in mind

And at every step, the same reflex: check who sings, who produces, who wrote, and where the sound comes from. An audience that asks those questions becomes an audience that protects the music it loves.

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.

Credit, dance and language

Language matters a great deal in this story. Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix is not a studio accident. It tells of an island that talks to its neighbours, that understands itself from one shore to another, that can move from one sentence to the next without losing the thread. For a French listener discovering the genre, that shared Creole is a doorway: the French Antilles hear it with immediate familiarity, while discovering an island that is English speaking and Creole speaking at the same time. So take the lyrics seriously. Even when the tone is light, even when the joke leads, the way of saying things is part of the music. A call, a nickname, an answer from the crowd: all of it builds the identity of the track. That is one reason this site names artists by their name and tracks by their exact title. Accuracy is a form of respect, in language as in credits.

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because wrecketeng does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

Reading, linking, passing it on

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

There is also a very concrete side to wrecketeng: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

Protecting what makes the party

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of wrecketeng. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

Frequently asked questions

What is wrecketeng?

Wrecketeng is a Dominican style derived from bouyon, built on sampled melodies and rap vocals. It remixed existing dancehall and hip hop recordings over bouyon instrumentals, heavily transforming the original material. Today's producers cite it as a root of modern bouyon and of RnBouyon.

Reketeng or Wrecketeng: which spelling is right?

The official spelling is Wrecketeng. According to Sean “Smokiller” Francis, founder of Bouyon Shattaz and a participant in the Nursery Krew era, it comes from DJ Cut himself, the founding member and pioneer of the style. The form “reketeng”, widespread online and repeated as far as Billboard, is a misspelling that became common: this site writes it only to flag it.

Who created Wrecketeng?

DJ Cut, a Dominican DJ and producer tied to Bucktown Sounds, is recognised among the style's early pioneers, and Smokiller presents him as its founding member. His track “Party”, with Joanne on vocals, is described as the bridge between bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng. The style then became a collective testing ground. See DJ Cut.

When and where was Wrecketeng born?

Wrecketeng was born in Dominica, in the laboratory that followed WCK's creation of bouyon in 1988, in the wake of 1990s bouyon-muffin. Its grammar has been practised in Roseau since the late 1990s, and the Nursery Krew Inc. developed it in the early 2000s through original recordings. See the origins of bouyon.

Which groups popularised Wrecketeng?

The French reference page on bouyon lists the Bushtown Clan, the Klockerz Krew and the Nursery Krew Inc. among the style's first groups and crews. The Bushtown Clan established it through DJ performances, remixes and recordings, and the Nursery Krew carried it on with Burn Brain Jolly, Charlie Black, Watty and Smokiller. See Nursery Krew Inc..

What is the difference between Wrecketeng and bouyon-muffin?

Bouyon-muffin is first a way of singing: from the 1990s, Dominican singers put raggamuffin-style vocals on bouyon. Wrecketeng is a way of producing: the DJ samples hip hop and dancehall records and rebuilds them on bouyon instrumentals. One comes from the stage, the other from the DJ booth. See bouyon-muffin.

How does a wrecketeng remix work?

The method is to take an existing recording, strip it down and rebuild it on the bouyon machinery: sampled melodies, programmed percussion and electronic bass, with a vocal set back down at party tempo. Like dub, it is mostly an art of the instrumental remix. The melody may come from elsewhere, but the treatment, the tempo and the voice make it Dominican.

Is Wrecketeng the ancestor of New Bouyon and RnBouyon?

For Bouyon Shattaz, yes: the 2020s labels, New Bouyon and RnBouyon alike, reuse Wrecketeng's method, taking hip hop, trap or R&B songs and rebuilding them on the bouyon machinery. Wikipedia, in English and in French, reads it the same way. The new labels change the vocabulary; the grammar was born in Roseau. See RnBouyon.

Which artist works in the wrecketeng style?

Smokiller works explicitly in this style: his remix method takes familiar melodies and voices and re-dresses them at bouyon tempo. A Dominican-born producer, he took part in the Nursery Krew era, and his practice continues an older Dominican method, the openly sampled track. See Smokiller's page.

Does wrecketeng still exist?

Yes. On December 3, 2025, Dominica News Online announced a collaborative album produced by Smokiller, devoted to the origins and development of Wrecketeng, with contributors including DJ Cut, Klass Money, Charlie Black, Keith Goddard, O Banga and Mopo Neck. The project also credits the influence of Burn Brain Jolly and the Nursery Krew Inc.: the style is being actively revived.

Where can I listen to wrecketeng?

Start with Smokiller's bouyon remixes, since he works explicitly in this style, then the Dominican classics documented by Bouyon Shattaz in its Bouyon Road Mix, on Spotify and YouTube. When a familiar voice lands on a bouyon rhythm, you hear two stories at once: the original track and the Dominican treatment. Open the bouyon playlist.
