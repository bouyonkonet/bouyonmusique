Cadence-lypso roots: what bouyon owes to cadence

Published September 3, 2026

Before bouyon, there was cadence-lypso. To understand WCK’s 1988 fusion, you have to hear what came before it: traditional jing ping on one side, and on the other the Dominican bands of the 1970s who carried cadence-lypso across the Caribbean. Exile One and the Grammacks carried this music, its aggressive rhythm guitars and its Creole lyrics telling island life. Bouyon did not replace that inheritance: it plugged it into machines.

Jing ping, the traditional base

Jing ping is Dominica’s traditional dance music: accordion in front, lapo kabwit drums, boom-boom and bamboo flute. It is a group sound, made for fetes and collective dances, long before modern studios. Every history of Dominican music starts from that base, because it sets the island’s relationship to dance: music exists to bring people together.

When WCK fused cadence-lypso and jing ping in 1988, the move therefore had two parents. Cadence brought the modern band form and the Creole narrative; jing ping brought the memory of the dances. Electronic keyboards and drum machines replaced part of the orchestra, but they did not replace the intention: making Dominica dance to its own rhythms, at the speed of its time.

Exile One, the Grammacks and the 1970s

In the 1970s, Dominican bands such as Exile One and the Grammacks carried cadence-lypso across the Caribbean. The formula was made for dancing: a cadence that pushes you to move, an aggressive rhythm guitar cutting the time, and Creole lyrics telling the life of the island. Cadence-lypso was party music and sung social chronicle at the same time.

That is the inheritance bouyon received. From cadence, it kept the guitar heritage, the Creole storytelling and the party function. Understanding this avoids a frequent mistake: believing bouyon appeared from nowhere with the machines. The machines are the novelty of 1988, not the starting point. The starting point is a tradition of Dominican bands that already knew how to make people dance and tell stories at once.

Midnight Groovers, the living bridge

The Midnight Groovers of Grand Bay are the living bridge between those two worlds. Formed in the late 1960s and led by Phillip “Chubby” Mark, the band has released more than 39 albums and remains an active cadence-lypso legacy act. Above all, it is still on the bill of the World Creole Music Festival in 2026: the root and the present share the same stage, in Roseau.

Their page, Midnight Groovers, tells that journey in detail. For a new listener, hearing them before modern bouyon changes the ear: you hear what the cadence guitar handed down to the genre, and why the elders are not decoration in this story. They are still booked, still heard, still able to hold a festival night.

Grand Bay, one village, two eras

Grand Bay concentrates that continuity in an almost novel-like way. It is the village of the Midnight Groovers, the cadence-lypso legends. It is also the village of Asa Bantan, the modern bouyon figure presented on his Asa Bantan page. The roots and the modern boss therefore share one village, one piece of land in the south of Dominica.

That geographic coincidence says something deep about Dominica: it is a small island where musical generations physically cross paths. The young bouyon artist grows up where cadence was played for decades. Bouyon did not need to import its roots; they were at the end of the street. That is also why the genre’s origin debate is not abstract for Dominicans: it plays out in precise places, with precise names.

There is also a very concrete side to the cadence-lypso roots of bouyon: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

Credit, dance and language

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of the cadence-lypso roots of bouyon. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

Reading, linking, passing it on

Language matters a great deal in this story. Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix is not a studio accident. It tells of an island that talks to its neighbours, that understands itself from one shore to another, that can move from one sentence to the next without losing the thread. For a French listener discovering the genre, that shared Creole is a doorway: the French Antilles hear it with immediate familiarity, while discovering an island that is English speaking and Creole speaking at the same time. So take the lyrics seriously. Even when the tone is light, even when the joke leads, the way of saying things is part of the music. A call, a nickname, an answer from the crowd: all of it builds the identity of the track. That is one reason this site names artists by their name and tracks by their exact title. Accuracy is a form of respect, in language as in credits.

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because the cadence-lypso roots of bouyon does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

Protecting what makes the party

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

To listen to the cadence-lypso roots of bouyon with good ears, you have to slow down a little. Bouyon moves fast. It is built for the party, for the body, for the crowd answering back right away. But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. A genre is never just its best known title. It lives in the transitions, in the choruses sung back together, in the way a producer decides to let a loop breathe or to fire it up again. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

Frequently asked questions

What is cadence-lypso?

Cadence-lypso is the Dominican music that bands such as Exile One and the Grammacks carried across the Caribbean in the 1970s. Its formula: a dancing cadence, aggressive rhythm guitar and Creole lyrics about island life. It is both party music and a sung social chronicle, and one of the two parents of bouyon.

What did bouyon keep from cadence-lypso?

Bouyon kept cadence-lypso's guitar heritage, its Creole storytelling and its party function. In 1988, WCK fused that heritage with jing ping, replacing part of the orchestra with electronic keyboards and drum machines. The machines changed the sound, not the intention: to make Dominica dance to its own rhythms.

Who are the Midnight Groovers?

The Midnight Groovers are a cadence-lypso band from Grand Bay, Dominica, formed in the late 1960s and led by Phillip “Chubby” Mark. The band has more than 39 albums and remains the living bridge between the cadence root and today's bouyon. Their full story is on the Midnight Groovers page.

Does bouyon come from cadence-lypso or jing ping?

Bouyon comes from both. When WCK created it in Roseau in 1988, the band brought together cadence-lypso, which supplied the modern band format and Creole storytelling, and jing ping, which supplied the memory of traditional dances. Keyboards and drum machines plugged these two Dominican heritages into the speed of the era.

Who are Exile One and the Grammacks?

Exile One and the Grammacks are Dominican bands that carried cadence-lypso across the Caribbean in the 1970s. Their sound, aggressive rhythm guitar and Creole lyrics about island life, made cadence-lypso the first Dominican style to become popular in the region. That is the heritage bouyon received before plugging it into machines.

What is the link between Exile One and the word jump up?

In the French Antilles, the term jump up already described very rhythmic carnival music, notably after Exile One's “L'hivernage” in 1987, a track nicknamed The Yo. When Dominican bouyon crossed the sea, audiences in Guadeloupe and Martinique filed it in that family. A band of the cadence generation thus prepared bouyon's Antillean name. See jump up.

Why does Grand Bay matter in bouyon history?

Grand Bay, in the south of Dominica, is both the village of the Midnight Groovers, the cadence-lypso legend, and the village of Asa Bantan, widely called the King of Bouyon. The root and the modern figure of the genre therefore share the same ground. Young bouyon artists grow up there where cadence was played for decades.

Do the Midnight Groovers still perform in 2026?

Yes. The Midnight Groovers are on the bill of the 2026 World Creole Music Festival, on Night 1, Friday October 23, 2026, at Windsor Park in Roseau, according to the official Discover Dominica Authority communication and the published guides. The cadence-lypso root and today's bouyon share the same stage in the same week.

What is the difference between cadence-lypso and bouyon?

Cadence-lypso is 1970s band music, driven by rhythm guitar and Creole lyrics. Bouyon was born in 1988 when WCK fused that heritage with jing ping and replaced part of the orchestra with electronic keyboards and drum machines. The rhythm became more regular, more insistent and more cut out for carnival.

What did jing ping bring to bouyon?

Jing ping brought bouyon the memory of Dominica's dances. This traditional dance music combines accordion, lapo kabwit drums, the boom-boom and the bamboo flute, and it sets the island's relationship to dance: music is there to bring people together. WCK combined it with cadence-lypso in 1988. See jing ping.

Where does the name cadence-lypso come from?

The name tells the blend: cadence-lypso combines Haitian cadence, calypso and the Creole language. Dominican bands of the 1970s made it their own, danceable and sung in Creole, to the point of making it the first Dominican style to become popular across the Caribbean. Details on the cadence-lypso page.

Is bouyon just machine music?

No. Keyboards and drum machines are the novelty of 1988, not the starting point. Bouyon rests on a tradition of Dominican bands that already knew how to make people dance and tell stories at the same time, and on the jing ping of village parties. Believing it came from nowhere with the machines is the mistake this chapter corrects.

Where can you hear bouyon's cadence roots today?

On stage, at WCMF 2026, where the Midnight Groovers play Night 1 in Roseau.
