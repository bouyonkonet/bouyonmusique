Suppa: from the first bouyon stage in Paris to a brutal end in May 2013

Published October 20, 2026

Suppa is a voice of bouyon gwada, the hardcore Guadeloupe branch of the bouyon born in Dominica. The Brussels collective Rebel Up, which documented that scene at the very moment it was exploding, presents him under two forms of the same name: Suppa in the account of the first bouyon performance in Paris, in December 2012, alongside the Gaza Girls and Dj Joe, and General Suppa in the sentence that announces his end, stabbed to death in May 2013. Between the two, a single title is verified on its original video: “Never Stop Di Bouyon”, with Ti Cho and Ghetto Princess, published in the Vadore Concept circle. This profile tells what those pieces establish, what they say about the scene around him, and what the public record does not give: no full name, no date of birth, no catalogue. A short career, a clear trace, and a death that made Suppa a name the scene has not forgotten.

Who Suppa was: a voice of the Vadore Concept circle

Suppa appears in the sources in a short form and a long form. The video that carries his verified title is published under the name Suppa, with Ti Cho and Ghetto Princess. The Rebel Up article on bouyon hardcore speaks of the Paris performance of Suppa, then names the featured singer of its mixtape General Suppa as it announces his death. This profile treats those two forms as the same artist, on the basis of that single article which runs them together in the same account, and it flags that single basis instead of disguising it as a certainty documented by ten sources. No source consulted gives his full name, his date of birth or his home town, and none of those elements is therefore printed here.

His world, on the other hand, is named precisely by his own video. The description of “Never Stop Di Bouyon” points to the site vadoreconcept.hautetfort.com, the site of the Vadore Concept circle, and to Facebook pages for Suppa and for Ghetto Princess. Vadore Concept is the banner that structures the whole trace: a production and party circle of bouyon gwada in the early 2010s, whose published heir, Vadore Concept Groupe, was still releasing in 2023 a single by DJ Joe. Suppa is therefore not an isolated name fallen from a platform: he belongs to a stable, a scene and a geography, the Guadeloupe of bouyon gwada and its Paris diaspora.

No artist page for Suppa was confirmed on the streaming platforms consulted during this research, and the other artists named Supa or Suppa in dancehall and other scenes have no documented link with him: this profile merges nothing. What the record gives is a voice, a circle, a title, a Paris stage and a date of death. That is little for a biography, and it is exactly why this profile is built as a file of pieces, in the order the sources deliver them.

Hardcore bouyon gwada: Suppa sound according to Rebel Up

To understand Suppa, first hear the sound Rebel Up describes, because it is the only period, first hand description of his scene style. The collective calls hardcore the recent substyle of bouyon, whose Guadeloupe equivalent is bouyon gwada: raw lyrics, often sexually explicit or violent, in English, French or Creole, over heavy percussive riddims, with melodies simplified and stripped down. One successful riddim receives several versions, in the manner of dancehall. Rebel Up places its Bouyon Hardcore mixtape at 155 BPM, seventy minutes of fast Caribbean party beats: that is the sound setting in which Suppa voice worked.

The same article describes a scene in full expansion with blurred borders. This new bouyon from the French Antilles was gaining popularity all over the Caribbean, competing with the local dancehall scene for the attention of the audience, with blurred lines between the two scenes: dancehall singers did bouyon and vice versa, playing for the same kind of audience. The associated dance moves, a mix of booty shaking and dynamic adult positions, likened to American twerking, Ivorian mapouka and Brasilian popozuda shaking in baile funk, were part of the show. Suppa belongs to that precise moment: when the bouyon of Dominica, become gwada, spoke the language of the rawest parties of the French Antilles.

Rebel Up also notes, with a distance this profile keeps, the official pushback against that scene. A Guadeloupean article quoted by the collective, at first neutral on the origins, went on to reject bouyon as pornophonie and to call for a ban, with this comment: if a porn movie is forbidden for under 18s, bouyon gwada should be too. The censorship battle is part of Suppa context: clips by bouyon artists from those small islands ran between 5,000 and more than 50,000 views according to Rebel Up, a real public weight, and the question of whether this music should be policed was already agitating Guadeloupe. Our bouyon gwada page traces that branch as a whole.

Never Stop Di Bouyon: the verified title and its keyword cloud

Suppa verified title is a YouTube video: “Never Stop Di Bouyon”, by Suppa with Ti Cho and Ghetto Princess. The title is a programme in itself, never stop the bouyon, and the video is published in the Vadore Concept circle, whose site is linked in the description. It is the only recording carried in the schema of this page, for want of any other title verified title by title on an original source. No album, no other single and no platform page under the name Suppa in this scene was found during this research.

The video carries a second piece, which has become well known in the files of this site: its keyword list. In the disorder proper to those lists written for search engines, it reads: bouyon, Vadore, Concept, Gaza, Crew, Gwada, Girls, Dj Joe, Triple Kay, Asa, Bantan, Suppa, Ghetto, Princess, and other names of the scene of the moment. It is in that sequence that the words Gaza and Crew touch each other, and it is the only place the name Gaza Crew survived in the whole research of this site. Our Gaza Crew page publishes that trace for what it is worth: it documents the vocabulary of the scene around Suppa, between Vadore Concept and the Gaza Girls Crew, without establishing the existence of a distinct group.

Read as a photograph, that list says who counted around Suppa in the early 2010s: his label Vadore Concept, his stage neighbours the Gaza Girls, his circle DJ Dj Joe, and the Dominican references Triple Kay and Asa Bantan, an artist widely called the King of Bouyon by the public and the media of the scene, an attributed formulation and not an official title. A keyword cloud is neither a cover nor a credit roll, but for an artist whose catalogue has been lost, it keeps the map of his world, and it is in that exact form that this profile publishes it.

Paris, December 2012: the first bouyon stage in the capital

The major stage fact of the file is Parisian. According to Rebel Up, it took until December 2012 before the first Dominican and Guadeloupean MCs and DJs came to Paris, home to a large part of the Antillean diaspora in France. The collective describes the first and impressive bouyon performance in Paris by Suppa, the Gaza Girls, Dj Joe and others, and points to a 50 minute documentary, in French and Creole, devoted to that first. Suppa therefore belongs, according to the only period source that tells it, to the very first wave of bouyon artists to carry the genre onto a Paris stage.

The reach of that moment goes beyond his name. Paris is the capital of the diaspora of the French Antilles, and bouyon lived there until then in parties and videos rather than in performances by artists come from the islands. That the documentary exists, in French and Creole, shot on that first, shows the scene itself felt a threshold was being crossed. Our bouyon in Paris page follows the settling of the genre in the capital, and Suppa stands in it, chronologically, as one of the first documented stones.

What that date does to Suppa story must be measured. The Paris performance takes place in December 2012. His death is announced for May 2013. Between the first Paris stage of the genre and his end, there are about five months. The file does not allow telling what he did between the two, no concert date, no other title and no interview being published in the sources consulted. But the very compression of those two dates gives his path its shape: a voice of bouyon gwada reaches the visible summit of its scene, the Paris first, and vanishes almost at once.

May 2013: the death of General Suppa and a funeral in bouyon style

The end of the file is given by Rebel Up in the most direct terms. The collective writes that bouyon is also hardcore because of an associated context of violence, drugs, alcohol and weapons, linked to global poverty as lived in the banlieues and disadvantaged districts of the world, and that it can go pretty fast sometimes: the featured singer General Suppa had been stabbed to death in May 2013. The same article points to footage from Suppa funeral, celebrated in bouyon style. Those are the only two published elements on his death in the sources consulted: the manner, the month, the year, and a funeral filmed in the style of his music. No place, no suspect name, no investigation account is given by the source, and this profile invents none.

The source is single, and this profile says so as it does for every thin file on this site. Rebel Up is a collective of DJs and diggers of the global bass scene who followed bouyon gwada closely, mixtape and article in hand, at the very time of the events: its article is a period piece, written by witnesses of the scene, not a late reconstruction. But a single piece remains a single piece. If a Guadeloupe press article, an obituary or a Vadore Concept testimony one day documents that death differently, this profile will be corrected, completed and dated, like every page on this site.

The funeral in bouyon style, meanwhile, tells something the scene will recognise: the genre buried one of its own with its own music, at its own tempo, like a last party. For an artist of whom neither an album nor an official page remains, that closing image, reported by the collective that had featured him while he lived, is the documented closure of the file. Suppa did not have the time to become an elder of bouyon gwada: he remains one of its first voices, and one of its first losses.

What the record does not give, and how to hear his world

This profile ends with its limits, because they are part of the file. Not established by any source consulted: the full name of Suppa, his date and place of birth, his town in Guadeloupe, any album, any title beyond “Never Stop Di Bouyon”, the precise circumstances of his death beyond the month, the year and the manner reported by Rebel Up, and any official account whose exact URL could have been verified. The Facebook pages of Suppa are mentioned in the description of his video, but the addresses are truncated in the fetched text, and none is therefore carried in the schema of this page: a social link is printed here only when verified character by character.

What the record does give, on the other hand, is enough to place Suppa in the history of the genre. A named voice of hardcore bouyon gwada, the rawest sound Guadeloupe plugged into the bouyon of Dominica. A title that sums up the watchword of a whole scene, never stop the bouyon. A place in the first bouyon performance in Paris, in December 2012, alongside the Gaza Girls and Dj Joe. A violent death in May 2013, told by the collective that documented his scene, and a funeral in bouyon style. Each of those facts is sourced, dated and attributed, and together they make a portrait: brief, but real.

To hear Suppa world, the doors are on this site: the record “Never Stop Di Bouyon” itself, our bouyon playlist, and the pages bouyon gwada, bouyon in Paris, DJ Joe and Gaza Girls Crew, the names of his circle. Between those pieces stands Suppa, General Suppa for the collective that announced his death: a voice gone five months after its Paris first, and published here for exactly what the sources give of it.

Did Suppa really play in Paris?

Yes, according to Rebel Up, the only period source that tells it: in December 2012, Suppa, the Gaza Girls, Dj Joe and others gave the first bouyon performance in Paris, before the Antillean diaspora, and a 50 minute documentary in French and Creole was shot on that first. Our bouyon in Paris page retraces the context.

How did Suppa die?

According to Rebel Up, the featured singer General Suppa was stabbed to death in May 2013, about five months after the first bouyon stage in Paris, and the collective points to footage from his funeral celebrated in bouyon style. It is the only source consulted on his death: it gives the manner, the month and the year, with no place and no investigation account, and this profile invents nothing more.

What is the link between Suppa and Gaza Crew?

A keyword link, and nothing more established. The keyword list of the video “Never Stop Di Bouyon” places Gaza, Crew, Gwada and Girls side by side, between Vadore Concept, Dj Joe, Triple Kay and Asa Bantan. It is the only place the name Gaza Crew survived in the research of this site, and our Gaza Crew page explains why that trace can also be read as the name Gaza Girls Crew cut into pieces.
