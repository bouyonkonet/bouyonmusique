World Creole Music Festival 2026: Roseau’s WCMF

By Smokiller · Published October 14, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

The World Creole Music Festival, the WCMF, is Roseau’s great Creole gathering in Dominica: three nights at Windsor Park, every October at independence time. The 2026 edition runs from Friday October 23 to Sunday October 25, 2026. The festival stages bouyon and cadence from Dominica alongside zouk, kompa and diaspora guests. For bouyon, it is the biggest international showcase at home, where the genre was born.

Three nights at Windsor Park

Windsor Park, in Roseau, hosts the festival over three consecutive nights. The format is that of a national celebration as much as a festival: October is Dominican independence month, and the WCMF concentrates the island’s Creole energy in one place. Bouyon, cadence, zouk and kompa follow one another, with guests from the Creole diaspora.

For bouyon, the stakes are particular. Everywhere else, the genre plays on invited ground. At Windsor Park, it plays at home, before the audience that watched it be born and grow since 1988. That is what makes the WCMF bouyon’s biggest international showcase at home: programmers, media and French Caribbean partners see the Dominican scene there in its most concentrated state.

Night 1: Friday October 23, 2026

Night 1, on Friday October 23, 2026, brings together, on the bouyon and Creole side, TK International, the Midnight Groovers, Ridge, Colton T, Pudaz and Maurane Voyer, with headliners Capleton, Skillibeng and Klass on the same night. That line-up follows the official Discover Dominica Authority media release and the published guides, including Sweet TNT Magazine and writeups24.

Night 1 alone tells the arc of this site: the Midnight Groovers carry the cadence-lypso root, TK International and Ridge carry today’s Dominican bouyon, and Maurane Voyer brings the Martinican voice of “Joli BB”. Readers of our culture chapters will recognise every name: the festival is the point where all the stories cross for real, on one stage.

Nights 2 and 3: Reo, Asa Bantan, Shelly and Signal Band

Night 2, on Saturday October 24, 2026, features Reo and Asa Bantan on the bouyon side. Reo arrives with “Joli BB”, his duet with Maurane Voyer released a few days earlier. Asa Bantan, from Grand Bay, plays on the island where his village shares its history with the Midnight Groovers, as our cadence-lypso roots chapter tells.

Night 3, on Sunday October 25, 2026, brings together Shelly with Signal Band, along with Gwada G. Shelly and Signal Band have stacked trending collaborations, and their meeting on the festival’s big stage is one of the edition’s awaited bouyon moments. Over three nights, WCMF 2026 therefore displays the full chain: the root, today’s bosses, the rising voices and the bands that carry the live sound.

Festival week and the Bouyon Shattaz view

Festival week also includes Creole Conversations, a beachside conversation series with WCMF-connected personalities, on Tuesday October 20, 2026 at 4 PM at La Salamandre Beach Restaurant, Mero Beach. Smokiller helped shape that event and connect it to its guests, without owning it. It is an example of the passeur role described in our producers and passers chapter.

Bouyon Shattaz covers the festival as the strategic moment where Dominican artists’ visibility and French Caribbean partnerships are decided, and ran a three-post countdown series for the 2026 edition. For the island’s artists, a good night at Windsor Park can be worth a season of work: provided the credits, the images and the contacts leave with them after the festival.

Listening with the story in mind

This chapter is also an invitation to move around the site. Prepare for the festival with the bouyon playlist, the pages of Ridge, Asa Bantan and the history of bouyon. Every page answers another one: an artist page lights up a culture page, a style page lights up a festival page, a playlist turns reading into listening. You do not have to read everything in order. Come in through the name that intrigues you, the rhythm that hooked you, the festival everyone is talking about. Then come back here and read this chapter again with those sounds in your head. The second reading will be different, because the names will have become voices. That is exactly the journey Bouyon Shattaz wants for bouyon: first curiosity, then listening, then loyalty. And at every step, the same reflex: check who sings, who produces, who wrote, and where the sound comes from. An audience that asks those questions becomes an audience that protects the music it loves.

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Credit, dance and language

Finally, keep in mind the collective side of the World Creole Music Festival. No artist succeeds alone, and no style survives alone. You need radios that play the titles, media that tell the story, dancers who invent the steps, audiences who come back. You also need passeurs, the connectors who link one island to another, one studio to a stage, an elder to a newcomer. This site recognises itself in that connector role, with one compass: Dominica first, because that is where bouyon was born. Sharing this chapter is already taking part in that chain. Share it with the site link, with the names of the artists mentioned, with the playlist that goes with it. Passing it on does not need to be complicated to be fair. It only asks that the starting address is not erased. Bouyon has enough strength to travel far. It is on us to make its story travel with it.

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.

Reading, linking, passing it on

Language matters a great deal in this story. Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix is not a studio accident. It tells of an island that talks to its neighbours, that understands itself from one shore to another, that can move from one sentence to the next without losing the thread. For a French listener discovering the genre, that shared Creole is a doorway: the French Antilles hear it with immediate familiarity, while discovering an island that is English speaking and Creole speaking at the same time. So take the lyrics seriously. Even when the tone is light, even when the joke leads, the way of saying things is part of the music. A call, a nickname, an answer from the crowd: all of it builds the identity of the track. That is one reason this site names artists by their name and tracks by their exact title. Accuracy is a form of respect, in language as in credits.

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

Protecting what makes the party

To listen to the World Creole Music Festival with good ears, you have to slow down a little. Bouyon moves fast. It is built for the party, for the body, for the crowd answering back right away. But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. A genre is never just its best known title. It lives in the transitions, in the choruses sung back together, in the way a producer decides to let a loop breathe or to fire it up again. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

There is also a very concrete side to the World Creole Music Festival: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

Frequently asked questions

When does WCMF 2026 take place?

The 2026 World Creole Music Festival runs from Friday October 23 to Sunday October 25, 2026: three nights at Windsor Park, Roseau, Dominica, at independence time. For bouyon, it is the biggest international showcase at home, in the very city where the genre was born in 1988.

Which bouyon artists play WCMF 2026?

Night 1: TK International, Midnight Groovers, Ridge, Colton T, Pudaz and Maurane Voyer. Night 2: Reo and Asa Bantan. Night 3: Shelly with Signal Band, and Gwada G. This line-up follows the official Discover Dominica Authority communication and the published guides, including Sweet TNT Magazine and writeups24. The whole bouyon chain passes through in three evenings.

What is Creole Conversations?

Creole Conversations is a beachside conversation series with WCMF-connected personalities, held during festival week on Tuesday October 20, 2026 at 4 PM at La Salamandre Beach restaurant, Mero Beach. Smokiller helped shape the event and connect it to its guests, without owning it.

Who headlines Night 1 of WCMF 2026?

On Friday October 23, 2026, Capleton, Skillibeng and Klass headline, according to the official communication and the published guides. On the Dominica side, the same night brings together TK International, the Midnight Groovers, Ridge, Colton T and Pudaz, with Martinican singer Maurane Voyer and the duo DJ MJ and MV on the Keys. The cadence root and today's bouyon share the stage.

Who plays Night 2 of WCMF 2026?

According to the published guides, notably writeups24 and Sweet TNT, Saturday October 24, 2026 brings together Ne-Yo, Alkaline and Tabou Combo with, on the bouyon side, Asa Bantan, widely called the King of Bouyon, and Reo, billed as the Prince of Bouyon. Edday and Trilla G are also on the bill. Reo arrives with “Joli BB”, released a few days earlier.

Who plays the closing night of WCMF 2026?

Night 3, Sunday October 25, 2026, brings together, according to the published guides, Machel Montano, Uncle Waffles, Fridayy and Maxi Priest, with Shelly and Signal Band, Gwada G, Unstoppable Shax, Nice and producer DJ Taffy for Dominica. Shelly and Signal Band meeting on the main stage is one of the edition's most awaited bouyon moments.

What is the theme of WCMF 2026?

The theme of the 2026 edition is Pulsating Rhythms. Writeups24 presents it as the festival's 26th edition, from October 23 to 25, 2026, during Dominica's Independence season, based on the official Dominica Festivals post. The festival takes place at the Windsor Park Sports Stadium in Roseau. See also the World Creole Music Festival.

How long has the World Creole Music Festival existed?

The World Creole Music Festival was created in 1997 and is held at Windsor Park in Roseau. Nicknamed the festival that never sleeps, it celebrated its 25th edition from October 24 to 26, 2025, under the theme Global Echoes of the Nature Island. The 2026 edition, the 26th, is therefore the first after that quarter century.

Who organises WCMF?

The 25th edition, in 2025, was launched by the Ministry of Tourism and the Discover Dominica Authority, the DDA. For 2026, the published programme follows the DDA's official communication, whose media release notably lists Gwada G among Dominica's homegrown artists. The Sweet TNT Magazine and writeups24 guides then detail the nights.

What music can you hear at WCMF?

WCMF stages Dominica's bouyon and cadence alongside zouk, kompa and guests from the Creole diaspora. In 2026, the bill adds Jamaican reggae and dancehall, Trinidad soca and South African amapiano with Uncle Waffles. Bouyon still plays at home there, in front of the audience that saw it born.

Why does WCMF take place in October?

Because October is Dominica's independence month. WCMF runs for three consecutive nights during those celebrations, which gives it the format of a national celebration as much as a festival: the island's whole Creole energy is concentrated in one place, Windsor Park in Roseau. Festival week also hosts events such as Creole Conversations.

How does Bouyon Shattaz cover WCMF?

Bouyon Shattaz covers the festival as the strategic moment when the visibility of Dominican artists and partnerships with the French Caribbean are decided. The network devoted a three-post countdown series to the 2026 edition. For an island artist, a good night at Windsor Park can be worth a season of work, provided the credits leave with them.
