Three tracks, a run that installs a name

Bye to de Old Me (Remix), with Nice and Pudaz, shows 691K views on the popular shelf of Smokiller YouTube channel. It is the largest of the three figures recorded for Shelly in Bouyon Shattaz coverage, and the title through which many online listeners first crossed his name. A remix, two guests, and a voice that stays at the centre: the formula installs a signature.

With Signal Band, Where Did You Come From shows 334K views and What YOU Say 283K views on the same shelf. Taken together, the three tracks draw a readable path: Shelly does not depend on a single song. He follows a band collaboration with a shared title with Nice and Pudaz, and each release feeds the audience of the others. That is the sign of an artist building steadily, not a viral accident.

All three tracks sit in the Bouyon Shattaz ROAD MIX playlists. To hear them in the order of the scene, go through our bouyon playlist page: Shelly stands there alongside the other Dominican voices of the moment, and that proximity helps measure what sets him apart, the clarity of his voice inside productions built for the party.

A singer in a band scene

Dominican bouyon is historically band music, and Shelly carries his own name on the bill next to the band's, as in “Shelly and The Signal Band”. Presenting him as one of the headliners of Dominica new bouyon generation is not an empty compliment: it is a reading of his 2026 programme, his trending tracks and his festival place.

His path also shows the two routes of a bouyon career today. On one side, work with an established band, Signal Band, which brings the stage and the live tradition. On the other, titles shared with artists of his own generation, Nice and Pudaz on Bye to de Old Me (Remix), speaking the language of platforms and fast collaborations. Shelly moves between those two worlds without losing his name.

To place Shelly among his peers, the Dominica artists hub gathers the site profiles, from the founding bands to the current voices. The comparison is instructive: every generation had its formats, and Shelly generation is the first to build its reputation at the same time on the Windsor Park stage and on online video shelves.

Understanding bouyon before the name

To read the story of Shelly properly, bouyon first has to be put back at its source. The genre was created in Dominica, and its full story is told on our history of bouyon page. That reminder matters, because bouyon now travels through Guadeloupe, Martinique, France and the diaspora, sometimes far from the island that created it. Keeping the names of Dominican artists at the centre of the story keeps the map of the genre readable for new listeners.

Bouyon is band music, street music and party music as much as it is listening music. A track can live in a playlist in the morning, in a fete in the evening and on a big stage the following month. That is why a profile like this one is not limited to a list of songs: it is also about the stage, the audience and the place the island holds inside its own creation.

This page therefore follows a simple rule: verified facts only, titles attributed when they are a matter of reputation, and no invented numbers. When something is not confirmed, it is not written. That discipline is part of the work of Bouyon Shattaz, which documents the Dominican scene for French-speaking listeners as well as for the island's own English-speaking audience.

Rights, the other stage

Behind the party sits a question the Dominican scene is now asking more loudly: who owns the music, who signs the contracts and who is paid when a track travels? A song can fill a room and circulate online without its creators being properly protected. That is exactly the ground of the Bouyon Shattaz Rights Clinic, an audit carried out with artists and bands on the island to check contact routes, credits and the protection of their work.

Shelly is part of that Rights Clinic audit cohort, with a verified contact route. The detail may sound administrative, but it changes everything that follows: an artist who can be reached, who is correctly credited and who is well advised can turn one strong season into a lasting career. Bouyon Shattaz argues this point across its whole network strategy: Dominica created bouyon, so its artists should also benefit from its value.

The subject also returns often in public conversations around the scene, around contracts, royalties and intellectual property. Putting those questions on the same level as releases and concerts is a way of recognising that Creole music deserves the same professional standards as any other music industry.

Each profile points to the others, because no single artist tells the island's story alone. The scene is an ensemble, and it is that ensemble Bouyon Shattaz works to make visible.

Finally, the event that brings all of these worlds together remains the World Creole Music Festival, the WCMF, three nights in Roseau that place Dominica's Creole music in front of an audience from across the Caribbean. It is in that setting that the profile of Shelly takes its most concrete meaning in 2026.

Frequently asked questions

What are Shelly's trending songs?

“Bye to de Old Me (Remix)” with Nice and Pudaz, at 691K views on the popular shelf of Smokiller's YouTube channel, then “Where Did You Come From” and “What YOU Say” with Signal Band, at 334K and 283K views. All three are in Bouyon Shattaz's ROAD MIX playlists and trace a steady run, not a viral accident.

Where is Shelly from?

Shelly comes from Dominica, the island where bouyon was born. Shelly's songs belong to the current Dominican scene, with Signal Band, a bouyon band from the island, and with artists of the same generation such as Nice and Pudaz, up to Night 3 of the 2026 World Creole Music Festival at Windsor Park, in Roseau.

What are Shelly's songs?

Shelly's listed songs include “Bye to de Old Me” and its remix with Nice and Pudaz, “Where Did You Come From”, “What YOU Say”, Signal Band's album “The Electric Room” (2024) and “We Ready” with Trilla-G, plus features on “Stronger”, “Doh See Me” (2021), “Ani Ba Yo Love” (2018), “Dwivé” and “Me With People” (2024).

Which artists have collaborated with Shelly?

Signal Band is the most constant partner, from “The Electric Room” to “Where Did You Come From”. Shelly also shares “Bye to de Old Me (Remix)” with Nice and Pudaz, “We Ready” and “Dwivé” with Trilla-G, “Me With People” with Moxie Shervy, “Make Her Bawl” with DJ Taffy and Trixx, and “Home” with Taffy. See DJ Taffy.

Why did Bye to de Old Me make such an impact?

Because the song became a reset anthem: Sokah2Soca, picked up by Island Vybe in November 2025, describes a chorus made to stick, “Bye, bye, bye to the old me”, and presents it as the track set to carry bouyon to Trinidad carnival 2026. Its video won video of the year at the Dominica Music Awards in January 2026.

Who produced Bye to de Old Me (Remix)?

According to production notes published by Sokah2Soca and picked up by Island Vybe, the version by Shelly, Nice and Pudaz was composed and produced by Dernel "Dernz" Green. Fr3EzE added riddim programming, Giovannie "Gio" Green played live bass, and backing vocals came from Darren, Dernel and Giovannie Green with Liam Sebastien. See Nice.

What are Shelly's 2026 nominations?

At the 2026 Caribbean Music Awards, the bouyon artist of the year finalist list published by Black Island Girl includes “Shelly and Signall Band”, as the name was printed, alongside Asa Bantan, Reo, Mr Ridge and Trilla G among others. This site therefore reports that listing as a nomination: the results published in September 2026 give the bouyon artist of the year award to Mr Ridge.

When does Shelly play WCMF 2026?

According to published festival guides, Shelly and Signal Band play Night 3 of WCMF 2026, on Sunday October 25, 2026, at Windsor Park, the closing night. It is the central date of their year, at home, in front of a crowd that knows the songs. On Thursday October 22 at 7 pm, they also close the Ti Vilaj Kwéyòl 2026 grand finale in Roseau.
