Denmath, the musical Swiss-army knife of bouyon between Nantes and Paris

Published October 9, 2026

Denmath, whose legal name is Mathurin DENMATH Denis, is a producer, sound engineer and artistic director from Saint-Martin, of Haitian heritage, based between Nantes and Paris. His official site denmathbeats.fr lists his specialties plainly: bouyon, kompa, shatta, dancehall, soca and zouk. In a genre where singers take most of the spotlight, Denmath is a reminder of a simple truth: bouyon is made in the studio first, riddim after riddim, by producers who know several Creole traditions at once. This page gathers what is documented about his path, his riddims and his collaboration now under way with Bouyon Shattaz.

A producer from Saint-Martin, shaped by several Creole worlds

Denmath comes from Saint-Martin, an island where French, Dutch, English-speaking and Caribbean cultures meet every day. That grounding helps explain his work: bouyon itself is a music of mixture, created in Dominica from cadence-lypso, jing ping and other traditions of the island, as our page on the history of bouyon explains. A producer who grows up at a Creole crossroads learns early that genre borders are porous, and that the same rhythm can carry several languages and several dances.

His Haitian heritage adds another layer to the profile. Kompa, the reference music of Haiti, is one of the specialties listed on his site, alongside bouyon and zouk. That dual culture, Saint-Martin and Haitian, shows in how he presents himself: not as the specialist of a single sound, but as a craftsman who can move from one world to another depending on the artist in front of him. It is exactly the profile of the passers this site follows on its page about the producers and passers of bouyon, the behind-the-scenes figures who move the sound between the islands and France.

Based between Nantes and Paris, Denmath works from mainland France, where a large part of the French Antillean bouyon audience lives and listens online. That position, between the home islands and the French scenes, has become strategic for bouyon: riddims travel as files, voices are recorded at a distance, and tracks are tested on social media before they reach a stage. Denmath embodies this new geography of the genre, where the studio no longer has a single address but a network.

Sound engineer, artistic director, musical Swiss-army knife

On his Instagram account @prod.denmath, with 823 followers and 34 posts at the October 17, 2026 check, Denmath calls himself a couteau-suisse musical, a musical Swiss-army knife, with the SXM flag in his bio. The phrase fits: producer, sound engineer and artistic director are three different trades, and he claims all three. Producing means building the riddim and the structure of a track. Sound engineering means recording, mixing and giving a song the level and impact that platforms and dancefloors demand. Artistic direction means helping an artist choose the right direction, the right tempo and the right tone.

That versatility explains the range of formats his name appears on. There are his own bouyon riddims, built as open supports for singers. There are productions for specific artists, where the beat serves a voice and a piece of writing. There are feature appearances, where the producer steps out of the studio and into the credits as an artist. And there is sound supervision of whole projects, as on T-Odah’s Crash to the Moon EP, where his role covers production and sound supervision. For a young Dominican or Antillean singer, working with a complete profile like this changes a lot: instead of buying an anonymous beat online, he enters a real making process.

That way of working also matters for rights, which sit at the heart of the Bouyon Shattaz project. A producer who knows who did what on a track, and who keeps track of files and versions, protects his own work and the artists’ work too. It is a professional culture that bouyon, long informal in how it circulated, is now adopting, and Denmath is one of the profiles carrying it on the France side.

His bouyon riddims: Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim

The riddim is the basic unit of modern bouyon: an instrumental loop built to last, on which one or several artists can record. Denmath released his own bouyon riddims in 2022, with Tropical Riddim in the summer of 2022 and Harley Riddim the same year. Two titles that sum up his method: instrumentals designed for the season, for dancing and for the voices that will claim them. A riddim is not a simple backing track, it is a complete rhythmic proposal, with its rises, its breaks and its spaces left open for the singer.

Working in riddims also means thinking in networks. The same instrumental can travel from one island to another, receive voices in Kweyol, French or English, and build the producer’s reputation with each new version. It is the historic model of Jamaican dancehall, long adapted by bouyon and soca. To hear the spirit of those instrumentals and of the tracks carrying the genre today, the ROAD MIX bouyon playlist from Bouyon Shattaz gathers the songs that matter, from classics to the newest producer work.

Looking back at those 2022 riddims also measures the distance travelled: between his first bouyon releases and his 2025 and 2026 collaborations, Denmath has widened his network, from Saint-Martin toward Martinique, Dominica and France. The riddims remain the starting point, the sound signature from which the meetings happen.

The collaborations: Koko Bwa, Lyte, Jojo Trippy, Rio Crows and T-Odah

The track that brought Denmath into Bouyon Shattaz coverage is called Tarif. Released in April 2025, it is credited to Koko Bwa, featuring Denmath with Boutcha Bwa. The lead artist, Koko Bwa, is from Martinique, and Tarif is his first single. For Denmath, appearing as a feature on the first track of an artist closely followed in the French Antilles is a way of signing the sound from inside, not only making it behind the scenes. In October 2026, Smokiller agreed to share and post Tarif on Bouyon Shattaz, at his management’s request.

His credits go well beyond that one track. The Shazam credits for Lyte’s Issa Cultural Ting EP list his production on the track In She Garden. In 2020, he produced Ou kon Tebe for Jojo Trippy. In March 2024, he appeared as a feature on Rio Crows’ Can’t Blame You, on the Wet Jersey project. And on T-Odah’s Crash to the Moon EP, his role rises another level, with production and sound supervision of the whole project. Taken together, these credits draw a network producer, working with artists from different scenes without losing his bouyon and Creole centre of gravity.

These collaborations should be read with care, as this site does: only documented credits are kept here, with nothing inflated or invented. That is the house rule for every artist profile, and it matters most for producers, whose work is often credited loosely on social media. When a credit is verified, it is named. When it is not, it does not appear.

October 2026: the link-up with Bouyon Shattaz and the beat pool

In October 2026, Denmath’s management, Ambre Lazaro of Mycro Nova Studio, contacted Bouyon Shattaz to pitch a feature. What followed moved fast: Smokiller agreed to post Tarif, Ambre then shared a Google Drive folder of Denmath’s beats, leading with his 2026 productions, and she green-lit a first collaboration with BONNA, the Dominican bouyon artist from La Plaine. Her reply was one sentence: We can collaborate with him to begin. The profile of BONNA on this site tells the artist’s story on the Dominica side; the meeting with a France-based producer shows the other half of the circuit Bouyon Shattaz wants to build.

The most structural part of the exchange is not only musical, it is legal. Bouyon Shattaz proposed a beat protection agreement, approved in content by Denmath’s management: ownership of the beats stays with Denmath, pool members may record freely on a beat, but any public release needs the producer’s prior written OK, routed through Bouyon Shattaz. Downloads are logged and emailed to all parties, the artist, Smokiller and the producer. On their side, Denmath’s team registers the beats on Musicstart as its own protection measure. This simple, one-page agreement in plain language sets the base for a private pool reserved for artists in the network, where riddims circulate without ever escaping their author.

For bouyon, that is an important culture change. Too many tracks came out with no clear agreement, with producers uncredited or unpaid, and artists exposed afterwards. The model defended here is the opposite: simple access to record, a clear rule to release, and a written trace at every step. If the collaboration with BONNA lands as planned, it will become the first concrete case of this system, between a producer from Saint-Martin based in France and a singer from La Plaine, Dominica.

Why Denmath matters for the bouyon of tomorrow

Denmath does not chase the singers’ spotlight, and that is precisely why his profile interests this site. The bouyon of 2026 is played as much in studios and shared folders as on stages: producers composing the riddims, engineers setting the sound level, artistic directors helping voices find their format. Following Denmath means following that workshop, from Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim to the 2026 beats destined for the Bouyon Shattaz pool.

His path also connects the territories the genre crosses today: Saint-Martin as origin, Haitian heritage and kompa, Nantes and Paris as work bases, Martinique with Koko Bwa, and now Dominica with BONNA. Few producers tick so many boxes of the French-speaking and English-speaking Creole world at once. To hear where the sound is going, start with the tracks named on this page, then continue with the site’s bouyon playlist and the other producer profiles followed by Bouyon Shattaz.
