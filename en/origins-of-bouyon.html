Origins of bouyon: Roseau, 1988, the WCK fusion

Published September 2, 2026

Bouyon was born in Roseau, the capital of Dominica, in 1988. Its creator was WCK, short for Windward Caribbean Kulture. The founding move was a fusion: take cadence-lypso and jing ping, the island’s two dancing inheritances, and plug them into electronic keyboards and drum machines. The result was not simple modernising. It was a new music, built for Dominica’s fetes, sung between English and Dominican Creole, which would become the sound of the Mas Domnik carnival and then cross the sea.

WCK and the move of 1988

In 1988, WCK made the choice Dominican musicians had been waiting for: keep the soul of the island’s traditions, but speak the rhythmic language of its time. Cadence-lypso brought the guitar, the Creole storytelling and the party function. Jing ping brought its instruments and its dances. Keyboards and drum machines brought the power and the steadiness needed to stand up to imported sounds.

The group’s full name, Windward Caribbean Kulture, already states the programme: a culture of the Windward Islands, fully owned and modern at once. WCK was not trying to copy an outside model. The group built a Dominican sound that could hold a stage against the music then dominating Caribbean airwaves, without giving up the dances and rhythms that had formed earlier generations.

Jing ping, the traditional root

Jing ping is Dominica’s traditional dance music. The accordion leads, the lapo kabwit drums mark the time, and the boom-boom and bamboo flute complete a sound made for dancing together. Around jing ping live dance traditions such as bèlè, kwadril, chanté mas and mazurka, which structure the fetes and the memory of the villages.

By bringing jing ping into the fusion, WCK plugged bouyon straight into that memory. The new genre did not float above the island: it descended from its dances. That is one reason Dominican audiences took to it immediately. Young listeners heard a modern machine, older listeners recognised familiar steps and gestures. Bouyon was born modern and rooted in the same movement.

A soup where nothing is lost

The word bouyon itself tells the method. In Creole, bouyon is a soup: many ingredients go in, everything mixes, and nothing is lost. The genre works exactly that way. Cadence-lypso, jing ping, spoken influences, and dancehall and ragga in the vocal delivery: every contribution finds its place in the pot without erasing the others.

Around WCK, other pioneers took part in the invention, notably Roots, Stems and Branches, known as RSB, and First Serenade. The birth of bouyon was therefore also collective, the work of a Dominican scene experimenting at the same time. Remembering those pioneers’ names is part of the memory work this site defends, alongside the date, 1988, and the place, Roseau.

Why 1988, why Dominica

The context explains the urgency. In the 1980s, zouk from Martinique and Guadeloupe dominated Caribbean airwaves. Dominican musicians wanted a modern sound of their own, not a copy. They wanted to keep the soul of their traditions while speaking to the young people of their generation. The WCK fusion answered exactly that demand: a Dominican identity, amplified by electronics.

In the 1990s, bouyon dominated Dominica. It became the music of the Mas Domnik carnival and of village fetes, the gathering where the island recognises itself. Vocal delivery shaped by spoken, dancehall and ragga influences opened branches such as bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng, also called wrecketeng. From its first years, the genre showed it could branch out without losing its centre, which remains Dominican.

But behind the speed there are layers: a guitar that recalls an older story, a keyboard pushing the rhythm forward, a voice that talks as much as it sings. Take the time to listen twice. The first time for the energy, the second time for the details. The chapter makes full sense on that second listen, because you start hearing where the sounds come from and where they are running to. Listening that way is already a form of respect for the artists’ work.

Credit, dance and language

There is also a very concrete side to the origins of bouyon: dance. Bouyon is a music of the body before it is a music of commentary. The steps, the challenges, the short videos and the group covers are part of how it normally circulates. A successful dance can carry a title further than a long speech, because it makes people want to redo it, film it and pass it on. That is why this site talks about bouyon danse as much as bouyon music: both tell the same story through different doors. When you watch a dance, listen for the exact moment the step changes. That is often where the producer placed the clearest idea. The body sometimes understands the rhythm before the head does, and bouyon has always known how to speak to the body first. Keeping that joy at the centre is also a way of not reducing the genre to an argument: it remains a party, and the party is its first language.

What a new listener often remembers first is a chorus. Then comes a question: who is behind this chorus, and what should I listen to next? This chapter tries to answer that second, most important question. Behind every featured title there is a chain: an artist, a producer, sometimes a label, sometimes a media outlet that relayed it at the right moment. Following that chain is how you move from passing listener to member of a scene. You do not need to know everything to start. Just remember a few names, look them up, follow them, and share their titles with the right credit. The rest comes with regular listening. Go back to the site playlist between readings: it is designed as the soundtrack to these chapters, and every return will make the names more familiar and the differences between the styles clearer.

Reading, linking, passing it on

The credit question runs through this whole chapter, and it is not an administrative detail. Naming a genre’s origin means naming the people who invented it, the villages that carried it, the fetes that made it grow. When a sound travels, it can easily lose its address. Titles travel, dances travel, clips travel, but the name of the place it started does not always travel with them. Bouyon Shattaz defends a simple rule: the bigger bouyon gets, the louder its Dominican origin should be stated. That is not closing the door to other scenes. It is opening the door properly, knowing who built the house. Every scene that takes up bouyon adds something, and this chapter says so. But adding is not replacing. Memory is part of the sound, and an audience that knows the story listens better, shares better and credits better.

Language matters a great deal in this story. Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix is not a studio accident. It tells of an island that talks to its neighbours, that understands itself from one shore to another, that can move from one sentence to the next without losing the thread. For a French listener discovering the genre, that shared Creole is a doorway: the French Antilles hear it with immediate familiarity, while discovering an island that is English speaking and Creole speaking at the same time. So take the lyrics seriously. Even when the tone is light, even when the joke leads, the way of saying things is part of the music. A call, a nickname, an answer from the crowd: all of it builds the identity of the track. That is one reason this site names artists by their name and tracks by their exact title. Accuracy is a form of respect, in language as in credits.

Protecting what makes the party

Let us talk plainly about rights and money, because the origins of bouyon does not live on applause alone. A track that travels should bring something back to the people who made it. That means clean credits, written agreements, well named files and identified rights holders. That language can feel far from the party, but it decides who will still be able to record tomorrow. The scenes that last are the ones that learn to protect their creators without killing spontaneity. Bouyon was born in village fetes and carnival, and it can keep that soul while becoming professional on paper. This site therefore insists on both sides at once: celebrate the sound, and document the work. When an agreement protects a producer, when a credit names a writer, when a page tells the right origin, those are small concrete wins. Added together, they change an island’s place in the industry.

It is also worth saying what this chapter does not claim to do. It replaces neither the artists, nor their teams, nor the island’s archives. It offers a documented story, built from the sources Bouyon Shattaz has gathered, and it shows its support where that is needed. If a detail feels missing to you, treat it as a doorway: go and listen to the artist, read their page, watch how the scene itself tells its own story. Bouyon culture is alive, so no chapter can close it. This text instead aims to give solid markers so nobody gets lost: an origin, names, places, forms. With those markers, everyone can then build their own path, their own favourites, their own arguments. Bouyon loves an argument; it was born inside one. But a good argument starts with shared facts, and that is what these pages are for.

Frequently asked questions

Who created bouyon, and in which year?

Bouyon was created in Roseau, Dominica, in 1988 by WCK (Windward Caribbean Kulture), with pioneers such as Roots, Stems and Branches (RSB) and First Serenade around the group. The founding move was a fusion: plugging the island's cadence-lypso and jing ping into electronic keyboards and drum machines.

Is bouyon from Dominica or Guadeloupe?

Bouyon comes from Dominica: it was born in Roseau in 1988, with WCK. Guadeloupe then received it from its neighbour island, danced it as jump up and built its own scene, bouyon gwada. Guadeloupe made the genre bigger, but the invention is Dominican. See Gwada seen from Dominica.

What does the word bouyon mean?

Bouyon is the Creole word for a soup: you put in many ingredients, everything mixes and nothing is lost. The name describes the genre's method exactly, fusing cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines, then welcoming dancehall and ragga into the way vocals are delivered. See the meaning of bouyon.

What does WCK stand for?

WCK stands for Windward Caribbean Kulture, a culture of the Windward Islands, proud and modern at once. The name already states the programme of the band that created bouyon in 1988: not copying an outside model, but building a Dominican sound able to hold the stage against the music that dominated Caribbean airwaves at the time. See the WCK page.

What music does bouyon come from?

Bouyon descends from two dance traditions of Dominica. Cadence-lypso brings the guitar, Creole storytelling and the party function. Jing ping brings its instruments and its dances. Keyboards and drum machines add the power and regularity needed to compete with imported sounds, and the vocals later borrow from dancehall and ragga.

What is jing ping, the root of bouyon?

Jing ping is Dominica's traditional dance music: the accordion leads, lapo kabwit drums keep time, and the boom-boom and bamboo flute complete the sound. Around it live the bèlè, the kwadril, chanté mas and the mazurka. By bringing it in, WCK plugged bouyon into the memory of the villages. See jing ping.

Who were the bouyon pioneers alongside WCK?

Around WCK, other Dominican bands took part in the invention, notably Roots, Stems and Branches, known as RSB, and First Serenade. The birth of bouyon was therefore also a collective affair: a scene experimenting at the same time. Remembering those names is part of the memory work, just like the date, 1988, and the place, Roseau.

Why was bouyon born in the late 1980s?

Because in the 1980s, zouk from Martinique and Guadeloupe dominated Caribbean airwaves. Dominican musicians wanted a modern sound of their own, not a copy, able to keep the soul of their traditions while speaking to the young people of their generation. WCK's fusion answered exactly that demand: a Dominican identity, amplified by electronics.

What language is bouyon sung in?

Bouyon mixes English and Dominican Creole, and that mix tells the story of an island that talks to its neighbours. That shared language later helped the genre cross the sea to Guadeloupe and Martinique, where audiences hear it with instant familiarity. A call, a nickname or a crowd response are all part of a track's identity.

Is bouyon the music of Dominica's carnival?

Yes. In the 1990s, bouyon dominates Dominica and becomes the music of the Roseau carnival, Mas Domnik, and of village fetes: the moment when the island recognises itself. The genre was born for those parties, sung in English and Dominican Creole, before it crossed the sea. See Dominica carnival.

Which styles grew out of bouyon in Dominica?

From the 1990s, vocals shaped by dancehall and ragga opened bouyon-muffin, then Wrecketeng, the sample-based branch. In 2003, Dada launched bouyon soca in Dominica, according to the first-hand testimony of Sean “Smokiller” Francis. Each branch grew without losing its centre, which stays Dominican. See Wrecketeng.

What were WCK's first records?

WCK albums logged by this site include Culture Shock (1990), with “Culture Shock” and “Dance Floor”, and Follow the Leader (1991), with “Follow the Leader” and “Land Of Sunshine”. They are followed by Tou Cho Tou Flam (1995), with “Balance Batty”, Pride and Joy (2000) and On Top (2002). These are the recordings of the band that created bouyon.
