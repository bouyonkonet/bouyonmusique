Bouyon glossary: the words of the genre, explained

By Smokiller · Published October 21, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Bouyon speaks several languages at once: Dominica's Kweyol, the vocabulary of carnival, the words of producers and those of the genre's historians. This glossary explains, in plain language, the terms that return across this site: riddim, jing ping, lapo kabwit, chante mas, cadence-lypso and Wrecketeng, with a definition for each word that opened sources can verify.

Words of the roots: jing ping, lapo kabwit, chante mas

Jing ping is Dominica's traditional dance music, carried by the accordion, the lapo kabwit and the bamboo flute. When WCK created bouyon in Roseau in 1988, the band plugged that tradition into electronic keyboards: the accordion can still be heard in early bouyon tracks. The lapo kabwit is Dominica's goat skin drum, the drum of chante mas and of carnival: at Mas Domnik, whole groups parade behind its drummers.

Chante mas, the masquerade song, is the singing tradition of Dominican carnival, sung in a responsorial, call and response style. Its lyrics, almost all in French Creole, retell the gossip and scandals of the moment with humour, and they were traditionally led by women, the chantwel, while the instrumental accompaniment fell to men. Bouyon keeps that tradition's call and response energy in its fete vocals.

Words of the studio: riddim, bouyon soca, Wrecketeng

A riddim is the instrumental over which several artists record their voices. It is the central support of bouyon and dancehall production: one riddim can carry several tracks, and the great riddims become trademarks for producers, like Denmath's Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim, both released in 2022. Bouyon soca is the fusion of Dominican bouyon with the festive codes of soca. According to the first-hand testimony of Smokiller, who was in the studios at the time, bouyon soca was started in Dominica by Dada in 2003, before Triple Kay and other bands popularized it.

Wrecketeng is the Dominican practice of rebuilding existing recordings over bouyon instrumentals, in the lineage of bouyon-muffin. Its official spelling, Wrecketeng, comes from DJ Cut, the pioneer of the style: the reketeng form, spread across the internet, is a misspelling, and this site writes it only to flag the error. Keeping the spelling matters, because it is what makes it possible to find the right sources and credit the right pioneers.

Words of carnival: Mas Domnik, J'ouvert, Kweyol

Mas Domnik is the name of Dominica carnival, known for its traditional costumes, its chante mas and, since the late 1980s, bouyon as its soundtrack. J'ouvert is its opening moment, in the early hours of the morning: bands, paint and bouyon and soca music in the streets of Roseau, before the main parades. Kweyol is Dominican Creole, the language of the island's traditional songs and of a large part of bouyon's choruses, alongside English and French depending on the artist.

Cadence-lypso completes this vocabulary base: it is the Dominican sound of the 1970s, born from the meeting of cadence and calypso, carried by bands like Exile One and the Grammacks. With jing ping, it is one of bouyon's two direct parents, and understanding the word helps explain why bouyon sounds both old and electronic.

How to use this glossary

Every word in this glossary points to detailed pages on this site: the jing ping page for the traditional root, the bouyon riddim page for the producers' work, the instruments of bouyon page for the lapo kabwit and the chakchak, and the Wrecketeng page for the full history of the practice and its spelling. The definitions stay deliberately short: a glossary gives the meaning, the linked pages give the history.

If a word is missing from this list, it is often because no reliable opened source yet makes it possible to define it properly. This site prefers a shorter, verified glossary to a long, invented dictionary, and the list will grow with verified research, like the rest of BouyonMusique.com.

The bouyon glossary from A to Z

Jump to letter: A · B · C · D · F · G · I · J · K · L · M · N · O · Q · R · S · T · V · W · Z

Accordion
The lead instrument of jing ping, carrying the melody of Dominica's traditional dances. When WCK built bouyon, its role passed to electronic keyboards, yet the accordion can still be heard in early bouyon tracks. Read more
Bamboo flute
A wind instrument of the classic jing ping line-up, played alongside the accordion, the lapo kabwit and the boom-boom. It belongs to the acoustic layer of Dominican dance music that bouyon later plugged into machines. Read more
Bele
One of Dominica's traditional Creole ball dances, alongside the quadrille and the mazurka, danced to jing ping at village fairs and evening gatherings. It belongs to the oldest layer of the sound bouyon grew from. Read more
Bele drum
The traditional drum at the heart of the bele dance. It sits among the Dominican percussion that a bouyon drum kit starts from, next to the lapo kabwit, the chakchak and the syak. Read more
Bouyon
A fast party and carnival music created in Dominica in the late 1980s, mixing jing ping, cadence-lypso and other local traditions with keyboards and drum machines. The name means soup in Kweyol, a dish where everything mixes together. Read more
Bouyon dance
There is no single official bouyon step: each era and each riddim brings its own moves, from swaying on the choruses to collective jumps on the build-ups. On social media, producers dance to their own riddims to launch a track. Read more
Bouyon drum kit
The set of sounds a producer uses to recreate Dominican carnival in software: sampled traditional percussion such as the lapo kabwit and the chakchak, fast drums, synth bass, keyboards and crowd vocals. Read more
Bouyon gwada
The branch of bouyon that grew between Dominica and Guadeloupe, also called hardcore bouyon. Harder, faster and rawer than the bouyon of the live bands, it sets blunt lyrics over heavy percussive riddims, and its origin stays Dominican. Read more
Bouyon instrumental
The riddim without the voice: the loop, the bass and the percussion on their own. DJs use it to mix, dancers to create and producers to show their work, and a good one holds a dance floor without a singer. Read more
Bouyon Monarch
A bouyon crown competed for in Dominica. Carlyn XP won the Bouyon Monarch title two years running, and Clint H and Sa Sa Sammy were among the finalists of the 2017 competition. Read more
Bouyon Road March
A competition first held in Dominica during Mas Domnik 2025 to crown the bouyon track of the carnival by popular vote. The first title went to RAGS, by Trilla G, Shelly and Skinny Fabulous. Read more
Bouyon soca
The fusion of Dominican bouyon with the festive codes of Trinidad soca. According to the first-hand testimony of Smokiller, it was started in Dominica by Dada in 2003, before Triple Kay and other bands popularized it. Read more
Bouyon-muffin
One of bouyon's first offshoots, from the 1990s, when Dominican singers laid voices inspired by Jamaican dancehall and raggamuffin over bouyon riddims. Skinny Banton, who worked with WCK from 1995, is its key figure. Read more
BPM
The tempo measure that matters so much in bouyon, a deliberately fast genre. There is no single figure, but the examples documented on this site sit around 160 BPM, such as HYPNO's Mauvais Plan. Read more
Bushtown Clan
A Dominican collective at the centre of the early development of Wrecketeng. Born of bouyon-muffin and inspired by DJ Cut, the Clan helped establish the style through DJ performances, remixes and recordings at the turn of the 2000s. Read more
Cadence-lypso
The Dominican sound of the 1970s, born from the meeting of cadence and calypso and carried by bands like Exile One and the Grammacks. With jing ping, it is one of the two direct parents of bouyon. Read more
Chakchak (shak shak)
The Creole maracas of Dominica, also written shak shak. It is one of the traditional instruments listed among the sounds of bouyon, and producers still keep it in their bouyon drum kits. Read more
Chante mas
The masquerade song, the singing tradition of Dominican carnival, performed in call and response. Its lyrics, mostly in French Creole, retell the gossip of the moment with humour, and bouyon keeps its call and response energy. Read more
Chantwel
The women who traditionally led chante mas, the masquerade song of Dominican carnival, while the instrumental accompaniment fell to men. Bouyon still carries the call and response energy they led. Read more
Dancehall
The Jamaican genre that is one of bouyon's original ingredients. From dancehall voices on bouyon riddims in the 1990s, through bouyon-muffin, to today's Martinican shatta, its thread runs through the whole Dominican sound. Read more
Dennery Segment
The fast carnival sound of Saint Lucia, born in the village of Dennery in the 2010s and first called Lucian kuduro. It blends soca, dancehall and zouk over heavy percussion, with lyrics in Saint Lucian Creole. Read more
Drum machine
The electronic rhythm box that, together with keyboards, turned Dominica's traditions into bouyon. WCK built the genre on this technology, and producers now chase the same carnival pressure in software. Read more
First Serenade Band
An eleven piece band from Pointe Michel, Dominica, named with WCK and RSB among the leading groups of early bouyon. It received a lifetime achievement award at the 1999 World Creole Music Festival and is still active in 2026. Read more
Free up
A free session, often live, where a DJ and musicians play the party in real time, with keyboard improvisation, calls to the crowd and the season's records chained together. DJ MJ and MV on the Keys work in this format. Read more
Gospel bouyon
The branch that sets Christian praise lyrics on the machinery of bouyon: fast tempo, keyboard loops, a pushing bass and choir responses. It shows how bouyon serves the street, the carnival and the church in Dominica. Read more
Independence season
The weeks of celebration around 3 November, the date Dominica gained independence from the United Kingdom in 1978. The 2026 season ran from 19 September to 4 November, with the Wob Dwiyet pageants and, in October, the World Creole Music Festival. Read more
J'ouvert
From jour ouvert in French Creole, the dawn fete that opens carnival's big days. From 4 in the morning the street belongs to the crowd, to mud, paint and music, and in Dominica bouyon leads the way. Read more
Jing ping
Dominica's traditional dance music, led by the accordion with the lapo kabwit, the boom-boom and the bamboo flute. It accompanies the quadrille, the mazurka and bele, and is one of the two founding ingredients of bouyon. Read more
Jounen Kweyol
Creole Day in Kweyol, celebrated in Dominica and Saint Lucia with songs in the language. In Saint Lucia, labels release whole riddims for the occasion, voiced by several artists, such as the Qwadrill Riddim in 2014. Read more
Juggling (riddim album)
The set of tracks born from the same riddim, also called a riddim album. Each artist keeps their own writing and flow over a shared instrumental, so listeners can compare the voices on an equal frame. Read more
Jump up
The name Guadeloupe and Martinique give to the bouyon of Dominica, borrowed from an older label for very rhythmic carnival music. It sums up the effect of the sound: a fast tempo that makes everybody jump. Read more
Keyboards
The melodic backbone of bouyon since WCK: synth lines play the melody, the chords and the calls that structure a party track. In modern bouyon the keyboardist often arranges too, building the loop and preparing the breaks. Read more
Kweyol
Dominican Creole, the language of the island's traditional songs and of most bouyon choruses, mixed with English. Close to the Creoles of Guadeloupe, Martinique and Saint Lucia, it let bouyon cross the sea. Read more
Lapo kabwit
Dominica's goat skin drum, the drum of chante mas and of carnival: at Mas Domnik, whole groups parade behind its drummers. Producers still sample it in their bouyon drum kits. Read more
Mas Domnik
The Creole name of Dominica's carnival, held in Roseau before Lent and known for its old forms: chante mas, lapo kabwit bands and traditional characters. Bouyon has been its music since the 1990s. Read more
Mazurka
A Creole ball dance of Dominica, danced to jing ping alongside the quadrille and bele. Inherited from the island's Creole history, it is one of the traditional ingredients that bouyon draws on. Read more
Mix
The natural format of bouyon: tracks chained at the same tempo so the party never stops. A good bouyon mix blends Dominican new releases, bouyon soca and carnival classics, a craft inherited from carnival sound systems. Read more
New Bouyon
A label for today's bouyon that covers several distinct uses, each with its own author and date. Ricky Bishop, for one, presents his New Bouyon concept, dated 2024, as a modern reinvention with electro and trap elements. Read more
Nursery Krew Inc (NKI)
A Dominican crew of the early 2000s that pushed Wrecketeng from DJ and remix practice into original recordings. Its names include Burn Brain Jolly, Charlie Black, Watty and Smokiller, on tracks like Peanuts and Sorti Desandre. Read more
Old school bouyon
The bouyon of the live bands that ruled Dominica in the 1990s, such as WCK, Roots, Stems and Branches and First Serenade. Played by full line-ups for long carnival nights, it remains the purists' reference. Read more
Quadrille (kwadril)
A Creole ball dance of Dominica, also written kwadril, danced to jing ping with the mazurka and bele. It appears in the recipe of bouyon, which draws on these traditional dances. Read more
Remix
A well known track, often an Afro, pop, rap or R&B hit, rebuilt with the codes of bouyon: faster tempo, keyboard loop, Dominican percussion and carnival energy. The original voice stays while the ground underneath changes. Read more
Riddim
The instrumental over which several artists record their own voices, the central support of bouyon production as in dancehall. Great riddims carry names and become producer trademarks, like Denmath's Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim. Read more
RnBouyon
The fusion of bouyon and R&B, spelled as one word: Dominica's party rhythm carried by sung voices, soft melodies and urban production. Deezer used the term in 2026 to present the singer Fallon. Read more
Roots, Stems and Branches (RSB)
A Dominican band that reference works name, with WCK and First Serenade, among the leading groups of the bouyon born in Roseau in the late 1980s. Beyond that, no album or named member appears in the public record. Read more
Shatta
A dancehall genre born in Martinique, tied to today's Antillean producers and their short, hard riddims made for dance and social media. It is distinct from bouyon, and shatta bouyon is where the two meet. Read more
Shatta bouyon
The most visible form of bouyon today on social media and in clubs: short, hard, danceable riddims from the new Antillean and French generation, keeping Dominica's fast tempo, looping keyboards, synth bass and Creole. Read more
Soca
The carnival music of Trinidad, born in the 1970s from calypso fused with Indian instruments and rhythms such as the dholak. It is a parallel invention to bouyon, and the two meet in bouyon soca. Read more
Syak (gwaj)
The scraper of Dominica's traditional percussion, also called gwaj. Listed among the traditional instruments of bouyon, it sits with the lapo kabwit, the bele drum and the chakchak in the first family of sounds of a bouyon drum kit. Read more
Ti Vilaj Kweyol
Kweyol for the little Creole village: a festival of Dominican food, crafts, live music and language at the Botanic Gardens in Roseau. Its fifth edition ran from 19 to 22 October 2026, ending on the eve of the World Creole Music Festival. Read more
TK International (Triple Kay)
A Dominican bouyon band, also known as Triple Kay Band and Triple Kay International, in the live band tradition that followed WCK. One of the major Dominican references of the 2000s, it helped popularize bouyon soca. Read more
Voicing
Recording a new vocal over an existing riddim. A producer builds the instrumental, then several artists voice it, each with their own lyrics and flow, before the versions come out as singles or on a riddim album. Read more
Warm up
An instrumental version or a remix designed to heat up parties and DJ mixes before carnival. Labels such as bouyon warm up 2026 tell DJs which season the track is aimed at, not the year it was uploaded. Read more
WCK (Windward Caribbean Kulture)
Windward Caribbean Kulture, the band from Roseau, Dominica, credited with creating bouyon in the late 1980s by fusing cadence-lypso and jing ping with keyboards and drum machines. Formed in 1986, the band marks its 40th anniversary in 2026. Read more
Wob dwiyet
Dominica's national dress, at the centre of two Independence season pageants: Miss Wob Dwiyet for women aged 18 to 30, and Madam Wob Dwiyet for women aged 50 and over who pass the dress on. Read more
World Creole Music Festival (WCMF)
Dominica's great music gathering: three nights of Creole music in Roseau every October, during the Independence celebrations. Bouyon and cadence share the stage with zouk and kompa, making it the biggest home showcase for bouyon. Read more
Wrecketeng
The Dominican practice of rebuilding existing recordings over bouyon instrumentals, in the lineage of bouyon-muffin, pioneered by DJ Cut. The official spelling comes from him, and the reketeng form seen online is a misspelling. Read more
Zouk
The music of Guadeloupe and Martinique, popularized in the early 1980s by groups like Kassav: clean, danceable and often romantic. It is also one of the ingredients of bouyon, which Dominican musicians made faster and rawer. Read more

Frequently asked questions

What does bouyon mean?

Bouyon means soup in Kweyol, in Antillean Creole: a slow cooked dish that blends its ingredients. The name sums up the genre, which blends jing ping, cadence-lypso, chante mas and electronic production since its creation by WCK in 1988.

What is a bouyon riddim?

The instrumental over which several artists record their voices. One bouyon riddim can carry several tracks, as in dancehall culture, and riddims often carry a name, for example Denmath's Tropical Riddim.

How is Wrecketeng spelled?

Wrecketeng, with that exact spelling. It comes from DJ Cut, the pioneer of the style, according to the spelling rule followed by this site. The reketeng form, spread across the internet, is an error.
