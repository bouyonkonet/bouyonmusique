WCK: the band that created bouyon

Published September 8, 2026

WCK, short for Windward Caribbean Kulture, is the founding band of bouyon. Formed in Roseau, Dominica, in 1988, it created an entire genre by fusing cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines. Everything this site tells starts here, with WCK.

Who WCK are: a Roseau band with a Caribbean ambition

The band’s full name already states its project: Windward Caribbean Kulture, the culture of the Windward Islands. WCK formed in Roseau, the capital of Dominica, in 1988. That is not an administrative detail: Roseau is the centre of the island carnival, Mas Domnik, the home of the fetes, the bands and the crowds who would test, approve and carry the new music. A band born in Roseau is born at the heart of the Dominican party machine.

WCK belongs to a founding generation. Our dossier places it alongside other originating bands such as Roots, Stems and Branches, known as RSB, and First Serenade. Together, those groups belong to the moment when Dominica invented a new music, faster, more electronic and more direct than what came before it. But in that story WCK holds the reference place: it is the name you cite when you want to explain where bouyon comes from, how it sounds and why it carries that name.

Calling WCK an important band would therefore be an understatement. WCK is the reference point for this entire site. Bouyon Shattaz tells the genre starting from WCK and defends the Dominican origin credit. Every history page, every playlist and every artist profile on BouyonMusique.com leads back, one way or another, to that 1988 foundation. Without WCK there is no bouyon to tell, no bouyon gwada, no bouyon soca, and no second life for the genre in France and on social media.

That position explains the editorial choice behind this profile: it is not only about presenting a band, but about laying down the foundations of the genre. Understanding WCK means understanding the bouyon recipe, its instruments, its language, its audience and its journey, from Roseau to the French Antilles and on to France and the platforms of today.

The WCK sound: the fusion that changed everything

The creation of bouyon by WCK rests on a precise fusion. On one side stands cadence-lypso, the great Dominican dance music that preceded bouyon. On the other side stands jing ping, Dominica’s traditional dance music. WCK took those two inheritances and plugged them into technology that was new for the local scene at the time: electronic keyboards and drum machines. The result was neither a simple modernisation nor a copy of music from elsewhere. It was a new sound, born from Dominican materials.

Jing ping deserves a closer look, because it is the oldest root in the recipe. In jing ping, the accordion leads the dance. The lapo kabwit drums provide the beat, the boom-boom marks the bass and the bamboo flute brings the high melody that cuts through the fete. It is traditional dance music, built to move an entire village. When WCK met it with the machines, the accordion and the percussion did not disappear: they became the rhythmic and melodic memory of bouyon.

The electronic keyboards and drum machines changed the scale and the energy. The rhythm could become steadier, more insistent, more tailored to the fete and to carnival. The fusion gave bouyon its immediate character: music that lands fast, holds the dance and leaves the vocal a central place to call, answer and comment. It is that combination, Dominican tradition on one side and machines on the other, that sets bouyon apart from just another carnival music.

The genre name tells the same idea. Bouyon is the Creole word for soup or stew: everything mixed, nothing lost. Cadence-lypso, jing ping, keyboards, drum machines, English and Dominican Creole all end up in the same pot. The word is not a slogan invented afterwards to sell the genre. It describes its method of manufacture. To go further into that birth, read our page on the origins of bouyon and our history of bouyon.

The voices of bouyon: language, fete and commentary

Bouyon vocals mix English and Dominican Creole. That mix is not an accident and not a flaw: it is the real language of the fete in Dominica, the language of calls to the crowd, of choruses everyone sings back and of lines that stay in your head after carnival. Bouyon singing is direct and festive. It speaks to the crowd, lifts it again and makes it take part. A bouyon track does not settle for being listened to: it looks for an answer.

Those voices also carry, at times, social commentary. Bouyon can make people dance and say something in the same track. That double function, fete and speech, has been part of its identity since the founding era. It explains why the genre holds up so well at village fetes as well as at the big gatherings: it talks about shared life in the words of shared life, without ever leaving the dancefloor.

The way the voice is delivered matters as much as the language. The spoken, ragga-influenced delivery that bouyon developed would feed entire branches of the genre. Our historical dossier connects that delivery to the forms that followed, such as bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng, also called wrecketeng. In other words, the WCK voice and the early bouyon voice are not a closed chapter: they are still at work in Dominican styles today, including the ones closest to rap and sampling.

For a listener who discovers the genre through Guadeloupe or Martinique, this point is essential. Bouyon is not first of all an abstract production: it is a music of voices, calls and answers, in a Creole close to those of the French Antilles. That is one of the reasons why the crossing from one island to another felt so natural, as the rest of this profile explains.

The 1990s: bouyon dominates Dominica

In the 1990s, bouyon dominated Dominica’s music scene. It was not one presence among others: it was the island central music. It became the music of Roseau carnival, Mas Domnik, and of the village fetes. That double foothold, the capital and the villages, a great national gathering and local fetes, gave the genre a deep popular base. Bouyon was not confined to a studio or a city: it travelled everywhere Dominica partied.

Carnival played the role of both laboratory and consecration. Music that holds Mas Domnik has to hold the street, the crowd and the long haul. The bouyon of WCK and its generation passed that test and established itself as the sound of that moment. The village fetes extended the work for the rest of the year: every fete was a new listen, a new dance, a new transmission to listeners who would grow up with that sound as a given.

That 1990s dominance explains the solidity of the genre. When a music has been, for a decade, the soundtrack of an entire country, it does not vanish with a fashion. It becomes a repertoire, a memory and a reference that later generations can quote, revive and transform. Dominican artists today, from the fete generation to the social media generation, work in a landscape whose foundations were laid in that era.

That is also why the question of origin credit matters so much to Bouyon Shattaz. A genre that dominated its island, carried its carnival and its fetes, has an address. That address is Roseau, Dominica, and the name at the start of the story is WCK. Find the full context in our history of bouyon.

From Dominica to the French Antilles, then to France

Bouyon did not stop at Dominica’s shores. It crossed the sea to Guadeloupe and Martinique, where it was also known as jump up. That crossing was not a simple import: each scene worked the genre in its own way. From that circulation came, among others, bouyon gwada and bouyon soca, two branches that show bouyon’s ability to transform itself without losing its base energy.

Today the genre is living a second life in France and on social media. Audiences who have never set foot in Roseau discover bouyon through clips, dances, remixes and playlists. That second life is an opportunity: it gives the genre an audience the founding bands could never have reached from Dominica alone. But it also carries a risk, that of a music travelling faster than its own history. The further bouyon grows from home, the more important it becomes to recall where it was born.

That is exactly the work of this site and of Bouyon Shattaz. BouyonMusique.com tells bouyon in French and in English, for audiences in Guadeloupe, Martinique, France and beyond, but always starting from the Dominican origin. The site bouyon playlist plays the genre of today, while the history pages and artist profiles keep the full chain: WCK at the start, then the generations that followed, up to current Dominican artists.

WCK therefore remains essential listening, even for anyone who discovers the genre through its most recent forms. Going back to WCK is not a nostalgic detour. It is hearing the recipe in its founding form: cadence-lypso and jing ping meeting the machines, the Creole soup where everything mixes and nothing is lost.

Why WCK still matters in 2026

WCK matters in 2026 because bouyon matters, and because today bouyon still carries the mark of its creation. Every new branch, every remix, every carnival track that gets Roseau, Guadeloupe or a hall in France dancing works, knowingly or not, with ingredients assembled at the end of the 1980s. Naming WCK makes that debt visible.

WCK also matters as a landmark for listening well. Faced with the abundance of labels, shatta, bouyon gwada, bouyon soca, wrecketeng, returning to the founding band clarifies the differences: what comes from the original recipe, what comes from later transformations, what comes from other scenes. Our page on the origins of bouyon extends that comparative listening.

Finally, WCK matters for Dominica itself. Defending the Dominican credit for bouyon is not a quarrel over flags: it is defending the artists, bands and audiences who created a music that became international. Bouyon Shattaz does that work daily, on its social channels, in its playlists and in the pages of this site. This WCK profile is its first stone: everything starts in Roseau, in 1988, with Windward Caribbean Kulture.

Listening to WCK today: a guide for new ears

For a listener arriving in 2026, WCK is not always the first name encountered. Bouyon today is often discovered through a dance clip, a remix, a playlist or a younger Dominican artist. Coming back to WCK after those discoveries changes the listening: the keyboards, the call-and-response voices, the mix of English and Dominican Creole and the fete tempo stop looking like isolated effects and become what they are, the elements of a recipe assembled in Roseau in 1988 by Windward Caribbean Kulture and its founding generation.

Listen for the two roots first. On one side, the cadence-lypso inheritance, the great Dominican dance music that came before bouyon. On the other, the jing ping inheritance: think of the accordion leading the traditional dance, the lapo kabwit drums, the boom-boom and the bamboo flute, even when the sound you hear is carried by electronic keyboards and drum machines. Bouyon listening is comparative by nature: it asks what survived the fusion, what the machines changed, and why nothing was lost, as the Creole soup image promises.

Then listen for the journey. The sound forged for Mas Domnik and the village fetes of Dominica crossed to Guadeloupe and Martinique as jump up, fed branches such as bouyon gwada and bouyon soca, and now lives a second life in France and on social media. Our bouyon playlist plays that current life, while this profile and our history of bouyon keep the starting point in view. That double listening, the founders and the present, is the house method of Bouyon Shattaz: never enjoy the second life of bouyon at the price of forgetting its first address.

Frequently asked questions

Who created bouyon?

Bouyon was created by WCK, Windward Caribbean Kulture, a band formed in Roseau, Dominica, in 1988, by fusing cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines.

What does bouyon mean?

Bouyon is the Creole word for soup or stew: everything mixed, nothing lost. The name describes the fusion of the genre ingredients, from cadence-lypso to jing ping and the machines.

What is jing ping?

Jing ping is Dominica’s traditional dance music, led by the accordion, with lapo kabwit drums, the boom-boom and the bamboo flute. It is one of the two main roots of bouyon, alongside cadence-lypso.
