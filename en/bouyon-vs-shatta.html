Bouyon vs shatta: two genres, two islands

Published September 7, 2026

Bouyon and shatta are often set against each other like two generations. In reality, they are two distinct genres, shatta, a dancehall genre born in Martinique, and bouyon, born in Dominica, and shatta bouyon is their direct meeting point.

Bouyon, the historic genre

Created in 1988 in Dominica, bouyon has more than thirty years of history: live bands, carnivals, branches like bouyon soca and bouyon gwada. It is a genre with creators, classics and a clear territory of origin.

Shatta, the riddim generation

Shatta, a dancehall genre born in Martinique, is associated with today’s Antillean producers: short, hard riddims, made for dance and social media. When these producers work the bouyon material, Dominican keyboards, tempo and Creole, it is called shatta bouyon.

Which country created bouyon?

Dominica, in the Caribbean. Bouyon was created in 1988 in Roseau by the band WCK, which fused cadence-lypso and jing ping with electronic keyboards and drum machines. Bouyon therefore has more than thirty years of history, with its live bands, its carnivals and branches like bouyon soca and bouyon gwada.

What is the difference between bouyon and shatta?

They are two genres born on two different islands. Bouyon was born in 1988 in Dominica, created by the band WCK, with its live bands, its carnivals and its classics. Shatta is a dancehall subgenre born in Martinique, now associated with the short, hard riddims of Antillean producers, made for dance and social media. The two scenes often cross paths.

What is shatta bouyon?

Shatta bouyon is the direct meeting point between bouyon and shatta. The term is used when Antillean producers of the shatta generation work the bouyon material: Dominican keyboards, tempo and Creole, on short, hard riddims made for dance and social media. Even in this modern form, its roots remain Dominican.

Is shatta newer than bouyon?

Yes, in the timeline this site follows. Bouyon was created in 1988 in Dominica and has more than thirty years of history. Shatta, a dancehall genre born in Martinique, is presented here as the sound of today's generation of Antillean producers, the one of riddims made for dance and social media. Their fusion, shatta bouyon, is now the most visible form of bouyon on social media and in clubs.

Where does shatta come from?

Shatta comes from Martinique: it is a dancehall subgenre born in the French Antilles. This site describes it as the Martinique cousin of bouyon, part of the same sound territory without descending from it. Bouyon comes from neighboring Dominica, where the band WCK created it in Roseau in 1988, before it crossed the sea to Martinique and Guadeloupe.

Is shatta a branch of bouyon?

No. Shatta was born in Martinique in the wake of dancehall: it is not a branch of bouyon by birth. Bouyon's branches grew out of its own history, like Wrecketeng in Dominica in the 1990s, bouyon gwada between Dominica and Guadeloupe, or bouyon soca, launched in Dominica by Dada in 2003. Shatta bouyon is a meeting between two families.

Which producers work both bouyon and shatta?

Denmath is a documented example. A producer, sound engineer and artistic director from Saint Martin, based between Nantes and Paris, he lists bouyon, shatta, kompa, dancehall, soca and zouk among his specialties. His documented bouyon riddims are Tropical Riddim and Harley Riddim, both released in 2022.

How can you tell shatta bouyon from classic bouyon?

By the production. Shatta bouyon keeps the markers of the genre WCK created in 1988: fast tempo, looping keyboards, synth bass and Creole on the mic. But its production is digital, minimal, cut for the club and the phone, whereas classic bouyon comes from live bands and carnival. Producers often dance to their own beats themselves to launch the movement.

Is bouyon gwada a kind of shatta?

No. Bouyon gwada, also called hardcore bouyon, is a branch of bouyon born from collaborations between musicians from Dominica and Guadeloupe. Harder and more electronic than the bouyon of live bands, with vocals in Guadeloupean Creole, it bridges Dominican bouyon and modern forms like shatta bouyon, but it remains bouyon.
