The instruments of bouyon: from the traditional drum to the keyboard

By Smokiller · Published September 22, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

The bouyon sound comes from a meeting: the traditional instruments of Dominica, inherited from jing ping and the Creole dances, plugged into the machines of the 1980s, keyboards, synthesizers and drum machines.

On this page
  1. The traditional instruments
  2. The machines
  3. Frequently asked questions

The traditional instruments

The lapo kabwit, the goat-skin drum of chanté mas and carnival. The bele drum, at the heart of the dance of the same name. The chakchak, Creole maracas. The syak or gwaj, the scraper. The accordion, the voice of jing ping, with the bamboo flute and the boom-boom. These sounds are the soul of bouyon, and good producers still sample them.

The machines

WCK built bouyon on technology: keyboards up front, synthetic rhythmic patterns, electronic basses. Today, producers work on software, but they are looking for the same result: reproducing the pressure of a carnival band with machines. To go further, see the bouyon drum kit page.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I start with bouyon?

Start with the history of the genre, then with Bouyon Shattaz's bouyon playlist, the Bouyon Road Mix, which mixes Dominica carnival classics with new bouyon. To hear the instruments, start with the classics of the WCK era, where the lapo kabwit rhythms of carnival meet keyboard patterns and synthesizers, then compare them with today's riddims produced on software.

Which country created bouyon?

Dominica, in the Caribbean, in the late 1980s, with WCK, formed in Roseau in 1988, as the pioneering band. WCK built the genre on technology, with keyboards up front, synthetic rhythmic patterns and electronic basses, while keeping the soul of the island's traditional instruments.

What instruments are used in bouyon?

Bouyon combines two families. On one side, the traditional instruments of Dominica: lapo kabwit, bele drum, chakchak, syak or gwaj, accordion, bamboo flute and boom-boom. On the other, the machines of the 1980s: keyboards, synthesizers and drum machines, replaced today by producers' software.

What is the lapo kabwit?

The lapo kabwit is a goat-skin drum, the drum of chanté mas and Dominica's carnival. At Mas Domnik, whole bands play in lapo kabwit. It is one of the traditional sounds that form the soul of bouyon, and good producers still sample it.

How do producers make bouyon sound today?

Producers now work on software, but they are after the same result as WCK with its keyboards: reproducing the pressure of a carnival band with machines. Many still sample traditional instruments such as the lapo kabwit or the accordion. For drum sounds, see the bouyon drum kit page.

What is the bele drum?

The bele drum is the drum at the heart of the bèlè dance, one of Dominica's traditional dances inherited from the island's Creole history. Along with the lapo kabwit, the chakchak and the strikes of chanté mas, it is one of the Dominican percussion sounds a producer puts into a bouyon drum kit to give a track its identity.

What do the chakchak and the syak do in bouyon?

They are two percussion instruments of Dominican tradition: the chakchak is a Creole maraca, and the syak, also called gwaj, is a scraper. In the account of Cornell "Fingers" Phillip, WCK's founding keyboardist, the syak and the tambal of the traditional bands became the drum machine: bouyon's machines took over their rhythmic role.

What happened to the jing ping accordion in bouyon?

It became the keyboard. In jing ping, the accordion leads the music. Cornell "Fingers" Phillip, founding keyboardist of WCK, told Billboard that the accordion turned into the keyboard when the Phillip brothers began accompanying, on their own instruments, the traditional bands their brother Daryl recorded at the family home.

What is the boom-boom?

The boom-boom, also spelled boumboum, is the instrument that marks the bass in jing ping. According to Cornell "Fingers" Phillip, it is a hollowed-out bwa kan, and at the birth of bouyon it became the bass guitar. With the accordion, the bamboo flute and the lapo kabwit, it makes up the classic jing ping line-up, one of bouyon's two parents.

Why is the keyboard so important in bouyon?

Because bouyon is a music led by the keyboard. WCK built the genre with keyboards up front, and its founding keyboardist, Cornell Phillip, owes his nickname "Fingers" to his speed at the keys, according to DOM767. His rhythmic patterns replaced a whole jing ping accordion, and keyboard loops remain the signature of today's bouyon. See Cornell Phillip.

How do bouyon instruments differ from cadence-lypso instruments?

Cadence-lypso relied on acoustic instruments, with acoustic drums and an aggressive rhythm guitar. WCK's bouyon bet on technology: keyboard rhythm patterns, synthesizers, drum machines and electronic basses. From cadence, bouyon still kept the guitar heritage and the Creole storytelling. See the cadence-lypso page.

How fast do bouyon drum machines play?

Fast: tempo is part of bouyon's identity. There is no single figure, but the examples documented on this site sit around 160 BPM, such as Mauvais Plan by the producer HYPNO. That speed comes from the traditional dances and carnival, which the machines extend with fast percussion and keyboard loops. See bouyon BPM.

Where can you hear bouyon's traditional instruments in Dominica?

At Dominica's carnival, Mas Domnik, which has kept its old forms: chanté mas, lapo kabwit bands, traditional characters and sensays. Soca and calypso are heard there too, but bouyon has been the music of the Mas since the 1990s, and it is itself made from these traditions.
