Dada: the producer who started bouyon soca in 2003, Dominica

By Smokiller · Published October 20, 2026

Dada is a producer from Dominica whose name is four letters long and whose story rests, in large part, on one testimony. According to Smokiller, founder of Bouyon Shattaz and a direct participant in that era who was in the rooms with Dada and the Nursery Krew, it was Dada who coined the term bouyon soca and started the style in Dominica in 2003. This profile tells that signed origin story, sets it against what published reference works say, then follows the producer documented credits, from Triple Kay International to “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”.

Who Dada is: a producer, a short name, almost no archive

Dada is a Dominican producer. That is nearly everything the public archive says about him in a few lines, and that scarcity is itself a fact. Where the founding bands of bouyon, such as WCK, have a history documented album by album, the producer who worked in Roseau studios in the early 2000s left few written traces. His name travels in credits, in the memories of participants and on a handful of specialist pages, but he never received the chapter his role deserved.

This profile does not fill that gap with invention. This site record does not hold his full name, so no full name is printed here, pending direct confirmation. What the record does hold is first-hand: the testimony of a man who was there. Smokiller, a Dominican-born producer and a member of the Nursery Krew scene in that period, recounts Dada from the inside, as a studio colleague and not as a figure read about in a book.

That position gives this profile its particular shape. Part of what follows is attributed, signed testimony, presented as testimony. Another part comes from published sources any reader can check: the reference article on bouyon soca, an artist biography Dada published himself on a music showcase platform, Trinidad entertainment press, and the official credits of his biggest records. The two registers are never blended: when something is testimony, this profile says so; when something is a published credit, it names the credit.

Why publish a profile of a producer this poorly documented? Because bouyon soca became one of the most popular branches of bouyon, heard across the Caribbean and in the French Antilles, and its starting point deserves a name and an address. The name is Dada. The address is Dominica. The rest of this profile explains how this site can state that, and within what limits.

The testimony: 2003, Dominica, the Nursery Krew

The central fact of this profile is a testimony, and it is presented here exactly as what it is. Smokiller states that he was present in the rooms with Dada and the Nursery Krew when Dada coined the term bouyon soca and started the style, in Dominica, in 2003. This is not oral tradition collected years later from strangers: the witness is a participant in the scene in question, a producer of the same generation who himself recorded in the Nursery Krew Inc. circuit in the early 2000s.

The testimony also specifies how the style spread. According to Smokiller, the style Dada started in 2003 became popular when Triple Kay and other bands adopted it in their own version of bouyon. That detail matters, because it separates two moments that short histories often merge: the invention of a style inside a studio, and its adoption by the bands who carry it to carnival crowds. Dada belongs to the first moment. Triple Kay, a major band on the Dominican scene, belongs to the second, the moment of amplification.

The studio context helps explain what starting a style meant in 2003. Dominica was coming out of a decade dominated by live band bouyon. Around Nursery Krew Inc. and neighbouring circles, a generation of producers worked differently: on machines, on keyboards, with voices drawn from dancehall, hip hop and soca. That is the same ground that produced Wrecketeng, the branch built on sampled melodies and rap vocals. Dada bouyon soca was born in that laboratory, not in a marketing office on another island.

This testimony is already carried, signed, on this site bouyon soca page. This profile stands on it under the same rule: a direct participant testimony counts as a primary source on what he lived, and it is published under its author name so every reader can judge his position. Smokiller is not a neutral observer of this history, he is an actor in it. That is precisely why his account carries weight, and precisely why it is signed rather than disguised as anonymous truth.

What the references say: two stories that do not agree

Published reference works do not tell this origin in one voice, and the disagreement can be read in the texts themselves. One widely copied text, the article on the music of Dominica, describes the term bouyon soca as coined by non-Dominican producers and musicians, mainly from Saint Lucia, who blend Trinidad soca with Dominica bouyon. In that story, Dominica supplies the raw material, but the label and the fusion are born elsewhere. It is that outside story that Smokiller testimony directly contests.

Yet the reference article devoted to bouyon soca itself does not say exactly the same thing. Its summary box places the style cultural origins in the early 2000s, in Dominica. Its text presents the fusion as a genre originating from Dominica, names producer Dada among the makers who made the style more popular across the Caribbean, alongside the artist ASA, and holds up “Famalay” and “Conch Shell” as noticeable hits of the genre. One section of the same article even attributes the term to Dominican producers and musicians. In other words, the most read reference hesitates between two geographies, and one of them meets the testimony published here.

This site decides, and it says how. Between a summary text written from outside, itself copied from article to article, and the signed testimony of a participant who was in the room, Bouyon Shattaz stands on the testimony for the fact of origin: Dada, Dominica, 2003. That does not erase the rest of the published picture. Everyone agrees on what followed: the style then also grew in Saint Lucia and Antigua, it became very popular in Guadeloupe and Martinique, where audiences know bouyon as jump up, and its scenes today cover the Virgin Islands, Saint Vincent and Trinidad and Tobago.

It is also worth hearing what the debate says about Dominica itself. The same references stress that in Dominica, bouyon always kept a clear, recognisable style, distinct from soca, and that the fusion was less claimed there than on neighbouring islands. A style can therefore be born in a Dominican studio, be adopted by Triple Kay, then be named and celebrated more loudly abroad than at home. That is exactly the kind of story in which origin credit gets lost, and exactly the kind of story this site exists to document. Read the full account on our history of bouyon page.

Bouyon soca: the fusion Dada started

Bouyon soca is the fusion of Dominica bouyon and the soca of Trinidad and Tobago. On the bouyon side: the rhythms of the 1990s, the fast tempo, the looping keyboard patterns, the heavy synth bass and the voice that calls the crowd the way it does at Roseau carnival. On the soca side: the festive structure built for fetes, parades and choruses sung back by a crowd. Mixed together, they produce a sound that speaks at once to Dominicans and to soca audiences across the English speaking Caribbean. The style is also referred to, in some sources, as jump up soca.

Understanding that fusion helps explain the 2003 move. The point was not to dilute bouyon inside a foreign music, but to plug the Dominican rhythm machine into the most exportable party form in the Caribbean. A studio producer, working on keyboards and machines, was the person best placed to make that join: bouyon soca is a producer music before it is a band music, and its history logically begins behind a console rather than on a stage.

The published sequel confirms the scale the fusion reached. Saint Lucia developed its own fusion experiments from the early 2000s, Antigua worked it in its own way, and the French Antilles adopted it on a large scale. The records the references themselves hold up as emblems of the genre, “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”, are Dada productions, which connects the origin told by testimony to the consecration told by credits: the same producer stands at both ends of the chain, at the start of the style in 2003 and at its biggest Caribbean successes at the end of the 2010s.

To hear the branch today, our bouyon soca page details its ingredients and its territories, and our bouyon playlist mixes bouyon, bouyon soca and new Dominican releases. Dada does not appear there as a name from the past: he is the documented starting point of the most travelled branch of bouyon.

The documented productions: Triple Kay, Nursery Krew Inc, then the big records

Beyond testimony, Dada published credits draw a coherent path. His artist biography, published on the Showcase Your Music platform, shows him formed in the circle of the Bushtown Clan, the dancehall, soca, hip hop and bouyon group of his youth, then moving into production at fifteen, on drum machines, keyboards and above all on computer with FL Studio. The same biography lists among his production mentors Cornel “Fingers” Phillip, the founding keyboardist of WCK. That lineage, published by the producer himself, places Dada in the direct line of the keyboards of original bouyon.

Again according to that artist biography, Dada worked with Triple Kay International, for whom he produced tracks such as “All Out”, “Do You Believe” and “Doh Blow My High”, and co-produced the party mix “The After Dust” on the Triple Kay album “All Out”, released in 2006. The same source says he then brought out the group Nursery Krew Inc., with tracks such as “Peanuts” and “Calaloo”, all produced by Dada. Those published credits meet the testimony on one precise point: the connection between Dada, the Nursery Krew and Triple Kay is not a reconstruction by this site, it also appears in the account the producer published of his own path.

Regional consecration arrived at the end of the 2010s, and this time it is documented by the press and by official song credits. The Trinidad site Ebuzztt presents Dada as the producer behind “Famalay”, the hit performed by Machel Montano, Skinny Fabulous and Bunji Garlin, the 2019 Road March title of Trinidad and Tobago. The record official credits name DadaMuzic and DSM as producers, and DadaMuzic as mixer. Ebuzztt also credits him with producing “Conch Shell”, the collaboration of Machel Montano, Skinny Fabulous and Iwer George, whose official credits name Dadamanufakture as producer and state that Skinny Fabulous was recorded at Dadamanufakture, in Dominica.

The same Ebuzztt article documents the method Dada claims for his “Smoke Riddim”: a hybrid riddim blending soca and Dominica bouyon, given to Bunji Garlin for the track “Forward” and to Shurwayne Winchester for “Wet Dem Down”, two artists chosen, Dada explains, for their ability to carry big fetes. The article adds, for the same season, a production for Patrice Roberts, the track “Look Meh Friend”, and a mix engineer credit on Nadia Batson “Market”. From 2003 to those credits, the line reads clearly: the bouyon soca producer never stopped working the join between the two genres, in both directions.

Why Dada matters, and what this profile does not yet know

Dada matters because bouyon soca matters. The branch he started in 2003, according to the signed testimony published here, became one of the most popular forms of bouyon, a passport for the Dominican sound to soca audiences across the Caribbean and to the French Antilles. Without that start, the history of the genre would read differently: bouyon would have stayed longer a matter of live bands and local carnival, instead of also becoming a music of producers, riddims and collaborations between islands.

Dada also matters as a case study in the problem Bouyon Shattaz documents across this site: credit. A studio producer leaves fewer traces than a stage singer. When references tell his invention from outside, with a hesitant geography, his name can vanish from his own history. This profile exists to prevent that, with the honest means available: a participant testimony, signed, and published credits that can be checked record by record.

Finally, this profile states its limits, because they are part of the record. Dada full name does not appear in this site record and is therefore not printed here. The exact title of the first bouyon soca track or tracks of 2003 is not documented in our sources, so no title is invented to fill that blank. No verified official account of the producer was confirmed during the research for this profile, so this page links to no social profile. If those elements are confirmed, this profile will be completed, dated and corrected like every page on this site.

In the meantime, listening starts at the end of the story: “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”, the two records the references themselves hold up as emblems of bouyon soca, then back to the source, our bouyon soca page and the bouyon playlist. Between the two stands the path of a Roseau producer the books nearly told without him.

Frequently asked questions

Who started bouyon soca, and in what year?

According to the first-hand testimony of Smokiller, a Dominican-born producer who was present in the rooms with Dada and the Nursery Krew, bouyon soca was coined and started by Dada in Dominica in 2003. The style then became popular when Triple Kay and other bands adopted it in their own version of bouyon.

Why do reference works tell a different origin story?

Because the published references do not agree with each other. One widely copied text describes the term as coined by non-Dominican producers, mainly from Saint Lucia, while the article devoted to bouyon soca places its cultural origins in Dominica in the early 2000s and names producer Dada among those who popularised the style. This site stands on the signed testimony of a direct participant for the fact of origin, and presents it as testimony.

Did Dada produce “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”?

Yes, according to the press and official credits. Ebuzztt presents Dada as the producer behind “Famalay”, by Machel Montano, Skinny Fabulous and Bunji Garlin, the 2019 Road March title of Trinidad and Tobago, whose credits name DadaMuzic and DSM as producers. For “Conch Shell”, by Skinny Fabulous, Machel Montano and Iwer George, the credits name Dadamanufakture as producer.

What is the link between Dada, the Nursery Krew and Triple Kay?

Smokiller testimony places the birth of bouyon soca in 2003 in the rooms shared with Dada and the Nursery Krew, and its spread in the adoption of the style by Triple Kay. The artist biography Dada published points the same way: it credits him with productions for Triple Kay International and with bringing out Nursery Krew Inc., with tracks such as “Peanuts” and “Calaloo”.

What is Dada's “Smoke Riddim”?

According to Ebuzztt, the “Smoke Riddim” is a hybrid riddim by Dada blending soca and Dominica bouyon. Bunji Garlin sings “Forward” on it and Shurwayne Winchester delivers “Wet Dem Down”. Dada explained that he chose those two artists for their ability to carry big fetes and bring back the energy of fetes as they used to be.

Who is Dada?

His published credits link him to Triple Kay International, to Nursery Krew Inc, then to “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”, the two records the references hold up as emblems of bouyon soca.

Where is the producer Dada from?

Dada is from Dominica: he is a Dominican producer who worked in Roseau studios in the early 2000s, in the Nursery Krew circle. The official credits of “Conch Shell” keep a trace of that address: Skinny Fabulous was recorded there at Dadamanufakture, in Dominica. This site does not print his full name, pending direct confirmation.

What do DadaMuzic and Dadamanufakture mean in the credits?

They are the two names under which Dada appears in the official credits of his biggest records. On “Famalay”, DadaMuzic is credited as producer with DSM, and alone as mixer. On “Conch Shell”, Dadamanufakture is credited as producer, and the credits state that Skinny Fabulous was recorded at Dadamanufakture, in Dominica. Both point to the same producer.

Which songs did Dada produce for Triple Kay International?

According to the artist biography Dada published on Showcase Your Music, he produced tracks such as “All Out”, “Do You Believe” and “Doh Blow My High” for Triple Kay International, and co-produced the party mix “The After Dust” on the band's album “All Out”, released in 2006. This site cites that self published biography with attribution.

Who taught Dada to produce?

According to his self published artist biography, Dada was formed in the circle of the Bushtown Clan, the dancehall, soca, hip hop and bouyon group of his youth, then moved into production at fifteen, on drum machines, keyboards and above all on computer with FL Studio. The same biography names among his mentors Cornel “Fingers” Phillip, the founding keyboardist of WCK.

Which soca artists has Dada worked with?

Beyond “Famalay” and “Conch Shell”, Ebuzztt documents Dada's “Smoke Riddim”, given to Bunji Garlin for “Forward” and to Shurwayne Winchester for “Wet Dem Down”. The same article credits him, for the same season, with producing “Look Meh Friend” for Patrice Roberts and with a mix engineer credit on Nadia Batson's “Market”.

What is the difference between bouyon and the bouyon soca Dada started?

Bouyon is the genre born in Dominica, with its fast tempo, looping keyboard patterns and heavy synth bass. Bouyon soca, started by Dada in 2003 according to Smokiller's testimony, plugs that rhythm engine into the festive structure of Trinidad and Tobago soca. In Dominica, bouyon always kept a style distinct from soca. See bouyon soca.

Why is bouyon soca also called jump up?

Some sources refer to the style as jump up soca. In Guadeloupe and Martinique, where bouyon soca became very popular, audiences also know bouyon as jump up. These names describe the same family of sounds, whose origin remains Dominica: the style was started by Dada in 2003, according to the Smokiller testimony published on this site.

Where did Dada's bouyon soca spread?

After its start in Dominica in 2003 and its adoption by Triple Kay, bouyon soca also grew in Saint Lucia and Antigua, then became very popular in Guadeloupe and Martinique. Its scenes today cover the Virgin Islands, Saint Vincent and Trinidad and Tobago, where “Famalay”, produced by Dada, was the 2019 Road March title. See the history of bouyon.
