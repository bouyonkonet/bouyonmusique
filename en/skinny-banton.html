Skinny Banton: the ragga voice that opened bouyon-muffin

Published October 20, 2026

Skinny Banton is the name the sources keep as the key figure of bouyon-muffin, the branch of bouyon that sets raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall vocals over Dominican rhythms. According to Wikipedia, in English and in French, he collaborated with WCK from 1995, with a ragga influenced vocal style, and his track “Party”, with Joanne and DJ Cut of Bucktown Sounds, is among the recordings that led toward Wrecketeng. He is later known as Shadowflow. This profile follows the sources step by step, and separates him clearly from the soca artist of the same name.

Who Skinny Banton is: the figure of bouyon-muffin

Skinny Banton belongs to the second wave of bouyon history. The genre was born in Dominica in the late 1980s with WCK, a band, a live formation, keyboards and machines plugged into cadence-lypso and jing ping. Skinny Banton arrived through the voice. Older versions of the English Wikipedia page on bouyon present him without detour as the most influential figure in the development of bouyon-muffin, and the French version carries the same reading: the most influential figure in the development of this style is him.

This profile attributes that stature the way the sources give it: a judgment carried by Wikipedia, in both its language versions, not an official title. But the consistency of the judgment matters. From one version of the page to another, from one language to the other, the name that returns when bouyon-muffin has to be embodied by one voice is Skinny Banton. No other voice is named in that place in the sources consulted for this page.

His public identity holds in two names. The first is Skinny Banton, the name of the bouyon-muffin era and the WCK collaboration. The second is Shadowflow, the name he is known by later, according to Wikipedia. The English Wikipedia page on WCK also gives his full name in its former members list: Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, also known as “Shadowflow”, on lead vocals. This profile uses those elements as they are, attributed to their sources, without adding a biography the public record does not contain.

It also states what this profile does not know, because no verified source provides it: no date of birth, no published complete discography, and no verified official platform accounts for this specific artist. The streaming pages under the name Skinny Banton belong to another artist, a soca singer, and the last section of this page explains that confusion in detail. Here, the subject is only the Dominican voice of bouyon-muffin.

There is also a matter of scale. Bouyon is a genre from a small island, and its branches were often carried by a handful of people. That a single voice can be named as the key figure of a whole branch is not unusual in this history: the same record names single pioneers for the sampling branch and single producers for later revivals. Skinny Banton’s place follows that pattern, one voice standing for a turn the whole genre took.

What bouyon-muffin is, and how it differs from classic bouyon

Bouyon-muffin is a branch of bouyon that fuses the Dominican rhythm with vocals from elsewhere: Jamaican raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall. The definition in the sources is stable: the English Wikipedia page describes an offshoot of bouyon incorporating elements of Jamaican raggamuffin music, hip hop and dancehall, developed in the 1990s as Dominican musicians began experimenting with ragga influenced vocals over traditional bouyon rhythms. The Air Caraibes page on the music of Guadeloupe independently describes bouyon-muffin as the variant incorporating hip hop and ragga elements.

The difference from classic bouyon sits first in the place of the voice. The bouyon of the founding bands is a formation music: musicians, keyboards, a rhythm section, singers carrying the fete in Creole and English. Bouyon-muffin keeps the bouyon rhythm base, fast and built for dancing, but it changes how the voice is laid over it: less singing, more toasting, rhythmic speech, the voice cut up like a dancehall deejay or a rapper, with the accent and attitude of raggamuffin.

The second difference is the openness to recordings. Bouyon-muffin lives in the same world as sound systems and DJs: it is a music of voices set over riddims, and that is why it works as a bridge. The sources say it explicitly: the bouyon-muffin experiments inspired artists such as the Bushtown Clan to incorporate more hip hop and dancehall, leading to the next style, Wrecketeng, the sampling and reconstruction branch. Bouyon-muffin is the direct parent of Wrecketeng.

The third difference is therefore genealogical. Classic bouyon descends from cadence-lypso and jing ping; bouyon-muffin descends from classic bouyon, but it looks toward Jamaica and toward American hip hop. It is the first branch of the genre to make the imported voice its main ingredient. To hear bouyon across its branches today, our bouyon playlist gives the panorama, and our history of bouyon places bouyon-muffin in the full chronology.

General descriptions of bouyon confirm this vocal shift. Older versions of the bouyon soca page, drawn from world music encyclopedias, note that bouyon involves chanting rather than singing and is strongly influenced by the dancehall, reggae and rap language style coming out of Jamaica, adding that bouyon-muffin and the sampling branch are offshoots of that tendency. In other words, the vocal tendency Skinny Banton embodies is no accident: it is written into bouyon in its oldest descriptions.

From 1995: the collaboration with WCK

The dated marker in the record is clear: from 1995, Skinny Banton collaborated with the WCK band. The wording comes from Wikipedia, in French as in English: from 1995, he collaborated with the founding band of bouyon, using a ragga influenced chant to toast over bouyon rhythms. The current version of the English page adds the overall reading: his collaborations with WCK helped introduce a stronger dancehall vocal influence into bouyon.

The collaboration carries particular weight because WCK is not one band among others: it is the band that created bouyon, formed in Roseau in 1988. When Skinny Banton’s ragga voice enters the orbit of the founding band, the fusion does not stay a side experiment: it touches the centre of the genre. Bouyon-muffin does not develop against classic bouyon, it develops with its creator band, over its rhythms, in front of its public.

The former members list published on the WCK page confirms the link from the inside. Among the band’s former members, the page names Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, also known as “Shadowflow”, on lead vocals, alongside figures such as Derick “Rah” Peters and Cornell “Fingaz” Phillip. Skinny Banton therefore does not appear only as a passing outside collaborator: in the band’s published record, he figures among the voices who were part of WCK.

What the sources do not give, this profile does not invent: no specific title from the 1995 collaboration is named in the sources consulted, no WCK album is designated as the home of that collaboration, and no end year is published. The verified fact is the collaboration itself, its 1995 starting point, its ragga vocal nature, and Skinny Banton’s presence in the former members list of WCK. That is already a solid marker in a history often told from memory.

The year 1995 is not just any year at WCK. According to the band page, it is the year of Tou Cho Tou Flam, presented as its most successful album, with seven hits out of eleven tracks, including “Balance Batty”, still popular today, and the band then toured the United States, Canada, Europe and the Caribbean between 1995 and 1998. Skinny Banton’s collaboration therefore begins at the peak of the founding band, at the exact moment Dominican bouyon was exporting itself.

“Party” with Joanne and DJ Cut: the track that bridges

Only one track is named in the sources to embody Skinny Banton’s bouyon-muffin moment: “Party”, with Joanne and DJ Cut, of Bucktown Sounds. Older versions of the English Wikipedia page cite it as the example of the style, and the current version keeps it: a recording combining elements of bouyon with dancehall style deejaying, with Joanne as a featured voice and DJ Cut, the future pioneer of Wrecketeng, already present in the equation.

This track matters less as a documented hit, no consulted source publishes its year or its numbers, than as a knot in the story. Three threads cross in it: the bouyon-muffin voice of Skinny Banton, a featured voice, Joanne, about whom the sources give no further detail, and DJ Cut, the Bucktown Sounds man who would open the next branch. Bouyon-muffin and Wrecketeng are not two separate chapters: they touch inside this track, through the people.

What followed is told by the sources as a transmission. According to older Wikipedia versions, repeated in French, songs like “Party” gave the products of bouyon-muffin, such as the Bushtown Clan, further inspiration to incorporate more hip hop and dancehall, until the style later called Wrecketeng was created. The current version phrases the same lineage with care: these experiments contributed to the emergence of other styles, including Wrecketeng.

One spelling point, because it runs through this whole history: older references write the next branch with the spelling “reketeng”, the widespread internet misspelling. The official spelling used by this site is Wrecketeng, which comes from DJ Cut himself. This page therefore points to the branch under the name Wrecketeng, and the old spelling is cited here only to flag the widespread error. The link that follows Skinny Banton in the lineage is read on our Wrecketeng page, then with Nursery Krew Inc, the crew that took over in the early 2000s.

From Skinny Banton to Shadowflow: what the record says, and what it does not

According to Wikipedia, Skinny Banton is later known as Shadowflow. The current English page writes “later known as Shadowflow”, and the French page says the same: known today under the name Shadowflow. The former members list of WCK gathers both names in a single line: Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, also known as “Shadowflow”. The sources therefore converge on the identity: one artist, two names, the second succeeding the first.

The sources consulted, on the other hand, do not document the Shadowflow period: no title, no project and no date is published in the record gathered for this page under that name. This profile therefore refuses to narrate a second career that the sources do not narrate. What is established is Skinny Banton’s role in the bouyon-muffin of the 1990s, and the fact that he is later known as Shadowflow. The rest belongs to a documentation still to be done, with interviews and archives.

That restraint is part of this site’s mission. Bouyon Shattaz documents bouyon from its Dominican origin, figure by figure, with verified facts and named sources. For the pioneers of the 1990s, the public record is often a few lines inside general pages, as it is here. Writing the profile anyway, saying exactly what is known and what is not, is worth more than silence: it is how a name like Skinny Banton stays findable, correctly spelled and correctly credited.

His case also shows how this site handles names that changed. The profile is filed under Skinny Banton, the name of the documented work, and Shadowflow is carried throughout as the later name, so a reader searching either name lands on the same verified record. Filing it the other way round, under the later name with no documented titles, would bury the bouyon-muffin work that makes the artist matter to this history.

To place Skinny Banton in the full scene, his era is the passage from bands to voices: the bouyon of the 1990s, dominated by formations like WCK and the bands that followed, begins to make room for individual voices coming from dancehall and hip hop. The next generation, the studio crews like Nursery Krew Inc and the producers, inherits that passage. And the modern voice of the genre, that of artists like Asa Bantan, widely called the King of Bouyon, works in a landscape that this opening helped to create.

Two artists called Skinny Banton: the confusion to avoid, and why ours matters

There is another artist called Skinny Banton, and confusing the two distorts everything. The other Skinny Banton is a soca artist: the sources documenting him, including his artist page on NTS and the streaming databases, give his name as Shirlan George, born in Trinidad and residing in Carriacou, Grenada. His titles, his credits and his platform pages belong to the soca scene of Trinidad and Grenada. He is not the artist on this page: nothing in his record links him to WCK, to bouyon-muffin or to the Dominica of the 1990s.

The Skinny Banton of this page is identified by the bouyon sources: Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson according to the former members list of WCK, collaborator of the band from 1995, voice of bouyon-muffin, later known as Shadowflow. Two artists, two different full names, two islands, two neighbouring but distinct genres, Dominican bouyon on one side, the soca of Trinidad and Grenada on the other. The shared stage name is a fact, and this profile states it honestly instead of letting the reader merge the two discographies.

That vigilance has a practical consequence for this site: no streaming page and no social media account under the name Skinny Banton is linked from this profile, because none could be verified as belonging to the bouyon-muffin artist. The links a reader finds in search results under this name lead, for the most part, to the soca artist. Rather than a wrong link, this profile prefers no link, and says so.

Why Skinny Banton matters, in the end, comes down to his position in the chain. He is the voice through which raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall entered bouyon durably, from 1995, with the founding band itself. Without that passage, the Wrecketeng of the DJs and the crews has no vocal parent, and today’s bouyon loses one of its explanatory links. His page is short on facts, because the record is thin, but the central fact is large: bouyon-muffin has a name, and that name is Skinny Banton.

Search engines make the confusion worse, because the soca artist is the one with a current streaming catalogue under the name. A reader who types Skinny Banton today is served the Trinidad and Grenada record first. This page exists so that the Dominican record is also on the shelf, with its sources showing, and so that the bouyon-muffin chapter of the 1990s keeps its own name attached to its own artist.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Skinny Banton?

Skinny Banton is the key figure of bouyon-muffin according to Wikipedia, in English and in French: an artist who, from 1995, collaborated with WCK, setting a ragga influenced chant over bouyon rhythms. The former members list of WCK gives his full name: Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, on lead vocals. He is later known as Shadowflow.

What is bouyon-muffin?

Bouyon-muffin is a branch of bouyon that incorporates elements of Jamaican raggamuffin, hip hop and dancehall. It developed in the 1990s, when Dominican musicians experimented with ragga influenced vocals over bouyon rhythms. Air Caraibes also describes it as the bouyon variant incorporating hip hop and ragga elements.

How does bouyon-muffin differ from classic bouyon?

Classic bouyon is the music of the founding bands like WCK: live formations, keyboards and machines over a cadence-lypso and jing ping base. Bouyon-muffin keeps the bouyon rhythm base, but changes the voice: toasting and rhythmic speech, in the manner of raggamuffin and dancehall, instead of band singing. It is also the branch that bridges toward the DJs’ Wrecketeng.

Which track connects Skinny Banton to DJ Cut?

The track “Party”, with Joanne and DJ Cut of Bucktown Sounds. According to Wikipedia, that recording combined bouyon with dancehall style deejaying, and experiments of that kind inspired artists such as the Bushtown Clan to push hip hop and dancehall into bouyon, which led to Wrecketeng. No consulted source publishes a release year for “Party”.

Are Skinny Banton and Shadowflow the same person?

Yes, according to the sources. Wikipedia states that Skinny Banton is later known as Shadowflow, and the former members list of WCK gathers both names in one line: Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, also known as “Shadowflow”. The consulted sources, however, document no title released under the Shadowflow name.

Is Skinny Banton the soca artist from Trinidad and Grenada?

No. There is another artist called Skinny Banton: a soca singer whose sources give his name as Shirlan George, born in Trinidad and residing in Carriacou, Grenada. He is a different artist. The Skinny Banton of this page is the Dominican bouyon-muffin voice, linked to WCK from 1995 and later known as Shadowflow. The two artists share a stage name, and nothing more in the verified record.
