How to make a bouyon beat, step by step

By Smokiller · Published September 4, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

How to make a bouyon beat? Not by speeding up a dancehall beat: the job is to rebuild, with today’s tools, the mechanics of a Dominican carnival band, with a percussion grid that pushes, a keyboard loop that never lets go, a heavy and simple bass and room for the crowd. This guide follows the work in order, from the first decision to sharing the finished beat.

How to make a bouyon beat: start from the WCK recipe

Bouyon was created in Roseau, Dominica, in 1988 by WCK, who plugged cadence-lypso and jing ping into electronic keyboards and drum machines. Jing ping supplies the genre’s memory: the accordion leads the dance, the lapo kabwit gives the beat, the boom-boom marks the bass and the bamboo flute cuts across the fete. Producers now work in software, but they are after the same result as WCK: reproducing the pressure of a carnival band with machines.

The second decision comes before the first note: what the beat is for. A riddim for several voices, an instrumental for DJs and dancers, a type beat, the flip of an existing song or a custom production: the foundation is shared, but the destination decides the structure, the space left for the voice and the way the beat is released. Steps 1 to 4 cover that foundation; the sections after them take the destinations.

Choose the tempo: speed as a signature

Bouyon is a fast genre, and its tempo is part of its identity. There is no single figure, but the examples documented on this site sit around 160 BPM: HYPNO’s Mauvais Plan is built at 160 BPM, Shazam lists Billy Bybf’s Bombe Atomique at 160 BPM, and three recent Triple Kay singles show the same tempo there. The bouyon BPM page explains that speed: traditional Dominican dances, carnival and the competition between bands push towards maximum energy.

So set the tempo before anything else. At 160 BPM, the loop has to hold the speed without collapsing, with readable percussion and breathing space in the right places. But speed alone does not make bouyon: without the percussion and the spirit of Dominica it is only a fast beat, and that is what separates a bouyon beat from a dancehall beat pushed faster.

Step 1: the rhythm first

Start with the rhythmic grid: percussion inspired by lapo kabwit and jing ping, fast and steady drums, build-ups that imitate the calls of carnival. Work the loop until it makes you want to move with no vocal on it at all.

The choice of the sounds themselves, sampled traditional percussion, kicks, snares and hats, is covered on the bouyon drum kit page: this guide deals with how to put them together. Three markers matter. Regularity first: with drum machines, WCK made the rhythm more regular, more insistent, more cut out for the fete. Build-ups next: they play the role of a carnival band’s calls. Identity last: a generic kit makes a fast beat, a kit fed with jing ping and lapo kabwit makes bouyon.

The test for this step, which the bouyon instrumental page applies to the finished track: if the grid alone, with no keyboard and no voice, does not make people move, no extra layer will save it.

Step 2: the keyboard loop and the bass

The keyboard carries the melodic signature: a short, repetitive, almost hypnotic motif, inherited from the WCK method, which put the keyboards up front. It has stayed the genre’s signature instrument: in Triple Kay, one of Dominica’s most enduring bouyon bands, it is the lead keyboardist, Killa Laurent, who produces all of the band’s music.

Repetition is not a flaw: the ear must be able to enter the track at any second and know where it is. To give the motif a Dominican colour, the bouyon type beat page advises citing the sources without copying them: an accordion or keyboard melody in the jing ping way.

The synth bass follows, heavy and simple: an engine, not a soloist. In jing ping, the boom-boom marks the bass; in WCK’s bouyon, electronic basses took over. Finally add background vocals, shouts and responses, to recreate the crowd. In Creole, they set up the language of bouyon, the mix of English and Dominican Creole that is the real language of the fete.

Step 3: arrange for the road and for the dancefloor

A bouyon beat has two grounds. The first is the road: the Roseau carnival, Mas Domnik, and its J’ouvert, where from 4 in the morning the sound systems and bands play the year’s bouyon. Music that holds Mas Domnik has to hold the street, the crowd and the distance. The stakes are competitive: Triple Kay presents Heat as the first People’s Bouyon Road March, in 2019, and CaribMagPlus reported in March 2025 the victory of RAGS at Dominica’s first Bouyon Road March.

For the road, the arrangement favours continuity: little intro, a main loop that arrives early, drums that follow the carnival model with regular build-ups, and an energy that almost never drops. The voice must be able to shout, sing and make the crowd answer: leave it room for calls and responses.

The second ground is the dancefloor and the screen, where formats are shorter: Billy Bybf’s album DI YO SA 2 lines up tracks between a minute and a half and under three minutes, formats built for the dancefloor and social media that have become the norm of 2020s bouyon gwada. Every passage then has to work on its own, since a video clip can start anywhere. Both versions share the loop and the tempo; what changes is the length and the order of the sections.

Step 4: test it dancing

The final test of bouyon is physical: dance to it, or watch someone dance. If the body does not follow, the beat is not finished. That is why so many producers launch their riddims in dance videos before the official release.

MB On The Track gives the most direct example: on October 16, 2026, he posted a reel of himself dancing, at night, in a bus shelter, to his own bouyon instrumental, with the phrase Rock Bouyon on screen. The comments asked for the track and a long version: the beat passed the test in front of an audience before any release.

The test also checks that every part of the beat does its job. The bouyon dance page describes the moves the genre calls for: swaying on the choruses, collective jumps on the build-ups, danced answers to the calls of the singer or the DJ. If the build-ups do not make people jump and the calls get no answer, that is where the beat needs more work.

Riddim, instrumental or type beat: choose the destination

The riddim is the instrumental rhythm several artists voice over, a system that came from Jamaica and that Dominica made its own. Bouyon riddims often carry a name and become trademarks: Denmath released Tropical Riddim in summer 2022 and Harley Riddim the same year. A riddim is a complete rhythmic proposal, with its rises, its breaks and open spaces for singers the producer does not know yet. On Triple Kay’s Whistle While You Work, Killa Laurent is credited as riddim producer.

The instrumental is that riddim without the voice: the loop, the bass and the percussion in their pure form. It has to hold a whole track with no singer: it is the format of dance videos, battles and DJ blends. It is also a showcase: MB On The Track publishes his beats under titles such as “Cigarettes” Bouyon Instrumental 2026 (Prod By MB On The Track), which announce the genre, the year and the producer credit.

The type beat is an instrumental built on bouyon’s codes in order to be placed: beatmakers look for it to lay bouyon, shatta and dancehall vocals. That leaves the custom beat, made for one specific voice. In Dcamp & Lewismelo, Lewis produces the duo’s riddims, built with the voices rather than bought from outside; MB On The Track offers custom productions by direct message, where the artist discusses tempo, key and intended use.

Flip an existing song: the Wrecketeng method

Wrecketeng, the Dominican style built on sampled melodies and rap vocals, established this method: take existing melodies, sample them and lay voices over them. The melody can come from elsewhere, but the treatment, the tempo and the voice make it Dominican. The official spelling, Wrecketeng, comes from DJ Cut, the founding member who pioneered the style.

Smokiller, a Dominican-born producer and the founder of Bouyon Shattaz, works explicitly in that style. His method changes three elements: the instrumentation, carried by bouyon keyboards; the speed, set to the tempo of the Dominican fete; the energy, that of a music built to make a crowd answer. The remixer keeps the voice and the emotion of the original but rebuilds the whole floor: a rhythmic grid drawn from jing ping and lapo kabwit, a fast synth bass, carnival band build-ups.

The success test: if the audience recognises the original hit but dances to another machine, the flip has worked. Smokiller has applied it to Coi Leray’s Nothing Into Something and Players, Shenseea’s Vulnerability and Ayra Starr’s Heaven Baby, and he has also produced an original track, Klass Money’s Throw Dat (Bouyon 2026). RnBouyon applies the same method to the R&B repertoire; the bouyon remix page details these examples.

Finish, protect and share the beat

Bouyon today is often finished in public. Savion works in snippets: a few seconds of unreleased material as a short video, then the reactions in the comments steer the decision: the snippet becomes a full track, stays in the workshop or gets reworked. HYPNO shows the tempo from the reel itself, mauvais plan 160bpm: announcing your BPM has become a selling point.

Mixing and mastering are a craft of their own, often credited separately: on Whistle While You Work, they are signed by Cornel Phillip. For DJs and radio, the working format is WAV: Smokiller offers several of his remixes as free WAV downloads on smokiller.com, and the rotation packs Bouyon Shattaz sends to Caribbean stations include those remixes.

Before any release, settle the rights in writing. The pool agreement proposed by Bouyon Shattaz and approved by Denmath’s management offers a simple model: ownership of the beat stays with the producer, pool members record freely, but any public release requires the producer’s prior written approval. The credit of Litleboy LSBeats767, artist and beatmaker credited on Pow Pow / Allo Doudou, a track certified Single Or by SNEP, shows what a name written properly in the credits makes real.
