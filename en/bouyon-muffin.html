Bouyon-muffin: when bouyon crosses ragga

By Smokiller · Published September 25, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Bouyon-muffin is one of bouyon’s first children: from the 1990s, Dominican singers lay voices inspired by Jamaican dancehall and raggamuffin over the fast rhythms of bouyon. The result is a very recognizable vocal style, between singing, shouting and toasting.

The Jamaican influence

Bouyon draws from chanté mas and the tradition of the carnival cry, but the 1990s generation also listens to Kingston dancehall. Bouyon-muffin owns that mix: raggamuffin-style voices, lyrics that comment on everyday life and island politics, laid over the rhythmic machine of bouyon.

A style that shaped modern bouyon

Bouyon-muffin installed a way of singing over bouyon that still influences the current scene: fast delivery, chanted refrains, Creole and English mixed together. It is one of the bridges between the bouyon of the live bands and the bouyon of today’s producers.

Frequently asked questions

What is bouyon music?

Bouyon is a music genre born in Dominica in the late 1980s, pioneered by the band WCK, formed in Roseau in 1988. Its name comes from the Kwéyòl word for soup: it blends jing ping, cadence-lypso, calypso and electronic production. Bouyon-muffin is one of its first branches, born when singers of the 1990s laid ragga voices over it.

What is bouyon-muffin?

Bouyon-muffin is one of bouyon's first children: from the 1990s, Dominican singers lay voices inspired by Jamaican dancehall and raggamuffin over the fast rhythms of bouyon. The result is a very recognizable vocal style, between singing, shouting and toasting, with lyrics about everyday life and island politics.

How did bouyon-muffin influence modern bouyon?

Bouyon-muffin installed a way of singing over bouyon that still marks the current scene: fast delivery, chanted refrains, Creole and English mixed together. It is one of the bridges between the bouyon of the live bands, like old school bouyon, and the bouyon of today's producers.

Who is the key artist of bouyon-muffin?

Skinny Banton, according to Wikipedia in English and in French. From 1995, he worked with WCK, laying ragga-influenced vocals over bouyon rhythms. The list of former WCK members names him Wayne “Skinny Banton” Robinson, on lead vocals, and he later became known as Shadowflow. See the Skinny Banton page.

When did bouyon-muffin appear?

In the 1990s, in Dominica. Its documented history starts in the middle of the decade: from 1995, Skinny Banton works with WCK and lays a ragga voice over bouyon rhythms. It is the era when bouyon dominates the island's scene and when a more spoken style, drawn from Jamaican dancehall, joins the classic band singing.

Does bouyon-muffin come from Jamaica?

No, it was born in Dominica. The Jamaican influence is in the voice: the Dominican generation of the 1990s listened to Kingston dancehall and took up its raggamuffin style. But the base stays the island's own bouyon, fed by chanté mas, the carnival cry tradition and the rhythmic machine launched by WCK in 1988.

What is the difference between bouyon-muffin and classic bouyon?

The rhythmic base is the same; the voice changes. Classic bouyon is the music of founding bands like WCK: live line-ups, keyboards and machines over a base of cadence-lypso and jing ping. Bouyon-muffin keeps those rhythms but swaps band singing for a toasted, spoken delivery, in the manner of raggamuffin and dancehall.

Which song links bouyon-muffin to Wrecketeng?

“Party”, with Joanne and DJ Cut of Bucktown Sounds. According to Wikipedia, the song combines bouyon with dancehall-style deejaying, and recordings like it pushed bouyon-muffin artists, such as the Bushtown Clan, to bring in more hip hop and dancehall. That chain led to the creation of Wrecketeng.

What are bouyon-muffin lyrics about?

Everyday life and island politics. Bouyon-muffin keeps bouyon's role as commentary, telling the life of the island as cadence-lypso did before it, but with raggamuffin-style voices: fast delivery, chanted refrains, Creole and English mixed together. The result is a very recognizable vocal style, between singing, shouting and toasting.

Is the Bushtown Clan part of bouyon-muffin?

Yes, the sources present it as a product of bouyon-muffin. This Dominican collective of DJs and producers, inspired by songs like “Party”, pushed hip hop and dancehall further into bouyon, and it played an important role in the early development of Wrecketeng through DJ performances, remixes and recordings. See the Bushtown Clan page.
