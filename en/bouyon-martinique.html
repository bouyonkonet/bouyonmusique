Bouyon in Martinique: jump up in the land of zouk

Published September 27, 2026

Martinique, land of zouk, is also a land of bouyon. Martinican audiences know the genre under the name jump up and live it at carnival as well as at parties all year round.

A shared Creole history

Dominica, Guadeloupe and Martinique share the Kwéyòl language and closely related carnival traditions: chanté mas, street bands, Creole dances. Bouyon has circulated among the three islands since the 1990s, carried by exchanges between musicians and audiences.

Artists who build the bridge

Collaborations like Joli BB by Reo, a Dominican artist, with Maurane Voyer, a Martinican singer, show the bouyon of 2026 at work: a Dominican creation, a Martinican bridge, an audience on both sides of the sea. See the Maurane Voyer page.

Frequently asked questions

What is bouyon called in Martinique?

In Martinique, bouyon is mostly called jump up. Martinican audiences live it under that name at carnival as well as at parties all year round. For many people in the French Antilles, bouyon and jump up mean the same sound: the fast music from Dominica that makes everyone jump. See the jump up page.

Which Martinican artists make bouyon?

Martinican singer Maurane Voyer is the most visible figure in 2026: she sings Joli BB with Reo, a Dominican artist, a song released on October 9, 2026 on the Mauve label. She is also billed on the first night of the World Creole Music Festival 2026 in Roseau, on Friday, October 23. See the Maurane Voyer page.

Where is bouyon popular?

Born in Dominica in the late 1980s, bouyon is now central in Guadeloupe and Martinique, where it is also called jump up. It is present in France, Saint Lucia, Saint Martin and the Caribbean diaspora, and has circulated among Dominica, Guadeloupe and Martinique since the 1990s.

Which country created bouyon?

Dominica. The genre was born there in the late 1980s, launched by the band WCK, formed in 1988 in Roseau, and it remains the sound of Roseau carnival. Martinique, which shares the Kwéyòl language and closely related carnival traditions with Dominica, adopted it under the name jump up.
