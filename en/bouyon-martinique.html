Bouyon in Martinique: jump up in the land of zouk

By Smokiller · Published September 27, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Martinique, land of zouk, is also a land of bouyon. Martinican audiences know the genre under the name jump up and live it at carnival as well as at parties all year round.

A shared Creole history

Dominica, Guadeloupe and Martinique share the Kwéyòl language and closely related carnival traditions: chanté mas, street bands, Creole dances. Bouyon has circulated among the three islands since the 1990s, carried by exchanges between musicians and audiences.

Artists who build the bridge

Collaborations like Joli BB by Reo, a Dominican artist, with Maurane Voyer, a Martinican singer, show the bouyon of 2026 at work: a Dominican creation, a Martinican bridge, an audience on both sides of the sea. See the Maurane Voyer page.

Frequently asked questions

What is bouyon called in Martinique?

In Martinique, bouyon is mostly called jump up. Martinican audiences live it under that name at carnival as well as at parties all year round. For many people in the French Antilles, bouyon and jump up mean the same sound: the fast music from Dominica that makes everyone jump. See the jump up page.

Which Martinican artists make bouyon?

Martinican singer Maurane Voyer is the most visible figure in 2026: she sings Joli BB with Reo, a Dominican artist, a song released on October 9, 2026 on the Mauve label. She is also billed on the first night of the World Creole Music Festival 2026 in Roseau, on Friday, October 23. See the Maurane Voyer page.

Where is bouyon popular?

Born in Dominica in the late 1980s, bouyon is now central in Guadeloupe and Martinique, where it is also called jump up. It is present in France, Saint Lucia, Saint Martin and the Caribbean diaspora, and has circulated among Dominica, Guadeloupe and Martinique since the 1990s.

Which country created bouyon?

Dominica. The genre was born there in the late 1980s, launched by the band WCK, formed in 1986 in Roseau, and it remains the sound of Roseau carnival. Martinique, which shares the Kwéyòl language and closely related carnival traditions with Dominica, adopted it under the name jump up.

Do shatta and bouyon come from Martinique?

Shatta does: it is a dancehall subgenre born in Martinique, which this site describes as the Martinique cousin of bouyon. Bouyon does not: it was born in Dominica, created by WCK in Roseau in 1988, and Martinique adopted it under the name jump up. The two scenes often cross paths, and shatta bouyon fuses them. See bouyon vs shatta.

What is the difference between zouk and bouyon?

Zouk comes from Guadeloupe and Martinique: popularized in the early 1980s by bands like Kassav, it is clean, danceable and romantic in its zouk love version. Bouyon comes from Dominica: faster, rawer, more carnival than ballroom. Zouk is even one of bouyon's ingredients. See bouyon vs zouk.

Who wrote Joli BB by Reo and Maurane Voyer?

Joli BB was written by Maurane Voyer, Dario Yankey and Nicolas Lanclume. Dario Yankey is the real name of Reo, the Dominican artist presented as the Prince of Bouyon by 13th Street Promotions. The song came out on October 9, 2026 on the Mauve label: a Martinican singer, an artist from the source island and a named writing team.

Which other Martinican artists are linked to bouyon?

This site also points to Koko Bwa, a Martinican artist whose first single, "Tarif", came out in April 2025 with producer Denmath as featured artist and Boutcha Bwa. Bouyon Shattaz agreed to publish the track in October 2026. The site's Martinique hub grows this way, one verified profile after another. See the bouyon artists of Martinique.

Where can you dance to bouyon in Martinique?

At Martinique's carnival, where jump up is the soundtrack of the skin-drum bands, the déboulés and the street parties, and at parties all year round. Bouyon is danced there by jumping, a dance that turns a track into a joyful endurance test, and videos of these dances then carry the tracks further. See bouyon dance.

Why do Martinicans understand Dominican bouyon?

Because Dominica and Martinique share the Kwéyòl language: Dominican Creole is close to Martinique's Creole. A bouyon track can therefore be understood and sung along in Martinique without translation, and a Martinican voice sounds right on a Roseau riddim. This linguistic passport let bouyon become the jump up of the French Antilles. See bouyon and Kwéyòl.

Where does the name jump up come from?

The term jump up already existed in the French Antilles for very rhythmic carnival music, notably after Exile One's 1987 track L'hivernage, nicknamed The Yo. When Dominican bouyon crossed the sea, Martinican audiences naturally placed it in that family: the name sums up its effect, a fast tempo that makes everyone jump.
