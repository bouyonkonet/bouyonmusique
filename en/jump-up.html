Jump up: bouyon under its French Antillean name

By Smokiller · Published September 28, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

If you are looking for bouyon in Guadeloupe or Martinique, ask for jump up. It is the name the French Antilles give to the bouyon music of Dominica, a name that sums up its effect: a fast tempo that makes everybody jump.

The term jump up already existed in the French Antilles to describe very rhythmic carnival music, notably after the song L’hivernage by Exile One in 1987, nicknamed The Yo. Frequently asked questions

Are jump up and bouyon the same thing?

In Guadeloupe and Martinique, yes, in everyday language: jump up means the bouyon music that came from Dominica. The term already existed for very rhythmic carnival music, and the Antillean audience naturally filed bouyon in that family. For many people, bouyon and jump up are the same sound.

Why is bouyon called jump up?

Because the name sums up the music's effect: a fast tempo that makes everybody jump. It is how the French Antilles, Guadeloupe and Martinique, refer to the bouyon music of Dominica. So if you are looking for bouyon in Guadeloupe or Martinique, ask for jump up.

Where does the term jump up come from?

The term jump up already existed in the French Antilles to describe very rhythmic carnival music, notably after the song L'hivernage by Exile One in 1987, nicknamed The Yo. When Dominican bouyon crossed the sea, the Antillean audience naturally filed it in that family.

Where can you hear jump up?

Mainly at the carnivals of Guadeloupe and Martinique, where jump up is the soundtrack of the skin drum bands, the déboulés and the street fetes. It links the carnival traditions of the three Creole-speaking islands: Dominica, the birthplace of bouyon, Guadeloupe and Martinique. See also Dominica Carnival.

Does jump up come from Dominica or Guadeloupe?

The music comes from Dominica, the name from the French Antilles. Jump up is the bouyon created in Roseau in 1988 by the band WCK; it is the audience of Guadeloupe and Martinique that called it that, filing it in their family of very rhythmic carnival music. Guadeloupe later developed its own branch, bouyon gwada.

How do you dance to jump up?

By jumping: that is the whole point of the name. In Guadeloupe, the word also means the way of dancing bouyon by jumping, which became the signature of gwada parties. There is no official step: you sway on the refrains, jump together on the build-ups and answer the calls of the singer or the DJ. See the bouyon dance page.

Is jump up the same as zouk?

No. Zouk comes from Guadeloupe and Martinique, popularized in the early 1980s by groups like Kassav: a clean, danceable sound, romantic in its zouk love version. Jump up is the bouyon that came from Dominica, faster, rawer, more carnival than lounge. Zouk is still one of bouyon's ingredients. See bouyon vs zouk.

What is the link between Exile One and jump up?

The term jump up is notably associated with L'hivernage by Exile One, in 1987, a song nicknamed The Yo: after it, the word was used in the French Antilles for very rhythmic carnival music. Exile One is a Dominican band, a cadence-lypso star of the 1970s, one of bouyon's two musical parents along with jing ping.

Why did bouyon catch on so well in Guadeloupe and Martinique?

Because the islands speak close Creoles. Dominican Kwéyòl served as bouyon's linguistic passport: the music crossed the sea through carnival and exchanges between Creole-speaking islands. The Antillean audience filed it in its jump up family, and the sound became a must at parties and carnivals. See bouyon and Kwéyòl.

Is jump up fast?

Yes, that is its reason to exist: a tempo that makes everyone jump. Bouyon has no single BPM, but the examples documented on this site sit around 160 BPM, like Mauvais Plan by producer HYPNO. This speed comes from Dominica's traditional dances and from the carnival competition. See the bouyon BPM page.

What is the difference between jump up and bouyon gwada?

Jump up is the everyday name for bouyon in the French Antilles. Bouyon gwada, also called hardcore bouyon, is a specific branch, born from collaborations between musicians from Dominica and Guadeloupe: harder, more electronic and largely sung in Guadeloupean Creole. The first names the genre, the second one of its styles. See the bouyon gwada page.
