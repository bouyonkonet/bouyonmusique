WCMF 2026: the Creole debate, protecting culture or limiting its reach

By Smokiller · Published November 3, 2026 · Updated November 10, 2026

The festival is over, the debate is not. In an opinion column published on October 30 on BuzzDA, writer Aliyah Jean Jacques asks the question running through Dominica since the World Creole Music Festival 2026: by inviting American stars Ne-Yo and Fridayy, is the festival protecting its Creole culture or risking diluting it.

The critics, as the column reports them

Some believe the Dominica Festivals Committee has strayed too far from the festival Creole identity, the column reports. Others argue that international acts are overshadowing local and regional performers, and that perhaps these artistes belong at a separate festival altogether.

The debate went beyond social media: Dominica press also carried, from October 27, the public defence of Ne-Yo inclusion by the leadership of the Discover Dominica Authority, in a Dominica Gazette headline devoted to the subject. The place of international stars at WCMF has become a national conversation, not only a fan argument.

The column argument: protecting is not closing

We need to be careful not to confuse protecting our culture with limiting its reach, Aliyah Jean Jacques writes. She notes that over the past decade the festival has gradually welcomed genres such as Afrobeat, dancehall and reggae, and that much of the criticism of those steps eventually settled.

For Fridayy, the column stresses a Haitian heritage reflected in his music: several of his songs incorporate Haitian Creole, and he communicated in Creole on stage. For Ne-Yo, an African-American artiste with three Grammy Awards per the column, his catalogue is part of the soundtrack of Black pop culture, and both performances complemented the weekend musical journey, from bouyon and cadence-lypso to R and B.

The boundary the column proposes

The column does not say yes to everything. International acts should still keep a cultural and musical connection to the African, Caribbean and Creole experience: its author says she would not necessarily support artistes such as Ed Sheeran or Taylor Swift on this particular stage.

She also notes that the Dominica Festivals Committee involves patrons in the process of selecting performers. We cannot join those conversations, celebrate the arrival of international stars and then act surprised when the lineup reflects those interests, she writes, while judging it too soon to sound the alarm over two American performers: the festival direction should be evaluated over time.

Her ask: make the stars market the destination too

The column main recommendation targets the festival committee: turn international artistes into ambassadors for the destination. A day exploring the island, its hot springs, natural attractions and local cuisine, possibly for an added fee or a longer stay, plus promotional partnerships with local brands, with Kubuli Beer named as the example.

The column also slips in a proposal for the Jazz n Creole festival, which the author wants to grow into a two day event with bigger headliners. Her conclusion holds the line of the whole debate: we can love bouyon and still enjoy Ne-Yo, celebrate cadence-lypso and still sing along to Fridayy. The festival should never lose its identity, but that identity does not have to be restrictive.
